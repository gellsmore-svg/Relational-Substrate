# 2. Residual issue 1 — equivalence-class identity is not the same as numerical identity

This is the most important remaining conceptual issue.

Chapter 6 currently says, approximately:

> “A thing continues to be what it is when the changes it undergoes preserve its relevant equivalence class…”

This is an excellent account of **operational / dynamical equivalence**, but it risks collapsing that into the stronger question of whether a later thing is **numerically the same individual**.

The project’s own v2 conversational import already distinguishes:

1. functional recovery;
2. structural / recursive recovery;
3. equivalence-class recovery;
4. lineage / identity-specific recovery.

That distinction is correct.

The main manuscript should now reflect it explicitly.

## 2.1 Required distinction

The book should distinguish two different questions.

### A. State/type equivalence

What kind of thing/state is this, relative to the admissible distinctions that future processes can still consume?

This is answered by:

- relational equivalence;
- invariants;
- projection;
- future distinguishability;
- relevant equivalence class.

### B. Continuing individual identity

Is this **the same continuing individual**, not merely an equivalent replacement?

This additionally requires some account of:

- continuity;
- lineage;
- provenance;
- preserved causal/relational history;
- or another principled identity-bearing relation.

Do not claim that current RS research has fully solved this second question.

It has not.

## 2.2 Important consequence

If A is destroyed and later B is reconstructed so that no currently defined admissible future operation can distinguish B from A, then B may be in the same relevant equivalence class.

That does **not automatically establish** that B is numerically the same individual as A.

The current 502/reconstruction work already supports exactly this caution.

## 2.3 Required correction

Revise Chapter 6 so that it no longer uses “identity” ambiguously where it really means equivalence-class continuity.

A useful distinction may be:

- **state identity / relational equivalence**
- **individual continuity / lineage identity**

Do not force these exact labels if better language emerges.

But the distinction must be clear.

## 2.4 Acceptance test

A reader should be able to explain:

- why two states can be operationally indistinguishable without necessarily being the same individual;
- why equivalence-class recovery is not automatically lineage recovery;
- why provenance matters;
- why lineage can remain open as a research problem;
- why this strengthens, rather than weakens, the Chapter 14 claim that resurrection is not natural reconstruction.

Update:

- Chapter 6;
- Chapter 7 only if carriage terminology depends on “identity” too strongly;
- Appendix B;
- glossary;
- epistemic register;
- final vector review;
- conceptual graph if needed.

# 3. Residual issue 2 — “stochastic” still slips into ontic openness

Chapter 5 currently says:

> “Stochasticity makes the future open even though nothing in it is lawless.”

This is still too strong as a definition.

A stochastic model/process is described by probabilities over possible outcomes.

That alone does not decide whether:

- the probabilities are ontic;
- the process is fundamentally indeterministic;
- the probabilities express ignorance of hidden state;
- the process is deterministic underneath.

The manuscript itself correctly makes this distinction a few paragraphs later.

## 3.1 Required correction

Define stochasticity neutrally.

For example:

> A stochastic process is described by probabilities over defined possibilities.

Then separately say:

> RS proposes that physical actualisation is fundamentally stochastic in the ontic sense.

The RS hypothesis may still say:

> the future is genuinely open within constrained possibility.

But that openness belongs to the **RS hypothesis**, not to the generic definition of stochasticity.

## 3.2 Acceptance test

A reader should be able to distinguish:

- stochastic description;
- epistemic probability;
- ontic indeterminacy;
- deterministic hidden-state interpretation;
- RS’s own stronger hypothesis.

# 4. Residual issue 3 — “without forbidding, nothing is shaped” is now too simple

Chapter 4 still contains language close to:

> “Without forbidding, nothing is shaped; without favouring, nothing is distinctive.”

That overstates what the later research supports.

Generation 1/1b established a useful distinction:

- hard prohibition can change support/admissibility;
- weights can change likelihood/measure without changing support.

But later work shows that system structure is richer than those two axes.

Generation 3 demonstrates that:

- higher-order relation can change exact dynamics;
- hidden present relation can make a visible projection non-Markov;
- no new pairwise support need appear.

Similarly, coordination can change:

- path structure;
- fluctuations;
- exact conservation;
- response;
- future accessibility

without being reducible to “forbid versus favour.”

## 4.1 Required correction

Preserve the admissibility/tendency distinction.

But replace the sweeping rhetoric with something richer, e.g.:

> Structure depends on what is possible, how admissible possibilities are weighted, and how transitions are related, coupled and composed.

Do not present this wording mechanically if you find a better sentence.

## 4.2 Acceptance test

The chapter should allow all of the following to be true simultaneously:

- prohibitions shape support;
- weights shape measure;
- coupling shapes dynamics;
- higher-order relation can create hidden-state dependence;
- exact kernels can differ even where coarse support is unchanged.

# 5. Residual issue 4 — corruption is not defined by persistence

Chapter 13 still says, approximately:

> “What makes misalignment corrupt is that it persists and spreads without being restored.”

This is morally too strong.

Persistence and propagation explain:

- entrenchment;
- hardening;
- habit;
- structural spread;
- narrowing of future response.

They do **not** define the wrongness of the originating act.

A single lie followed immediately by repentance is still a lie.

A single act of cruelty is still morally corrupt even if it does not propagate.

## 5.1 Required correction

Keep the structural account of how corruption **entrenches**.

But separate:

- moral wrongness of an act;
- persistence of misalignment;
- propagation/entrenchment of corruption.

A better concept is:

> Misalignment becomes entrenched as it persists and propagates; persistence explains hardening, not the moral wrongness of the first act.

Again, use better wording if available.

## 5.2 Acceptance test

A reader should not leave Chapter 13 believing that wrongdoing only becomes corrupt if repeated.

# 6. Residual issue 5 — Genesis 1 darkness is still stated too declaratively

The manuscript currently says of Genesis 1:2 darkness:

> “It is a real condition of a real world: unmanifested, undifferentiated, not yet brought into visible order.”

The core point is good:

- darkness in Genesis 1:2 is not a co-eternal evil principle;
- it is not equivalent to moral darkness;
- visibility is not the measure of reality.

But “unmanifested” and “undifferentiated” are interpretive/theological descriptors.

Genesis does not lexically state them.

## 6.1 Required correction

Mark those descriptors explicitly as interpretation.

For example:

> It may therefore be understood here as a real created condition, not yet manifest in the visible order that follows…

Do not weaken the broader argument.

Just make the epistemic register accurate.

## 6.2 Acceptance test

The reader should be able to tell the difference between:

- what Genesis explicitly says;
- what the book infers from the sequence.

# 7. Residual issue 6 — Chapter 9’s premise about intrinsic bearers is still too broad

Chapter 9 is now much stronger and should remain structurally intact.

It correctly engages:

- powers ontology;
- field ontology;
- hylomorphism;
- process ontology;
- structural realism.

However, its central premise still says, in effect:

> an ontology that begins with intrinsic bearers must later introduce constitutive relation.

That is too universal.

A sophisticated powers theorist may hold that an entity’s essential powers are already inherently other-directed.

A hylomorphist may treat form and ordered capacities as constitutive from the beginning.

The book should not claim those views begin with fully relation-independent individuals unless they actually do.

## 7.1 Narrow the target

The argument should target:

> ontologies in which fundamental entities are individuated wholly or substantially independently of constitutive relation.

That is the actual contrast RS needs.

Then RS’s advantage becomes:

> relation is constitutive at the base rather than entering only after already-complete individuals are specified.

## 7.2 Preserve the correct conclusion

Do not say:

> all non-RS substance ontologies must fail.

Keep the more modest argument:

> relation-first gives an economical continuity of relational form across physical, living, personal and moral reality.

That remains a coherence preference, not a proof.

# 8. Residual issue 7 — make interactional versus constitutive relation explicit

The current book uses the word “constitutive” well in Chapter 9.

But Chapter 3 still does not teach the reader the explicit distinction strongly enough.

Add a very short clarification.

Something like:

> Two already-defined things may interact without their identity depending on that interaction. That is interactional relation. RS makes the stronger proposal that physical things are constituted within relation: relation partly belongs to what they are.

Do not make this a new technical taxonomy.

It should simply prevent the reader from reducing “relation-first” to “everything interacts.”

## 8.1 Acceptance test

A reader should be able to explain why:

> “everything interacts”

is not yet the RS ontology.

# 9. Residual issue 8 — soften the ontology of unreceived transmission

Chapter 7 currently says:

> “A distinction made available and carried, but which no receiver could ever be changed by, has a thin and strange reality.”

This is not needed.

It risks importing a stronger dispositional / reception-dependent ontology than the rest of the project requires.

The T-C-R grammar only needs to say:

- transmission makes a distinction available;
- carriage preserves it;
- reception makes it consequential in a receiver.

If no receiver ever receives it, then the **T-C-R chain has not completed consequential reception**.

That does not mean the propagation was ontologically “thin” or less real.

## 9.1 Required correction

Replace the sentence with a process claim rather than an ontological downgrade.

For example:

> A distinction may be transmitted and carried without ever reaching consequential reception; in that case the T-C-R chain remains incomplete at the receiving end.

Preserve the important claim:

> reception is consequence, not arrival.
