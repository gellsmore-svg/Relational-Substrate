## 1. What this revision is for

The 31 October material does three things for the book.

1. It adds an **epistemology the book has been practising without stating**. The book has said what proof is not on seven occasions and has never said what proof is. Its method of revisable, coherence-tested inference is described in an appendix and a research document, not in the argument. The material supplies the missing account: true logic and inferential logic, proof as warrant, coherence factors, the anatomy of error, independent convergence.
2. It completes **time**, which the book has handled in two paragraphs (Ch6 L151-155). It does so by making consequential succession a stated premise, separating cadence, measurement and direction, and naming oscillation as your favoured candidate for a created reference cadence.
3. It extends the theological chapters with your theses on **embodiment, the Fall, provenance and judgement**: created order designed to bear moral consequence; the curse as a deliberate realignment; history as its own record; God's complete knowledge as the ground of justice.

At the same time the §8 editorial pass removes what has accumulated: six restated clusters, one broken promise, and a habit of ending sections with disclaimers.

**What success looks like.**

- Each new idea is explained once, at a home that already exists.
- No term has two senses.
- Every theological thesis is marked as text, interpretation or mechanism.
- The book is no longer than it needs to be.
- Phase B can begin without your having to rewrite the plan.

> Behind this ordinary practice lies a distinction the book will need. **Logic** is the necessary structure of consistency, implication, exclusion and possibility. **Reasoning** is its application, which can be done well or badly. There is, first, the complete coherence of all that is, perfectly comprehended and perfectly applied by the One who made it. The author calls this **true logic**. It belongs to God's own nature, and no creature possesses it exhaustively. And there is the way finite minds move toward it. The author's definition is exact: "Inferential logic is the recursive refinement of partial understanding towards true logic through the discovery and application of relational coherence within the boundaries of created runtime order."
>
> Two words in that definition carry the weight. *Partial*: the doctor's diagnosis is genuine knowledge, and it is not everything. *Recursive*: a later finding can revise not only a conclusion but the assumptions behind it, and sometimes the question. Observation, measurement, experiment, mathematics, philosophical argument and the reading of Scripture are not routes running beside inference. They are what inference works with. And because revision can reach even the book's own foundational commitments, one distinction stays fixed: revising an account of coherence is never revising the coherence of what God has made. **Status:** theological premise (true logic); working definition (inferential logic).

**(d) "What observation decides", L126-130.** Proof, coherence factor, disciplined confidence. The flat-earth sentence and "says this once" stay. Units: P02–P06, M07, B18, B22. Depends on decision 1.

> What, then, is proof? In this book the word has a bounded sense. **Proof** is the achievement of enough warrant, under stated premises and constraints, for an inferred relationship to be relied on in further reasoning: to become a **coherence factor**. A mathematical proof has this character in its strongest form: within its axioms its conclusion is settled. An experimental result has it within what its procedures can show. A coherence factor keeps its scope, its source and its dependencies in view, so that it can be revised if later work requires. Proof in this sense is real. It is never the possession of the whole truth of what it concerns. The aim of inquiry is accordingly **inferential precision**: discriminating well between candidate relationships, tracing the warrant for each, and keeping what remains uncertain open to correction.

L130 then states what the book's warrant is, positively: a global coherence argument, not experimental demonstration. Add one sentence naming *disciplined confidence* (Ch16 L31) as the rule that over-claiming and under-claiming are both errors.

### 4.3 Chapter 6: succession, cadence and time

**Where.** Replace "A word about time" (L151-155) with a section of about 650 words, in two parts (HCL critical as one block). Units: T01–T07, T09, T12, T14, B05, B07, B08, B17. Depends on decisions 4, 7, 8, 9, 13.

> ## Succession, cadence and time
>
> If change is the actualisation of admissible transitions, then time, on the relational proposal, is the **ordering of actualisation**: the before-and-after of change itself, not an independent container that would still be ticking if nothing happened. [Retain the existing sentences on Leibniz, general relativity and clocks as physical processes.]
>
> The order has a direction, and the direction comes from consequence. When one thing happens because of another, the dependence runs one way: the bell sounds because it was struck, and not the reverse. The book calls this directed order of consequence **succession**. On the author's account it is not produced by anything more basic. It belongs to consequence itself, in an order made by a God of order. **Status:** theological and philosophical premise.
>
> Three other things are easily confused with succession. The first is **cadence**, the ordered progression of occasions and events. Picture a ferry that calls at a jetty twelve times a day. Each call is an occasion on which a passenger could cross, an **opportunity** in the sense of Chapter 4, and the pattern of calls is the **opportunity cadence**. On a given day a passenger crosses four times, and the pattern of what actually happened is the **actualisation cadence**. The two differ, and neither excludes the other: the calls continue whether or not anyone boards, and every crossing happened at a call. A cadence need not be regular. Regularity is one kind of cadence, not part of its definition.
>
> The second is **measurement**. To say how long something took is to compare one cadence with another taken as a reference: the swing of a pendulum, the turning of the Earth, the vibration of a caesium atom. Seconds are a convention. The ordering they count is not. The third is a **reference cadence** belonging to created order itself, something recurring steadily enough for comparison to be possible at all. The author's favoured candidate is oscillation (Chapter 8): an economical way for a relational order to furnish comparable intervals without an outside clock. It is a mechanism candidate. It would measure succession, not produce it. Scripture gives the lights of heaven "for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years" (Genesis 1:14), created regularity given as a measure. That is the text's statement of purpose, and it names no mechanism.
>
> The project's simplest models show why the premise has to be stated rather than assumed. In them succession does not come free. Rules that merely make some states more likely give preferred states but no direction. Direction appears only with a tendency that favours going round a cycle one way, or with a rule that makes some change one-way (Appendix E.8). A model must be given direction. No model claim about time is free of the modeller's clock. What remains open is mechanism: how clocks are tied to relational structure, which gravity makes pressing (Chapter 9); whether persistent consequence is what makes the past unrecoverable; and how an opportunity that passes unused is to be counted.

Chapter 13 introduces *time as opportunity* and points back here.

### 4.4 Chapter 4: opportunity and the two levels of constraint

**Where.** After L61. Units: C01–C04, C08, A02, A11, A13, L10. Depends on decision 7.

> Two further distinctions sharpen this. The first concerns constraint. Some constraints belong to the primitive configuration of created order itself, the conditions under which anything can happen at all. The book calls these **explicitly intrinsic** constraints. Total admissibility rests on them. Others arise only once a configuration has actually come about, and then follow from it necessarily: once the stone has cracked, it will split along the crack and not across it. These are **implicitly intrinsic** constraints. The two differ in level and express one order. One point about them matters for the next chapter. A configuration that came about by chance can carry constraints that are necessary. Nothing fixed where the crack would run, but given the crack, the split follows.
>
> The second concerns occasion. Possibility, opportunity, admissibility and actualisation are four different things. What is possible is what the constraints allow at all. What is admissible now is what the present state allows next. An **opportunity** is an occasion at which some admissible actualisation is actually available, and an opportunity can pass with nothing happening: the seed that could have germinated this spring, and did not. How such an unused occasion is to be counted is an open question (Appendix E).

### 4.5 Chapter 5: necessity is not determination

**Where.** After L31. Units: G09, L06, L07, C08, A13, MA-04(a).

> One confusion runs the other way and needs heading off. If the constraints are necessary, is the outcome determined after all? No. A constraint can be absolutely binding and still leave several outcomes open: the rules of chess are never broken, and they never say which legal move will be made. Necessity governs what must and must not hold. Determination would require that the present state, under its governing relations, leaves exactly one next state, a different and far stronger condition (Chapter 9 defines it). The proposal is that created order is necessary in its constraints and open in its actualisations.

### 4.6 Chapter 10: coherence, error and convergence

**Where.** Rebuild "Coherence is not the same as truth" (L106-114) under the heading "Coherence, error and convergence". The restatement (R-03, C-16) goes. The false attractor, the self-application and L114 stay. Units: E01–E03, E06, E07, E09, E11, E12, M06, M12, H09, B21.

> Chapter 2 said that coherence is not truth. The research behind this book met a sharper form of that danger. [Retain the false-attractor account from L110.] Agreement can be produced by a shared hidden premise as easily as by a shared grasp of reality.
>
> The remedy is to ask where agreement came from. When lines of inquiry that are genuinely independent, starting from different premises, drawing on different evidence and reasoning by different routes, arrive at relationships that fit together, their agreement carries weight no single line could. The book calls this **independent relational convergence**. The dodo of Chapter 8 is an example: sailors' accounts, bones, a preserved head and a genome, each with its own provenance, constrain the same bird. Its strength depends on the independence being real. Many voices repeating one source are one source. Several AI systems trained on overlapping material and fed one another's summaries are not independent witnesses because they are separate programs, which is how the false attractor formed.
>
> Error has its own anatomy, and it tells more than a verdict of "false". Take 2 + 2 = 5. Almost everything in it is sound. The numbers mean what they always mean. Addition is a well-defined operation. The question, what two and two make, has a definite answer, and five is an intelligible candidate. The error lies in one place: the asserted relation between the operation and its result. A statement that could not be understood could not even be wrong, so intelligible error depends on genuine coherence. That does not mean every falsehood hides a true conclusion. It means that when a claim fails, the right response is to find the relationship that failed, keep what remains sound, and notice what the failure has excluded. A failed hypothesis can correct the question as well as the answer. Stories show the same separation from the other side. A novel can be coherent throughout and never have happened: whether an account holds together, and whether it reports what occurred, are two questions.
>
> This is not the account of moral evil, which Chapter 14 gives. A mistaken representation is an error of relation. Wrongdoing is a person's turning against the good. They can coincide. One is not the other.
>

### 4.8 Chapter 12: embodied acts, knowing and choosing

**(a) After L29 (B01, B03; decisions 5 and 12).**

> It follows that a human moral act is not a spiritual event with a bodily sign attached. When a person lies, the words, the body that speaks them and the changes they make in others belong to the act itself. The act's moral reality, its wrong before God and against a neighbour, is not the same thing as those events, and it is not separate from them either: within one person the two are causally connected. Created order is fitted to bear such acts. It is, in the author's phrase, the theatre and the language of moral consequence.

**(b) A new section after "Consciousness": "Knowing and choosing as events".** Units: I02, I03, I09–I12, H01, H02, H04, H05, I14, L09. Concept order requires this after Ch7.

> Return once more to seeing the stone. When you recognise it, something happens in you: a distinction received from the world is related to what you already know, and you know something you did not. In the terms of Chapter 7, observation supplies a distinction and inference receives it. The recognition is an actual event, not a commentary standing outside the world. Knowing is one of the ways created order becomes actual in a person, and much of it happens without deliberate attention: you recognise a friend's voice before you think about it.
>
> Persons can do more. They can hold in view what is not yet actual, and choose among alternatives. A choice actualises one configuration rather than another, and its consequences pass into the world like any others: you pick up the stone, and it is somewhere it would not otherwise have been. Knowing and choosing are not on a higher floor of reality than physical change. In the person the epistemic and the ontological intersect: what is known is real, and so is the knowing. At higher orders the book calls this reasoning **relational inference**.
>
> None of this explains consciousness, and none of it makes created order a knower. Created order is not conscious, and it did not become capable of consciousness by growing complex. Creaturely personhood is where two kinds of reality meet by design: a non-conscious created order, coherent and fitted to bear consequence, and a conscious capacity for relation to God, given with the breath of Genesis 2:7.

### 4.10 Chapter 14: the subjection of creation

**Where.** A new section after "Real, but not ultimate" (L17). About 600 words. Units: F01, F03–F07, F09, F11, C09, C12, C13, F08. Depends on decisions 6, 10, 11. A reminder of Ch4's two constraint levels is required (recall distance: ten chapters).

> ## The subjection of creation
>
> Scripture traces the disorder of the world to an event. After the man and the woman had eaten, God's word reached past them into the ground itself:
>
> > `And unto Adam he said, Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee; and thou shalt eat the herb of the field; In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.`
> > *Genesis 3:17-19*
>
> Three things need to be kept apart.
>
> *What the text says.* The ground is cursed because of human disobedience. Work becomes toil, the earth yields thorns, and the man will return to dust. Paul adds that the creation "was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope" (Romans 8:20). The subjection is God's act.
>
> *The author's interpretation.* The curse is a deliberate divine realignment of created runtime order to the consequences of spiritual disobedience. Its purpose is that creation should go on telling the truth. A world in which the breach between humanity and God had no visible consequence would teach falsely about what had happened. On this reading creation remains an instructor after the Fall, and what it now teaches includes decay, toil and loss. **Status:** theological interpretation. Genesis does not describe a mechanism, and the interpretation does not claim that it does.
>
> *The mechanism, which is open.* The author's thesis is that God realigned the primitives themselves, the explicitly intrinsic constraints of Chapter 4. Two other readings belong in view. God may have changed settings within a primitive order that stayed fixed. Or he may have brought about a new state of creation under its original constraints, so that the new conditions follow as implicitly intrinsic constraints. These are different claims, and they would explain different things. Appendix E records them as a research question.
>
> One consequence holds on every reading. The subjection of creation is God's act, and God's acts are not corrupt. Corruption, as the next section describes it, is parasitic: a distortion with no being of its own. The futility to which creation was subjected is a judgement, coherent and purposed, and given "in hope". Corruption suffered in the higher orders of creation does not mean that its primitive order has become incoherent.
>
> Nor does every suffering follow from the sufferer's own sin. Asked whose sin had made a man blind, Jesus answered:
>
> > `Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him.`
> > *John 9:3*
>
> Creation's subjection is the condition of all. It is not a ledger of each.

Decision 11 adds one sentence on non-human death, either way.

### 4.11 Chapter 15: judgement, provenance and the cost foreknown

**(a) After the 1 Peter 1:18-19 quotation (L120): B12, B14.**

> > `Who verily was foreordained before the foundation of the world, but was manifest in these last times for you,`
> > *1 Peter 1:20*
>
> The cost was known and accepted before it was paid. Scripture states the foreordination. The author reads it as a real and irrevocable commitment made before creation, concerning an event within its history: a decision, not a forecast. And because God knows every wrong completely, what was paid for was known exactly. That is a way of speaking about the completeness of his knowledge, not a price that could be counted.

Revelation 13:8 is not used, to avoid its translation question (brief §7.3).

**(b) Judgement, after L147: B11, B13.**

> Such judgement does not depend on what a damaged or partial record can establish. Created order carries the consequences of every act, but not every act's provenance, and much is lost to any creaturely inquiry. God's knowledge is not so limited. He knows the whole history of each act: its intention, its circumstances, its effects, and who is responsible for them. That is why his judgement can be just where every human court would fail. Greater material damage is not by itself greater guilt. Intention, knowledge and capacity weigh too.

**(c) After L100: B13.** "Restoration does not erase history. Forgiveness changes what can follow from a wrong without undoing every effect the wrong has had. The wound and the repair both remain part of the story."

### 4.12 Chapter 16: knowing, inquiry and wonder

**After L45: W02, W04–W10.** Depends on decisions 5 and 14 (beauty).

> Inquiry of this kind is a way, and Scripture's deepest word for it is Christ's own:
>
> > `Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me.`
> > *John 14:6*
>
> The author reads the three words together: the way as a road actually walked, the truth as what grounds it, the life as participation in it, with the Father as the destination, who is one with the Son ("I and my Father are one", John 10:30). That is a theological reading, offered as one. It fits what this book has described: partial understanding refined, step by step, toward a coherence that only God knows perfectly. Truth is what is so; the way toward it is walked by creatures.
>
> The fitting response to such a world is **wonder**: the response of a creature to coherence that is intelligible and yet exceeds what it can now comprehend. Wonder does not end inquiry. It invites it. On the author's account, creation was made to be read this way. Its order represents something of the spiritual order that is prior to it, and its beauty, which belongs first to that order, can be apprehended in created things without being exhausted by them.
