# Chapter 4: What Can Be and What Comes to Be

Water, cooled enough, becomes ice. It does not become glass, or iron, or a hexagonal crystal one day and a cubic one the next at random. Under ordinary conditions it freezes into the same characteristic lattice, whose six-fold symmetry shows in every snowflake. Snowflakes differ endlessly in their details, so no two are quite alike, yet every one of them is six-sided. Something leaves room for enormous variety and at the same time draws firm limits around it.

The stone on the path is like this too. Its minerals are arranged in crystal forms that are possible for those elements and not others. Its grains could have settled in countless slightly different ways, but not in any way at all. The previous chapter proposed that physical things are configurations within a created order of relation. This chapter asks what bounds the configurations that order can bear, and how a particular configuration, this stone rather than another, comes to be actual.

## Possibility is not a blank cheque

Begin with a distinction everyone uses without naming it: the difference between what *could* happen and what *does*. A coin could land heads or tails; it lands tails. A seed could grow into an oak; this one is eaten by a squirrel. The possible is wider than the actual, and the actual is drawn from the possible.

What is less often noticed is that the possible is itself structured. It is not "anything whatever". A coin cannot land on a colour. An acorn cannot grow into a sunflower. The range of what can happen is bounded before anything happens at all. Physics describes these bounds in many ways: conservation laws that no process violates, symmetries that constrain which interactions can occur, quantum rules that permit only certain energy levels in an atom and not the values between them. The bounds are not added after the fact to tidy up the results. They are part of what the world is.

The Relational Substrate project gives this structured space of possibility a name. It is called **T0**, or simply **constrained possibility**: what created physical order is capable of bearing, meaning the configurations and transitions that can occur at all. T0 is not a hidden substance or a second world. It is the shape of the possible within this world. The label "T0" is a piece of the project's internal shorthand. The book will mostly say "constrained possibility" instead, and uses the label only where it helps to keep levels apart. (Readers of the project's July 2026 research notes will find "T0" used there for a different idea, the first rung of a ladder of thought experiments. In this book it always means constrained possibility.)

## Forbidding and favouring

There are two quite different ways a rule can shape what happens, and the difference is worth getting exactly right, because the rest of the book depends on it.

A rule can **forbid**. It can make some transition impossible, so that the door simply is not there. A rule can also **favour**. It can make some transition more or less likely than another, while leaving every option open, like a path that is well worn rather than a door that is locked. The two are easily run together, since both "shape" outcomes. But they shape outcomes in different ways.

The project's constraint laboratory, begun in October 2026, studied this difference in the simplest setting it could devise. Imagine a handful of points, two, three, four or five, between which links can form and dissolve one at a time. Left alone, every possible link toggles with equal chance, and the system wanders through every possible pattern of links. Now add rules. A rule might say "never form the link between these two points", or "when two links are present, the third is four times as likely to form as usual", or "dissolving this link is a quarter as likely". The laboratory did not sample a few such rules. For the smallest worlds it listed every rule of this kind and every combination of up to three of them, hundreds of thousands of rule sets in all. It then worked out *exactly*, by calculation rather than simulation, how each rule set changed the long-run behaviour of the system.

The result was clean. Rules that **forbid** were the only rules that changed which patterns could be reached at all. Only they could create dead ends where the system stops, change the basic rhythm of its movement, or cut the space of possibilities in two. Rules that merely **favour** never did any of those things. They shifted how much time the system spent in each pattern, sometimes substantially, but every pattern that was reachable before stayed reachable. Two further findings were instructive. Rule sets could cancel. A later, more careful re-analysis showed that the simplest apparent case (one rule that quadrupled a likelihood and another that quartered it) was really one rule written twice with two weights, an artifact of how the census had been recorded. Genuine cancellation, where structurally different rules together leave the system exactly as it was, needed carefully matched sets of three. And some rules changed the flow of the system, how it moved between patterns, while leaving simple summaries such as the average number of links exactly where they were. More rules do not automatically mean more effect.

The laboratory then added a new kind of condition: rules that could respond to how many links were present in the whole system. Most of the new rule sets reproduced behaviour already found. The genuinely new patterns of possibility that appeared were all produced by *forbidding* rules gated by that count; rules that merely favoured never opened new possibilities. The distinction held again, in a richer setting.

The laboratory's own report is careful about what this means, and the book follows it. These are mathematical facts about a model, not discoveries about nature. What the model *exhibits* is a distinction that can be named:

- **Admissibility** is whether a transition is possible at all.
- **Tendency** is how likely an admissible transition is compared with the others.

Admissibility decides the shape of the possible. Tendency weights the possible without changing its shape. Tendency is not a probability that anyone, or anything, assigns. It names how created order is disposed among the admissible options: more often this, less often that. The model did not prove that the created world works this way. It showed that the distinction is real, sharp and fruitful in the simplest relational systems one can write down. The proposal of this book is that the same distinction runs through created physical order: that constrained possibility has both a shape and a weighting.

One caution about the word. In this book, *admissible* means "physically possible within created order". It never means "morally permitted". Chapter 12 will distinguish physical, operational and moral admissibility explicitly. Until then the word carries only its physical sense.

## Constraint makes things possible

It is natural to think of constraint as negative: a limit, a fence, a restriction on freedom. The previous section suggests otherwise. Without forbidding, nothing is shaped; without favouring, nothing is distinctive. A river is a river because of its banks. Remove them and the water does not become freer; it becomes a marsh. The rules that bound which crystal forms water can take are the reason ice exists at all, rather than a formless slush that never settles into anything.

Scripture presents created order in exactly these terms. Its account of creation is an account of division and bounding:

> `Who shut up the sea with doors, when it brake forth, as if it had issued out of the womb?… And said, Hitherto shalt thou come, but no further: and here shall thy proud waves be stayed?`
> *Job 38:8, 11*

> `Thou hast set a bound that they may not pass over; that they turn not again to cover the earth.`
> *Psalm 104:9*

> `He hath also stablished them for ever and ever: he hath made a decree which shall not pass.`
> *Psalm 148:6*

Light is divided from darkness, waters from waters, land from sea, kind from kind. Each division is a constraint, and each constraint makes room for a richer world. The theme reaches further in Scripture than physical order. Law, wisdom and discipline are presented throughout as the conditions of flourishing rather than its enemies, and Chapter 12 will return to that. Here it is enough that boundary belongs to how the world is made, not to how it is restricted.

## From the possible to the actual

Possibility, however well shaped, is not yet anything. A space of admissible configurations does not contain a single actual stone. Something must happen. Among all that can be, something comes to be.

The project calls this **actualisation**, and uses the shorthand **T1** for actual order: the configurations that have actually come to be, and the transitions actually occurring. If T0 is the shape of what can be, T1 is what is. What is actual is not a bare snapshot. As Chapter 6 will show, it carries its own history: what has happened leaves present relational traces that shape what can happen next.

An analogy helps here, and it must be used with its limits stated. Think of chess. The rules of chess define which moves are legal from any position. They are the admissibility of the game, and they are strict: a bishop cannot move like a knight. Among legal moves, some are commonly preferred and some rarely played, and that is something like tendency. And there is the game actually played: this sequence of moves, on this evening, ending in this position. That is actualisation.

The analogy captures three things well. Possibility is structured but not determining, since the rules never tell you which legal move will be made. The actual is always drawn from the admissible. And the same rules allow an endless variety of actual games. But the analogy fails at one point, and the failure is the most important part of it. In chess, the moves are chosen by *players*: minds with intentions, who want to win. In the relational substrate there are no players. Nothing inside created physical order is choosing which admissible transition to make, and nothing inside it wants anything. If the analogy is pressed to supply a player, it turns the substrate into a mind. That is precisely the error Scripture forbids.

So how does one admissible transition, rather than another, come to be actual, if nobody inside the substrate selects it? That is the question of the next chapter, and the answer the project proposes is *constrained chance*. Before reaching it, one boundary has to be set, because it governs how the answer must be understood.

## Purpose shapes what is possible

If nobody inside created order chooses, is the world purposeless? Scripture says no. The world is made with purpose, and its order serves ends:

> `All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.`
> *John 1:3*

> `While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.`
> *Genesis 8:22*

> `Thus saith the LORD, which giveth the sun for a light by day, and the ordinances of the moon and of the stars for a light by night… If those ordinances depart from before me, saith the LORD, then the seed of Israel also shall cease from being a nation before me for ever.`
> *Jeremiah 31:35-36*

The order of the heavens is called *ordinances*, and God binds his own faithfulness to it. The regularity of seedtime and harvest is promised. Creation's order is purposed through and through.

The question is *where* the purpose sits. The answer this book proposes is a theological inference, not a result of the research, and it should be stated as such:

- **Purpose shapes what is possible.** God's intention for creation is expressed in the shape of constrained possibility, in what the created order is capable of bearing and what it is not.
- **Possibility shapes what can be actualised.** Every actual configuration is drawn from that purposed space.
- **Actualisation proceeds without the substrate itself intending anything.** The substrate does not aim, want or plan. It is ordered without being an agent.

This arrangement protects two truths at once. It protects purpose: the world is not an accident, because its very possibilities were given with intent. And it protects the creatureliness of the world: physical order is lawful and real in its own right, not a puppet whose every twitch must be pulled, and not a mind in disguise. A purposive creation does not require a purposive substrate. Purpose belongs to the Creator, and the creature bears it in its form.

Two older mistakes are avoided. One is the view that God acts in every event as a fresh, bespoke intervention, so that created things have no real operations of their own; this makes nonsense of the stable regularity Scripture promises. The other is the view that God made the world and withdrew; this contradicts the continuous upholding of Colossians 1:17 and Hebrews 1:3. The position here is the mainstream Christian one: created order operates lawfully and really, under God's continual upholding, within a range of possibility that his purpose has shaped. The book calls this **non-agentic runtime**. The physical order is purposed without being purposeful in itself.

One more caution belongs here, and Chapter 14 will develop it. To say that physical order runs without the substrate intending anything is not to say that God's action is confined to the substrate's possibilities. God is not one more cause among the causes of the world. He is the Creator of all its possibilities. The book makes no claim to describe how God acts in miracles, in providence or in redemption. It describes the ordinary operation of what he has made.

## The stone, again

The stone on the path, then, is a configuration drawn from a structured space of possibility: the crystal forms its elements admit, the ways its grains could settle, the shapes erosion could leave. That space was shaped by its Creator's purpose. Within it, the stone's actual history unfolded through admissible transitions, none of which the substrate chose. Something weighted some transitions more than others, and something made this history actual rather than another. The next chapter takes up that something, and a word that is very easily misunderstood: chance.
