# Chapter 6: Identity Through Change

The ancient Greek thinker Heraclitus is remembered for saying that you cannot step into the same river twice. The water you stepped into is gone downstream, and new water has taken its place. Yet everyone who lives beside a river calls it by one name and knows it is the same river this year as last. A candle flame is the same: the burning gas is replaced from moment to moment, while the flame stays steady and recognisable. Your own body is similar. Over the years much of its material is replaced, yet you are not a different person from the child in your old photographs.

What, then, makes a thing the same thing through change? The old answer, rarely stated but deeply assumed, is that a thing stays the same because its stuff stays the same: some core of unchanging material carried along through time. The river, the flame and the body show that this cannot be right in general. Their stuff changes and they persist. The previous chapter added a sharper puzzle. If what happens at the deepest level is constrained chance, so that the history beneath every thing is open at each step, what keeps the stone on the path a stone?

## What every allowed change preserves

A first answer is that a thing stays the same because every change available to it preserves something. In the illustration of Chapter 5, the relations changed constantly and no two histories were alike, yet the *total* never moved. The number did not live in any particular relation or arrangement. It lived in what every allowed change preserved.

In mathematical language, such a preserved quantity is an **invariant**: something a set of transformations leaves unchanged. Physics describes the world this way at its foundations. The great conservation laws, of energy, momentum and electric charge, say that certain quantities are left unchanged by every physical process. Early in the twentieth century the mathematician Emmy Noether proved that such conservation laws are tied to symmetries: each conserved quantity corresponds to a way the laws of physics stay the same under some transformation. Physicists classify particles by such invariants. An electron is identified by properties that every electron shares, and that no allowed interaction alters without turning it into something else. It is not identified by being a particular bit of stuff.

There is also a more visual kind of invariant. Tie a knot in a loop of string, then pull, twist and squash the loop without cutting it. The knot can be made to look very different, but it cannot be undone. The *kind* of knot is invariant under every deformation that does not cut the string, and mathematicians measure it with whole numbers that can only stay the same or jump. The knot illustrates how identity can be carried by organisation rather than by stuff. It is not a claim about what particles are.

So the first lesson is that things persist through change because the changes available to them leave something invariant. That lesson is sound. But it is not the whole story, and the rest of the chapter is about what it leaves out.

## The same in what respect?

Imagine two coins in your palm. They are the same size, the same weight, stamped with the same design. For every purpose you have this morning they are the same. Then one is passed to a bank, tested, and found to be a counterfeit. The difference was there all along, in its metal and its making. Nothing you did with it this morning could find it. A bank's test could.

Or imagine two keys, cut by eye to look alike. Laid side by side they are indistinguishable. Try them in the lock, and one turns and the other does not.

These cases show something an invariant alone does not capture. Whether two things are "the same" always depends on *what could tell them apart*. Sameness of one visible summary, such as size, weight or design, is not sameness in every respect that might later matter. What decides whether a difference is real and relevant is whether some admissible future interaction could detect it: the bank's test, or the lock.

The same thing appears, exactly, in the simplest relational models. Rule systems sorted into one family by their coarse behaviour turned out, when examined exactly, to be many distinct systems, some of them sharing every recorded summary while differing in how unpredictably they moved. Observable equality is not equality of the underlying law. A description can map many different underlying things onto one visible picture, and the differences it hides can still matter for what those things do next.

![Figure 3. Same appearance, different identity. Two states that look identical under one description can differ in a hidden relational detail. That is the problem of **projection**, or coarse-graining. Every description summarises. Summaries are indispensable, and science could not proceed without them. But a summary can hide distinctions, and whether the hidden distinctions matter depends on whether anything that can actually happen will draw on them.

The principle can be stated simply. **A distinction can safely be ignored only where no reachable future consequence can draw on it.** If nothing that can happen will ever tell two states apart, treating them as one loses nothing. If something can, they were never fully the same.

## Identity as relational equivalence

With these cases in hand, the account of identity can be stated more carefully.

**A thing continues to be the same kind of thing, in the respect that matters, when the changes it undergoes preserve its relevant equivalence class: the set of states that no admissible future interaction, of the kind that matters for that thing, could tell apart from it.**

"Equivalence class" is a technical phrase for a plain idea. It means all the states that count as "the same" for a given purpose. The coins were in the same class for shopping this morning and in different classes for a bank.

An invariant, then, is one way of *carrying* or *certifying* such a class. A conserved total and a knot's whole number are excellent certificates. They mark, in a single quantity, what every admissible change preserves. But an invariant is not necessarily the whole of identity. Two states can share every invariant one has thought to measure and still differ in ways a later process will draw on, as the counterfeit coin shows.

This is not a slide into "sameness is in the eye of the beholder". The criterion is not what a human happens to notice or measure. It is what admissible interactions *within created order* could discriminate, whether or not anyone is watching. The counterfeit was different whether or not it was ever tested. Sameness of this kind is relational, because it concerns what can distinguish one state from another. It is not arbitrary.

Nor is the criterion new. The mathematics of stochastic processes has an established construction, part of the field called computational mechanics, that counts two histories of a process as equivalent exactly when they imply the same probabilities for everything that could follow. The resulting classes, called *causal states*, are provably the smallest description that predicts as well as any description can. The book's account of sameness belongs to that tradition. Two limits come with it. Predictive equivalence is always relative to a chosen description of what counts as the future, which is the problem of projection again. And it is not the same as being the same individual, which is the next question.

## Two questions about sameness

Everything so far answers one question: *what kind of thing, or what state, is this*, judged by what any reachable future interaction could tell apart? There is a second question, and it must not be folded into the first: *is this the same individual*? That is, is it the same continuing thing, and not merely an equivalent replacement?

The two can come apart. Suppose a thing is destroyed, and later something is built that no defined future interaction could distinguish from it: same structure, same behaviour, same invariants. The new thing belongs to the same equivalence class as the old. That does not settle whether it *is* the old thing. Two coins struck from the same die can be indistinguishable in every test and still be two coins. A second coin minted to replace a lost one is not the lost coin found again.

What answers the second question is not equivalence alone but continuity: an unbroken lineage, a history carried forward, the provenance described below. A system can rebuild structure that works, and even match a lost organisation, without recovering the particular lineage that was lost. The project's exploratory models met exactly that. Four achievements need to be kept apart:

- restoring *function*, so that the thing does the job again;
- restoring *organisation*, so that it has the same pattern again;
- restoring the *equivalence class*, so that nothing reachable could tell it from the original;
- restoring the *same individual*, the same lineage carried on.

The first three can each happen without the fourth. What carries individual continuity in physical things is an open question. For the rest of this chapter, *identity* means the first kind of sameness unless the second is named, and *individual continuity* means the second.

## Provenance: history in the present

There is a further consequence. Sometimes what distinguishes two present states is how they came to be. A coin and its counterfeit may differ in nothing but their making. A bent and straightened paper clip looks like a new one but is weaker, because its bending is still present in its crystal structure. A healed bone looks whole but carries the remodelling of its fracture.

The name for this is **provenance**: past distinctions that survive in present relational structure. Provenance is not a separate record kept somewhere else, a ledger attached to the thing. It is embodied in the thing's present configuration: in residual stresses and orientations, in correlations among its parts, in what is accessible to it now and what is not. And it belongs to the thing's identity exactly where it remains consequential, where some future interaction could draw on it. Where nothing could, it can be set aside for the purposes of identity. That does not make it unreal. What happened is carried in present relations whether or not anything can now trace it back. There is no separate ledger. The changed relations are the record, and consequence can persist where its provenance can no longer be recovered.

This matters for how the book understands what is actual. Chapter 4 called the actual order of things **actuality** (T1): what has come to be from among what was possible. Actuality is not a succession of memoryless snapshots, each owing nothing to the last. A present state can carry the relational consequences of its own past: its provenance, its accumulated constraints, what has become accessible to it and what has been closed off. What happens next depends on all of that, not merely on how things look at this instant.

## Persistence

**Persistence** can now be described more fully. It is the continued re-actualisation of a thing within its relevant equivalence class, and, for a continuing individual, along an unbroken history. The river persists because, moment by moment, flowing water keeps re-establishing a river-shaped pattern within its banks, carrying with it its channel's history. The flame persists because combustion keeps re-establishing a flame-shaped region of light and heat. On the relational proposal the stone persists in the same way, though far more slowly: its crystal lattices and the bonds that hold its grains keep re-establishing themselves through the small, ceaseless fluctuations of its atoms.

Chapter 5's three qualifiers apply: stability is of something, against some disturbance, for some time. The stone persists against rain for centuries and against a hammer for a moment. No created thing is stable against everything forever. To say that something persists is to say that the changes it actually undergoes stay within the class that preserves what it is.

The project's later models added a discipline to the word. *What is likely* and *what lasts* are different questions: two model systems agreeing on how often each state occurs, how dense and how active they are, differed more than twentyfold in how long their patterns survived. So a claim of persistence must say what it was compared with, must separate *reaching* a state from *surviving* in it, and should show that a structure outlasts its own parts before treating it as a point of reference (Appendix E.8).

## Preserving is not repairing

Preservation has a firm boundary. When a relation in the Chapter 5 model was destroyed rather than rearranged, the system never recovered the original number. It settled cleanly, and confidently, on a different one.

An equivalence class survives every change that keeps a thing inside it. It does not survive changes that move the thing outside it, and conservation alone does not restore what is lost. Identity, on this account, is robust but not indestructible. And as the previous section showed, even rebuilding something into the same equivalence class would not by itself restore the same individual. Where lineage was recovered in the project's explorations, it sometimes depended on help from the surrounding context, and sometimes it was not recovered at all. If anything is to be recovered after damage, the distinctions needed for recovery must survive somewhere: in redundancy, in a template, in a wider order, or in an agent able to restore them. The point will matter later. Living things repair themselves because they carry such surviving constraints (Chapter 11). Restoration in the theological sense is not the world's invariants reasserting themselves unaided (Chapter 15).

## Memory without a memory substance

Provenance also gives the clearest sense of what memory is at the physical level. Bend a paper clip back and forth and it does not return to its first condition; eventually it breaks. A piece of iron, magnetised and released, stays magnetised. Its present state depends on its history, not only on its present surroundings. Physicists call this **hysteresis**, and it is well understood in many materials. That much is mainstream science.

Three further pieces of support, of different strengths, point the same way. In published metal-fatigue data the *order* of loads matters: gentle cycles first leave a part more durable than the reverse at equal totals, though the simplest standard model says order makes no difference. The same direction appears in three unrelated domains, but the project's model predicted the size of the effect badly, so this is a directional finding only. In a small relational model, hiding a genuinely three-way relation made the visible links behave as though they remembered their recent past, although nothing in the rules recorded history (Chapter 9). And in later models one visible arrangement of links faced different futures depending on how it came about, until the carried history was counted as part of the present state. These are model results, not a law of memory in nature.

The book's proposal unites these. **Memory, at the physical level, is past distinctions embodied in present relations so that they alter what is currently reachable, how likely it is, or how a thing responds.** No separate memory substance is needed. The bent paper clip remembers its bending in its bent crystal structure and nowhere else. **Status:** RS working hypothesis. It will be useful later, when the book speaks of habits, of character, and of the slow hardening Scripture describes (Hebrews 3:13). There it bears on the embodied consequences of moral acts (Chapter 12). It never explains what makes an act right or wrong.

## Handling and perturbation

Chapter 3 fixed a working definition of **material**: persistent, spatially individuated identity that can in principle be mechanically manipulated or relocated while remaining the same identifiable thing. This chapter's tools show what it picks out and where it is tested. There are two quite different ways of acting on things. You can **handle** the stone: pick it up and carry it, and it arrives as the same stone. Or you can **perturb** something: change its condition, behaviour or configuration without relocating any separately identifiable thing. Clap your hands and the air carries a sound across the room at about 340 metres a second. The air molecules are material. But no molecule crosses the room with the sound. Each moves back and forth a tiny distance, and what crosses the room is an organised pattern of pressure, borne by a succession of different air.

The sound is real, and it is consequential, but it is not one more material object. The book calls it **material organisation**: a real configuration, pattern or process instantiated through material reality, whose organisational identity need not be an additional, independently material entity. Four things in the case need to be kept apart:

1. the **material carrier**, the air;
2. the **organised activity** actually occurring, the travelling variation of pressure;
3. the **identity of the organisation**, this note, or this spoken word;
4. the **mathematical description**, a wave equation.

None of these is any of the others. An equation is not the sound it describes, and the sound's occurrence does not make it an extra substance. Both mistakes are common.

The distinction is useful. It is not yet a universal classification, and several cases test it.

- **A bent ruler** is a material individual in a changed condition. Bending perturbs it; it does not add a new thing.
- **Magnetised iron** keeps its magnetisation for years. The magnetisation is an organisation of the iron's own atomic magnets, not a second object inside it.
- **A river** persists while its water is replaced. You can carry away water, but not the river.
- **A solitary wave** can behave very like a thing. In 1834 the engineer John Scott Russell followed on horseback a single rounded heap of water travelling along a canal near Edinburgh, keeping its shape for more than a mile. The water it passed through did not travel with it. Certain magnetic materials host tiny stable whirls in their magnetic order, called skyrmions, which can be counted and pushed along by electric currents.
- **Vibrations in a crystal** are described by physics as quantised packets called phonons, which carry energy and momentum and can be counted and scattered like particles. Nobody concludes that phonons are material entities in addition to the crystal. The particle-like mathematics fits; the ontology is settled on other grounds.
- **A composite**, such as the stone, is a material individual whose parts include material individuals, its grains. Not every constituent need be one: the electrons that bind its atoms are not separately individuated. A material individual can be composed partly of constituents that are not.

These cases do not dissolve the definition. They refine it in two ways. First, some material organisations acquire entity-like individuality: the solitary wave and the skyrmion persist, can be individuated and can be moved. Where that individuality belongs to an organisation of a distinct material carrier, the book counts it as *material organisation with entity-like individuality*, not as an extra material entity. That is a clarification, not a widening. Second, whether something counts as a material individual can depend on its circumstances. A single electron held in a trap is individuated and can be moved about. Electrons sharing one collective state in a metal are not separately individuated at all. Materiality belongs first to individuated configurations, and only derivatively to kinds of thing.

One consequence should be faced squarely. If the book's central thesis is right, then even carrying the stone is, at the deepest level, the continued re-establishment of a stable form, as the section on persistence suggested. The difference between handling and perturbation is therefore not that "stuff" travels in one and not the other. It is a difference in which identity is preserved, and in whether that identity belongs to an organisation of some distinct material carrier. That is why the definition is written in terms of identity.

## Matter as stable form

We can now say what the project proposes the stone *is*. On the relational proposal, **matter is stable form within the relational substrate**: configurations whose relevant equivalence classes are preserved by the changes they ordinarily undergo, so that they persist, can be encountered, and act on other things in consistent ways. Atoms, on this view, are very stable relational forms rather than tiny self-existing pellets. Molecules are larger stable forms built of coupled atomic forms. The stone is an immense, coherently coupled configuration that has stayed stable long enough to lie on a path and be picked up.

This does not make matter less real. It makes its reality a matter of form held stable rather than of brute stuff. Mainstream physics already undermines the picture of mass as simply the sum of tiny lumps. Most of the mass of ordinary matter arises from the energy of the interactions that bind quarks inside protons and neutrons, not from the masses of the quarks themselves. Inertial mass is measured as resistance to acceleration. The Relational Substrate reading is that what we measure as matter's properties are, all the way down, properties of stable relational form. **Status:** ontological interpretation.

Two kinds of emergence must be kept apart here. **Emergence within matter**, in which higher-order identities arise from the organisation of existing matter, is observed everywhere: crystals, flames, solitary waves, cells. **The emergence of matter**, in which material individuality itself is constituted within a created reality that is not material, is Proposition B of Chapter 3. *That* matter is so founded is the author's premise. *How* it is constituted is the book's working hypothesis. The first kind of emergence supplies analogies, constraints and questions for the second, not demonstrations of it. The skyrmion is the closest analogy: a pattern within matter that has acquired persistent, individuated, movable identity. On Proposition B, a material individual would stand to the substrate somewhat as a skyrmion stands to a magnet, except that the substrate is not material, while everything about the skyrmion happens within matter.

The author's remark that "matter begets matter" belongs with the first kind. It names a regularity everyone observes: material things come from, and are composed of, other material things. It does not assert that matter must ultimately come from prior matter. Physics has an instructive edge case. When energetic radiation passes close to an atomic nucleus, it can produce an electron and its antiparticle where there was no matter before. Material individuals arise from radiation, which physics itself distinguishes from matter. That fits Proposition B, and it does not establish it, because an ontology of fundamental fields describes it equally well.

The book's central ontological question can now be put exactly. **How does a configuration of something that is not itself material acquire persistent, manipulable individuality?** That materiality is such an acquisition is the author's premise. How it happens has not been shown. The question is coherent, it fits what is observed, and it can be investigated. Chapter 8 asks how such individuality could arise.

Scripture speaks of creaturely form as given and shaped:

> `But God giveth it a body as it hath pleased him, and to every seed his own body.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:38*

> `For ever, O LORD, thy word is settled in heaven. Thy faithfulness is unto all generations: thou hast established the earth, and it abideth. They continue this day according to thine ordinances: for all are thy servants.`
> *Psalm 119:89-91*

The earth "abideth", it persists, and it does so "according to thine ordinances". Created persistence is real and reliable, and it is held.

## Two kinds of sameness

The psalms also guard against a confusion. Scripture speaks of another kind of sameness, of a different order entirely:

> `They shall perish, but thou shalt endure: yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment; as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they shall be changed: But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end.`
> *Psalm 102:26-27*

Created things persist by remaining within an equivalence class through change, and they can wear out like a garment. God does not persist in this way at all. His sameness is not the preservation of anything through admissible transitions, because God is not a configuration and has no admissibility imposed on him from beyond. The word "same" is used of both, truly, but across the deepest category boundary there is. Creaturely identity is received and sustained. God's is underived.

The same caution applies, at a different boundary, to human persons. Persons have bodies, and bodies persist in the way described here. But whether a *person's* identity is simply an equivalence class of physical configuration is a different question, and the book's answer, given in Chapters 12 and 15, is no. Personal identity resembles both kinds of sameness described here, and the resemblance helps. It is not the same thing.

## Succession, cadence and time

If change is the actualisation of admissible transitions, then time, on the relational proposal, is the **ordering of actualisation**: the before-and-after of change itself, rather than an independent container that would still be ticking if nothing happened. This is an old view, held in different forms by Leibniz and others. It must sit with what is measured: clocks run at different rates at different heights and speeds, exactly as general relativity predicts. General relativity's mathematics treats time geometrically, as part of spacetime. Whether spacetime is a substance or an ordering of relations among events is a question of interpretation, not of measurement.

The order has a direction, and the direction comes from consequence. When one thing happens because of another, the dependence runs one way: the bell sounds because it was struck, and not the reverse. The book calls this directed order of actual consequence **succession**. On the author's account it is not produced by anything more basic. It belongs to consequence itself, in an order made by a God of order, and it is prior to every measurement of it. That is not to say that every logical implication is an event in time. Succession concerns what actually happens and what follows from it. **Status:** theological and philosophical premise.

Three other things are easily confused with succession. The first is **cadence**, the ordered pattern of occasions and events. Picture a ferry that calls at a jetty twelve times a day. Each call is an occasion on which a passenger could cross, an *opportunity* in the sense of Chapter 4, and the pattern of calls is the **opportunity cadence**. On a given day a passenger crosses four times. That pattern, of what actually happened, is the **actualisation cadence**. The two differ, and neither excludes the other: the calls go on whether or not anyone boards, and every crossing happened at a call. A cadence need not be regular. A **rhythm** is a recurrence with a cadence, and it may be irregular or made of several cadences at once.

The second is **measurement**. To say how long something took is to compare one cadence with another taken as a reference: the swing of a pendulum, the turning of the Earth, the vibration of a caesium atom. Clocks are physical processes, and seconds are a convention. The succession they count is not.

The third is a **reference cadence** belonging to created order itself: something recurring steadily enough for comparison to be possible at all. The author's favoured candidate is oscillation (Chapter 8), an economical way for a relational order to furnish comparable intervals without an outside clock. It is a mechanism candidate. It would make succession measurable; it would not produce it. Scripture gives the lights of heaven "for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years" (Genesis 1:14), created regularity given as a measure. That is the text's statement of purpose. It names no mechanism.

The project's simplest models show why the premise has to be stated rather than assumed. In them direction does not come free. Rules that merely make some states more likely give preferred states but no direction. Direction appears only with a tendency that favours going round a cycle of changes one way, or with a rule that makes some change one-way, and its characteristic pattern is a handover: a link forms, and the link that supported it dissolves. A model has to be given direction. That is a fact about models, and no model claim about time is free of the modeller's clock (Appendix E.8). What remains open is mechanism: how clocks are tied to relational structure, which gravity makes pressing (Chapter 9); whether persistent consequence is what makes the past irrecoverable; and how an opportunity that passes unused is to be counted. Chapter 13 takes up a further sense of time, its significance for persons as moral opportunity.

## The stone, made manifest

The stone is a stable, history-bearing configuration. It is also *manifest*: it presents itself to other things, scattering light, resisting pressure, making itself detectable and encounterable. Manifestation in this sense belongs to physical order. It is something a stone does whether or not anyone is looking. It is not the same as anyone's *awareness* of the stone, which belongs to the category of persons, as Chapter 12 will insist. For the stone to make any difference to you, some distinction in it must become consequential to you. In the daylight scene, light is how that happens. What is required for any distinction to become consequential to another state is the subject of the next chapter.
