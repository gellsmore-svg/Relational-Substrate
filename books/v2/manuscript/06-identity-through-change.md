# Chapter 6: Identity Through Change

The ancient Greek thinker Heraclitus is remembered for saying that you cannot step into the same river twice. The water you stepped into is gone downstream, and new water has taken its place. Yet everyone who lives beside a river calls it by one name and knows it is the same river this year as last. A candle flame is the same: the burning gas is replaced from moment to moment, while the flame stays steady and recognisable. Your own body is similar. Over the years much of its material is replaced, yet you are not a different person from the child in your old photographs.

What, then, makes a thing the same thing through change? The old answer, rarely stated but deeply assumed, is that a thing stays the same because its stuff stays the same: some core of unchanging material carried along through time. The river, the flame and the body show that this cannot be right in general. Their stuff changes and they persist. The previous chapter added a sharper puzzle. If what happens at the deepest level is constrained chance, so that the history beneath every thing is open at each step, what keeps the stone on the path a stone?

## What every allowed change preserves

The stochastic calculator suggested a first answer. It added numbers by letting populations of positive and negative relations change at random. Relations moved, neutral pairs appeared and vanished, opposites cancelled, and no two runs followed the same path. What stayed fixed was the *total*: positive relations minus negative ones. Every allowed change left that total untouched. The number's identity did not live in any particular relation, all of which changed, nor in any particular arrangement, which was never the same twice. It lived in what every allowed change preserved. In 4,000 deliberate disturbances of the kinds the rules allowed, the number never once changed.

In mathematical language, the number was an **invariant**: a quantity that a set of transformations leaves unchanged. Physics works this way at its foundations. The great conservation laws, of energy, momentum and electric charge, say that certain quantities are left unchanged by every physical process. Early in the twentieth century the mathematician Emmy Noether proved that such conservation laws are tied to symmetries: each conserved quantity corresponds to a way the laws of physics stay the same under some transformation. Physicists classify particles by such invariants. An electron is identified by properties that every electron shares, and that no allowed interaction alters without turning it into something else. It is not identified by being a particular bit of stuff.

There is also a more visual kind of invariant, which the project explored in July 2026. Tie a knot in a loop of string, then pull, twist and squash the loop without cutting it. The knot can be made to look very different, but it cannot be undone. The *kind* of knot is invariant under every deformation that does not cut the string. Mathematicians measure such features with whole numbers, which can only stay the same or jump. The project's computer models confirmed, without adjusting any parameter, that a knotted structure in a continuous field carried its identity in a whole-number invariant of this sort. More ambitious hopes attached to that work, such as deriving the properties of the electron, hold only under conditions that remain unproven, and the project records that limit openly. The knot is used here as an illustration, not as a claim about what particles are.

So the first lesson is that things persist through change because the changes available to them leave something invariant. That lesson is sound. But it is not the whole story, and the rest of the chapter is about what it leaves out.

## The same in what respect?

Imagine two coins in your palm. They are the same size, the same weight, stamped with the same design. For every purpose you have this morning they are the same. Then one is passed to a bank, tested, and found to be a counterfeit. The difference was there all along, in its metal and its making. Nothing you did with it this morning could find it. A bank's test could.

Or imagine two keys, cut by eye to look alike. Laid side by side they are indistinguishable. Try them in the lock, and one turns and the other does not.

These cases show something an invariant alone does not capture. Whether two things are "the same" always depends on *what could tell them apart*. Sameness of one visible summary, such as size, weight or design, is not sameness in every respect that might later matter. What decides whether a difference is real and relevant is whether some admissible future interaction could detect it: the bank's test, or the lock.

The project's constraint laboratory met exactly this in its own formal world. In October 2026 it re-analysed its census of small relational rule systems with exact calculation. It found that systems sorted into one family by their coarse behaviour, the patterns they could reach and the moves they favoured, often turned out to be many distinct systems once examined exactly. In one family there were 1,042 different exact behaviours under a single label. In the largest systems it studied, two rule systems shared every summary statistic the laboratory recorded, yet differed in a measure of how unpredictable their movement was. The laboratory put it plainly: observable equality is not kernel equality. A description can map many different underlying things onto one visible picture. When it does, the differences it hides may still matter for what those things do next.

That is the problem of **projection**, or coarse-graining. Every description summarises. Summaries are indispensable, and science could not proceed without them. But a summary can hide distinctions, and whether the hidden distinctions matter depends on whether anything that can actually happen will draw on them.

The project's exploratory work on this, carried out in conversational research sessions not yet published as formal experiment records, arrived at a principle that can be stated simply. **A distinction can safely be ignored only where no reachable future consequence can draw on it.** If nothing that can happen will ever tell two states apart, treating them as one loses nothing. If something can, they were never fully the same.

## Identity as relational equivalence

With these cases in hand, the account of identity can be stated more carefully.

**A thing continues to be what it is when the changes it undergoes preserve its relevant equivalence class: the set of states that no admissible future interaction, of the kind that matters for that thing, could tell apart from it.**

"Equivalence class" is a technical phrase for a plain idea. It means all the states that count as "the same" for a given purpose. The coins were in the same class for shopping this morning and in different classes for a bank. The number in the calculator stayed in the same class through every rearrangement, because nothing the calculator could do would ever distinguish one arrangement from another as a different number.

An invariant, then, is one way of *carrying* or *certifying* such a class. The calculator's total and the knot's whole number are excellent certificates. They mark, in a single quantity, what every admissible change preserves. But an invariant is not necessarily the whole of identity. Two states can share every invariant one has thought to measure and still differ in ways a later process will draw on, as the counterfeit coin shows.

This is not a slide into "sameness is in the eye of the beholder". The criterion is not what a human happens to notice or measure. It is what admissible interactions *within created order* could discriminate, whether or not anyone is watching. The counterfeit was different whether or not it was ever tested. Identity is relational, because it concerns what can distinguish one state from another. It is not arbitrary.

## Provenance: history in the present

There is a further consequence. Sometimes what distinguishes two present states is how they came to be. A coin and its counterfeit may differ in nothing but their making. A bent and straightened paper clip looks like a new one but is weaker, because its bending is still present in its crystal structure. A healed bone looks whole but carries the remodelling of its fracture.

The name for this is **provenance**: past distinctions that survive in present relational structure. Provenance is not a separate record kept somewhere else, a ledger attached to the thing. It is embodied in the thing's present configuration: in residual stresses and orientations, in correlations among its parts, in what is accessible to it now and what is not. And it belongs to the thing's identity exactly where it remains consequential, where some future interaction could draw on it. Where nothing could, it can be set aside.

This matters for how the book understands what is actual. Chapter 4 called the actual order of things **actuality** (T1): what has come to be from among what was possible. Actuality is not a succession of memoryless snapshots, each owing nothing to the last. A present state can carry the relational consequences of its own past: its provenance, its accumulated constraints, what has become accessible to it and what has been closed off. What happens next depends on all of that, not merely on how things look at this instant.

## Persistence

**Persistence** can now be described more fully. It is the continued re-actualisation of a thing within its relevant equivalence class. The river persists because, moment by moment, flowing water keeps re-establishing a river-shaped pattern within its banks, carrying with it its channel's history. The flame persists because combustion keeps re-establishing a flame-shaped region of light and heat. On the relational proposal the stone persists in the same way, though far more slowly: its crystal lattices and the bonds that hold its grains keep re-establishing themselves through the small, ceaseless fluctuations of its atoms.

Stability is always stability *of something*, *against some kind of disturbance*, *over some length of time*. The stone persists against rain for centuries and against a hammer for a moment. No created thing is stable against everything forever. To say that something persists is to say that the changes it actually undergoes stay within the class that preserves what it is.

## Preserving is not repairing

The calculator also drew a firm boundary. The researchers tested what happened if a relation was simply *deleted*: not rearranged or neutrally paired but destroyed, so that the total changed. In 1,800 such trials the system never once recovered the original number. It settled cleanly, and confidently, on a different one.

An equivalence class survives every change that keeps a thing inside it. It does not survive changes that move the thing outside it, and conservation alone does not restore what is lost. Identity, on this account, is robust but not indestructible. There is a further caution, which the project's exploratory work made sharp. Rebuilding something that works again, or that shows the same organisation again, is not the same as restoring *the same* thing. In those explorations, systems sometimes regenerated useful, well-organised structure without recovering the particular lineage they had lost, and identity-specific recovery sometimes depended on help from the surrounding context. Function restored, organisation restored, the same equivalence class restored, and the same lineage restored are four different achievements. If anything is to be recovered after damage, the distinctions needed for recovery must survive somewhere: in redundancy, in a template, in a wider order, or in an agent able to restore them. The point will matter later. Living things repair themselves because they carry such surviving constraints (Chapter 10). Restoration in the theological sense is not the world's invariants reasserting themselves unaided (Chapter 14).

## Memory without a memory substance

Provenance also gives the clearest sense of what memory is at the physical level. Bend a paper clip back and forth and it does not return to its first condition; eventually it breaks. A piece of iron, magnetised and released, stays magnetised. Its present state depends on its history, not only on its present surroundings. Physicists call this **hysteresis**, and it is well understood in many materials. That much is mainstream science.

The project has added evidence and proposals of different strengths, and they should be kept apart.

- **Directional evidence (June 2026).** The project tested whether the *order* of loads matters in metal fatigue, using published data. The test asked whether gentle cycles first, then harsh, leaves a part more durable than the reverse when the totals are equal. The standard simple model says order makes no difference. The data showed the predicted direction, and so did three further unrelated domains. The project's model predicted the size of the effect badly, many times too small, and the project records it as a directional finding only.
- **Carried state in the calculator.** In the calculator, a state flag carried forward from earlier steps changed which moves were available later. That is a designed instance of history altering what is admissible next.
- **Conceptual conclusion from exploratory research.** A prior path can be encoded in present relational structure without any separate memory substance.
- **Memory without a history variable (recorded, October 2026).** The constraint laboratory added a genuinely three-way relation to its smallest systems. When that relation was hidden from view, the visible two-way links behaved as though they remembered their recent past, although nothing in the rules recorded history. Apparent memory can come from present structure that a description leaves out (Chapter 8).

The proposal that unites these is this. **Memory, at the physical level, is past distinctions embodied in present relations so that they alter future admissibility, likelihood or response.** The bent paper clip remembers its bending in its bent crystal structure and nowhere else. That general claim is a working hypothesis. It will be useful later, when the book speaks of habits, of character, and of the slow hardening Scripture describes (Hebrews 3:13), always as a structural analogy and never as an explanation of moral life.

## Matter as stable form

We can now say what the project proposes the stone *is*. On the relational proposal, **matter is stable form within the relational substrate**: configurations whose relevant equivalence classes are preserved by the changes they ordinarily undergo, so that they persist, can be encountered, and act on other things in consistent ways. Atoms, on this view, are very stable relational forms rather than tiny self-existing pellets. Molecules are larger stable forms built of coupled atomic forms. The stone is an immense, coherently coupled configuration that has stayed stable long enough to lie on a path and be picked up.

This does not make matter less real. It makes its reality a matter of form held stable rather than of brute stuff. Mainstream physics already undermines the picture of mass as simply the sum of tiny lumps. Most of the mass of ordinary matter arises from the energy of the interactions that bind quarks inside protons and neutrons, not from the masses of the quarks themselves. Inertial mass is measured as resistance to acceleration. The Relational Substrate reading is that what we measure as matter's properties are, all the way down, properties of stable relational form. That is an interpretation, not part of the standard definition, and the book holds it as one.

Scripture speaks of creaturely form as given and shaped:

> `But God giveth it a body as it hath pleased him, and to every seed his own body.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:38*

> `For ever, O LORD, thy word is settled in heaven. Thy faithfulness is unto all generations: thou hast established the earth, and it abideth. They continue this day according to thine ordinances: for all are thy servants.`
> *Psalm 119:89-91*

The earth "abideth", it persists, and it does so "according to thine ordinances". Created persistence is real and reliable, and it is held.

## Two kinds of sameness

The psalms also guard against a confusion. Scripture speaks of another kind of sameness, of a different order entirely:

> `They shall perish, but thou shalt endure: yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment; as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they shall be changed: But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end.`
> *Psalm 102:26-27*

Created things persist by remaining within an equivalence class through change, and they can wear out like a garment. God does not persist in this way at all. His sameness is not the preservation of anything through admissible transitions, because God is not a configuration, does not undergo transitions, and has no admissibility imposed on him from beyond. The word "same" is used of both, truly, but across the deepest category boundary there is. Creaturely identity is received and sustained. God's is underived.

The same caution applies, at a different boundary, to human persons. Persons have bodies, and bodies persist in the way described here. But whether a *person's* identity is simply an equivalence class of physical configuration is a different question, and the book's answer, given in Chapters 11 and 14, is no. Personal identity resembles the identity of stable forms, and the resemblance helps. It is not the same thing.

## A word about time

If change is the actualisation of admissible transitions, then time, on the relational proposal, is the **ordering of actualisation**: the before-and-after of change itself, rather than an independent container that would still be ticking if nothing happened. This is an old view, held in different forms by Leibniz and others. It is an ontological interpretation, not a scientific result, and it must sit with general relativity, which treats time geometrically and has been confirmed with great precision. Clocks really do run at measurably different rates at different heights and speeds. The first edition offered a specific substrate mechanism for that effect. It was never made quantitative, so this edition leaves it to the technical notes in Appendix B and claims only the general reading: clocks are physical processes, and the time they keep is the ordering of the changes they undergo.

## The stone, made manifest

The stone is a stable, history-bearing configuration. It is also *manifest*: it presents itself to other things, scattering light, resisting pressure, making itself detectable and encounterable. Manifestation in this sense belongs to physical order. It is something a stone does whether or not anyone is looking. It is not the same as anyone's *awareness* of the stone, which belongs to the category of persons, as Chapter 11 will insist. To reach you, the stone's manifestation must be brought from where it is to where you are. In the daylight scene, the light does that. How it does so, how a distinction here becomes a consequence there, is the subject of the next chapter.
