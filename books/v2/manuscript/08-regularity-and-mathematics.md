# Chapter 8: Regularity, Coordination, and the Place of Mathematics

Pump up a bicycle tyre and feel it grow firm. The pressure you feel is the combined effect of an enormous number of air molecules, more than a hundred thousand billion billion in an ordinary tyre, striking the inner wall. Each molecule moves erratically, colliding with others billions of times a second, its path impossible to follow. Yet the pressure is steady, and it obeys a simple law: squeeze the air into half the volume at the same temperature and the pressure doubles. Out of the tumult of individual molecules comes a regularity you can write in a single line.

The previous chapters proposed that what happens at the deepest level of created order is constrained chance, and that distinctions reach us through chains of transmission, each made of many chancy events. This chapter asks how such a world can be so reliably regular. Light bends through a lens by exactly the amount the optician calculates. The stone is the same weight tomorrow. Planets keep their orbits for billions of years. And the chapter asks what mathematics, which describes this regularity so well, is in relation to the world it describes.

## Regularity from many chances

Part of the answer has long been understood, and it belongs to mainstream science rather than to the Relational Substrate project. When very many chance events are combined, their total can be highly predictable even though each event is open. Flip a coin ten times and you might get seven heads. Flip it ten million times and the proportion of heads will almost certainly lie within a tenth of a per cent of a half. This is the **law of large numbers**. In its simplest familiar form it concerns independent events. Mathematicians have proved versions of it for events that are dependent on one another, provided the dependence fades enough over time or distance.

Physics is built on this in many places. The gas laws describe the combined behaviour of countless molecular collisions. The restless jittering of tiny particles suspended in water, and the steady spreading of one substance through another, were traced in 1905 by Albert Einstein to the random jostling of molecules. His analysis became one of the first strong pieces of evidence that molecules are real. The precise half-lives of radioactive elements (Chapter 5) are the aggregate of countless open individual decays. In each case a deterministic-looking law at the large scale is the stable behaviour of many chancy events at the small scale.

Physicists call such laws **effective**: they hold to great precision at the scale where they apply, without describing what happens underneath. The book uses the phrase **effective determinism** for behaviour that is regular, reliable and predictable to the precision we can observe, whatever the underlying process. It is a fact about regularity, not a metaphysical claim that nothing could have happened otherwise.

Two neighbouring ideas need to be kept apart from it. **Mathematical determinism** is a property of equations: given the state at one moment, the equations fix the state at every later moment. Many of the equations of physics are deterministic in this sense, including the fluid equations discussed below, even when what they describe is the aggregate of very many chancy events. A deterministic equation can therefore describe excellently a process that is not deterministic beneath it. And an **attractor** is a precisely defined idea in the mathematics of changing systems. It is a state, or set of states, toward which a system tends from a wide range of starting points and to which it returns when slightly disturbed. The word is easily used loosely for any outcome that turns up often. The stochastic calculator's researchers warned against exactly that. Their system reliably reached the right answer, but on examination this was explained by conservation and an ordinary survival calculation, not by any "well" drawing it in. Reliable outcomes are not, by themselves, evidence of an attractor.

## Same odds, different behaviour

That is the first half of the answer. The second half is where a relation-first world says something distinctive, and it begins with a simple puzzle.

Imagine two rooms, each with a hundred people tossing fair coins at the same moment, again and again. In the first room everyone tosses independently. In the second, people are paired, and each pair agrees in advance that whatever one gets, the partner will report the opposite. For any single person, in either room, the chance of heads is exactly one half. Nothing about the individual odds differs. Yet the rooms behave completely differently as wholes. In the first room the number of heads wanders from toss to toss, sometimes 44, sometimes 57. In the second room it is exactly fifty, every time.

The individual chances, what statisticians call the **marginal** probabilities, are identical. What differs is how the events are related to one another: the **joint process**. Two systems can assign exactly the same probability to every individual event and still behave quite differently as wholes, because in one the events are tied together and in the other they are not. In short:

```text
the same individual odds
  are not the same joint process,
  which is not the same pattern of co-variation,
  which is not the same behaviour of the whole
```

The stochastic calculator did something like the second room by design. Neutral pairs of relations, one positive and one negative, were inserted and removed at random. Taken separately, the counts of positive and negative relations varied considerably. Because they came and went in pairs, their variations cancelled exactly, and the total never moved. In statistical language, each count had a variance of about 77, and their covariance, the measure of how they varied together, was exactly −77. The net variation was zero.

## Coordination that matters

The book calls such relations among chance events **coordination**, and the word needs care in two directions.

First, coordination does not mean a controller standing over the events and arranging them. In the second room nobody directs the tosses. In the calculator nothing supervised the neutral pairs. Their coordination lay in the fact that each pair came and went as one. Coordination is a relation among events, not an agent above them.

Second, and more important, not just any correlation produces stability. Exploratory research by the project, carried out in conversational sessions and not yet published as a formal experiment record, studied chance events arranged in rings of locally coupled units. It reached conclusions worth stating with that caveat attached. Units with the same local chances could produce very different global behaviour depending on how their fluctuations were coupled. What stabilised the whole was not correlation as such, but coordination aligned with consequence. It mattered which quantities varied together, under which conditions, through which relational channels, and with what effect on what could happen next. Coordination that depends on the current state, tightening where a fluctuation would otherwise do harm, behaves differently from coordination applied uniformly.

The same work drew a distinction that is easy to miss. A system can be stable *on average* while its fluctuations are not. The average of the whole may settle down, and a simple law may describe it well, while the size and shape of the variations around that average remain unlike those of another system with the same average. Two systems can agree about their mean and disagree about everything else. Mean stability is not full stability.

The conclusion that matters for the book follows. A deterministic-looking law at the large scale does not require deterministic events at the small scale, or independent chances, or a single unique history underneath. But neither does it arise from "randomness plus a vague tendency to cohere". The *precise relational organisation* of the chance events is what makes the difference. That is a conclusion a relation-first ontology expects, and an object-first picture, in which each event belongs to an isolated thing, tends to overlook.

## Local rules and global order

The calculator drew one more distinction worth carrying forward. Its researchers ran its arithmetic in two ways. In one, any two opposite relations anywhere in the system could cancel. In the other, relations were scattered across separate regions arranged in a ring, and could cancel only when they met in the same region; between meetings they wandered to neighbouring regions. The local version still gave exactly the right answers every time, but it took far longer. With two regions it needed about thirty steps on average, much the same as the global version. With thirty-two regions it needed about 356, while the global version still needed about thirty. For multiplication and division, the calculator needed whole-system bookkeeping that the researchers could not reduce to purely local rules at all.

Local rules can preserve global correctness. They do not make it cheap, and they do not always suffice. When this book speaks of the regularity of created order as a whole, it does not claim that local relations alone account for it. It claims that the regularity is real, and that local relation, coordination and constraint are part of how it holds.

## What mathematics is doing

The regularities of the physical world are described by mathematics, often with astonishing success. In 1960 the physicist Eugene Wigner wrote of "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences": structures worked out by mathematicians for their own sake turn out, again and again, to describe nature with precision nobody expected. What is the mathematics doing?

Consider the equations that describe moving fluids, such as air over a wing or water in a pipe. They are called the Navier–Stokes equations, after two nineteenth-century scientists, and they are used every day to design aircraft and forecast weather. They treat a fluid as a smooth, continuous substance whose velocity and pressure vary from point to point. Yet a fluid is made of molecules in constant chaotic motion. The equations work because, at the scale where engineers use them, the combined behaviour of the molecules is regular enough to be described *as if* the fluid were smooth. Physicists can derive the fluid equations, approximately, from the statistical behaviour of molecules, through averaging over many particles, conservation of mass, momentum and energy, and rapid local settling toward equilibrium. At very small scales or in very thin gases, the smooth description fails and the molecular picture must take over. The equations even have puzzles of their own: whether they always have smooth solutions is one of the great unsolved problems of mathematics.

This shows two things. First, deterministic mathematics at one scale does not imply that reality is deterministic at every scale. Second, a smooth field description need not be the ontology of what it describes. Water is not made of the continuous field the equations describe, and the equations are still true at their scale.

The Relational Substrate project takes from this a research question rather than a result. The project has not derived the Navier–Stokes equations, or any law of physics, from the relational substrate. The question it asks is this: *what kinds of relational constraint and coordination preserve the right invariants, correlations and continuity for stable effective laws to emerge at all?* That is a real question, because, as the coin rooms showed, not every arrangement of chance produces stable laws. It is also an open one.

## Hidden structure beneath a description

There is a further caution, connected to Chapter 6. A description can map many different underlying states onto one visible picture. When the constraint laboratory re-analysed its small rule systems exactly, it found that one family label could hide more than a thousand distinct exact behaviours, and that two systems could share every summary it recorded and still differ in how unpredictable their movement was. Exploratory work by the project on a quite different mathematical structure, the geometry used in some modern calculations of particle interactions, led to the same conceptual lesson. Different underlying configurations can project to the same mathematical object, and the hidden differences can still shape what happens next.

So **mathematical equivalence under one description does not settle whether two things are the same.** A coarse description is often exactly right for its purpose. But whether what it leaves out is truly irrelevant depends on whether any reachable future process draws on it. That is the same principle Chapter 6 applied to identity, now applied to the mathematics of regularity.

The general lesson is the one Chapter 2 drew, now sharpened. **A mathematical structure that successfully describes behaviour is not, by that fact, the thing that reality fundamentally is.** Mathematics describes regularities: stable relations, invariants and the aggregate behaviour of coordinated processes. It describes them so well because they are genuinely there and the world is ordered and intelligible. That is a reason for great respect toward mathematics, not for suspicion. The respect is owed to what mathematics captures, without confusing the description with the described.

On the relational proposal, mathematics works because created order is structured by constraint, preserved by equivalence and invariance, and regular in aggregate through coordination. Those are precisely the things mathematics is best at describing. Scripture speaks of the heavens in exactly this register of ordered disclosure:

> `The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard.`
> *Psalm 19:1-3*

> `Knowest thou the ordinances of heaven? canst thou set the dominion thereof in the earth?`
> *Job 38:33*

> `For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead.`
> *Romans 1:20*

Creation is intelligible: it can be "understood by the things that are made". Its ordinances can be known, though not commanded. This is the ground of science, and it is why science can be trusted within its domain. Scripture does not tell us the Navier–Stokes equations. It tells us why a world in which such equations hold, and in which human minds can find them, is exactly what one should expect of a creation made through the Word.

## Credit where it is due

It is worth pausing to say what this book is *not* saying about modern science, because the distinction between description and ontology is often misused.

Physics is not clueless about gravity, light or magnetism. It describes them with a precision unmatched in any other field of human knowledge, and it predicts their behaviour in circumstances nobody had observed before. Electromagnetism, from Faraday's lines of force through Maxwell's equations to quantum electrodynamics, is among the greatest intellectual achievements of humanity, with predictions that in the best cases agree with experiment to about one part in a trillion. Nothing in this book competes with those descriptions. Where the Relational Substrate offers a different reading, it is a reading of *what is there*, alongside a description it fully accepts. The project's rule is equivalence with unification, not replacement: where established physics and the relational reading overlap, the physics stands.

What the book insists on is that the interpretive questions are genuinely open. What a field is, whether spacetime is a thing or a set of relations, whether quantum chance is fundamental, what a particle is between measurements: these remain open. The answers people give are not dictated by the equations. When an answer is presented as though it were, a metaphysical assumption has been given the authority of a measurement. The remedy is not to distrust measurement. It is to notice the assumption.

## What would count against these proposals

A proposal that cannot be wrong in any identifiable way tells us little. So it is worth stating what would count against the specific claims of Chapters 3 to 7, and where they are currently hard to test.

- **Contradicting precise measurement.** Any specific Relational Substrate mechanism that contradicted a precise measurement would fail, however coherent it looked. This has already happened. A model the project built in 2026 predicted the direction of an effect in metal fatigue but its size many times too small, and the project recorded it as a failure of magnitude rather than adjusting the model to fit.
- **Intrinsic properties at the base.** The relation-first proposal (Chapter 3) would be seriously weakened if physics came to show that its most fundamental entities have fully intrinsic properties, fixed independently of every interaction. The trend of the last century runs the other way, but the question is open.
- **Entanglement and mediation.** The proposal that influence is always mediated (Chapters 3 and 7) faces real pressure from quantum entanglement. Measurements on two distant entangled particles show correlations that, by Bell's theorem, no theory satisfying certain locality assumptions can reproduce, and experiments have confirmed those correlations. They cannot be used to send a signal, which is why relativity is not violated, and several interpretations, including relational ones, give accounts of them. Bell's theorem rules out a specific class of local explanations, not every conceivable notion of mediation. But entanglement remains the hardest test case for an account of mediated relation, and it is not a solved problem for this one.
- **The stochastic hypothesis is currently hard to test.** The standard interpretations of quantum theory agree on every measurement made so far, so present evidence cannot decide between a fundamentally chancy world and a deterministic one at that level. (Theories that modify quantum dynamics with spontaneous collapse do make slightly different predictions, which experiments are now testing.) That protects the hypothesis from easy refutation, and it is also a reason to hold it no more firmly than a coherent hypothesis.
- **The models do not single out RS.** The calculator and the constraint laboratory demonstrate possibilities in models. Their own authors concluded that ordinary computer science and probability account for every result. The laboratory's most recent generation also recorded a negative result: a pattern of "dissolution leading to new organisation" that the project hoped to find in its simplest systems did not appear (Appendix B). Results of this kind narrow what the ideas can claim. They show that the ideas are coherent and workable, not that nature uses them.

These limits do not empty the proposals of value. They locate it. The value lies in coherence: in whether a relation-first account of physical order, with constrained chance, relational identity and consequential transmission, helps the whole of what we know hang together better than its alternatives. That is the test to which the next chapter turns.

## From the stone to everything else

The physical arc of the book is now complete. The stone on the path is a stable configuration within a created order of relation. Its possible forms were bounded and weighted before any became actual. Its actual history unfolded by constrained chance, and that history is still partly present in it. It remains itself because its changes keep it within its relevant equivalence class. You see it because a distinction at its surface is made available to light, carried recoverably across changing carriers, and received by an eye able to be changed by it. And its behaviour, along with that of the light, is regular enough to be written in exact mathematics, because many chance events, constrained and coordinated, add up to a reliable whole.

So far this is an account of stones and light. The book's claim, however, is that it matters for far more than stones and light, and that a relation-first physical world changes how the rest of reality, including you, can be understood. Chapter 9 makes that case.
