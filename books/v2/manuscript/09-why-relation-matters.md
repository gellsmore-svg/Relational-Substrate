# Chapter 9: Why Relation Matters Beyond Physics

On Monday you promise a friend that you will help her move house on Saturday. On Saturday morning you would rather stay in bed. You get up and go.

It is an ordinary scene, as ordinary as the stone on the path, and like the stone it rests on a great deal. For the promise to be what it seems, several things must be true. The person who gets up on Saturday must be the same person who spoke on Monday, though much in you has changed in between. Your words on Monday must have gone out from you and become something in her: an expectation, a plan, a reliance she has built on. What you do on Saturday must have consequences for her, and through her for others. And there must be something about the promise that *binds* you. It is not merely that you predicted you would go, but that you *owe* it. If you had stayed in bed, she would have been wronged, not merely surprised.

Identity through time, a distinction carried from one person to another and received as a change in her, consequence, obligation, belonging: these are among the most basic features of human life. This chapter asks how they sit with what the physical world fundamentally is. That is the question the earlier chapters have been preparing, and it is the hinge of the book.

## The tests the answer must pass

Chapter 2 distinguished local, regional and global coherence. It also argued that global coherence asks more than the absence of contradiction. The domains of an account must be genuinely connected. Ideas must cross from one domain to another without silently changing kind. Claims must keep their meaning through the layers. A claim taken round a cycle of domains must return the same. And the real distinctions must survive all of this.

The promise is a good place to apply those tests, because it crosses every boundary the book cares about. It involves a body that walks to a friend's house: physics and biology. It involves a person who remembers and intends: personal life. It involves an obligation: moral order. And for a Christian it involves faithfulness before God. A picture of the physical world must not only explain falling stones. It must allow the transitions from stone to body to person to promise to be intelligible, without contradiction and without reducing any of them to another.

## The serious alternatives

It would be easy to set up the question as a contest between the relational picture and a crude alternative: a world of hard, self-contained lumps, each with properties entirely its own, that only bump into one another. Some philosophers have held views close to that. A well-known modern version holds that the world is a vast mosaic of local, intrinsic facts, with everything else, including causes and laws, supervening on how those facts happen to be arranged. That view deserves a hearing. But it is not the strongest alternative, and an argument aimed only at it would prove little.

The serious alternatives already contain a great deal of relation.

- **Powers ontologies** hold that the basic properties of things are dispositions to act on, and be acted on by, other things. A power is already directed beyond its bearer.
- **Field ontologies,** which many physicists hold, treat fields spread through all of space as the basic physical reality, so that particles are disturbances in something continuous and everywhere interacting.
- **Hylomorphic accounts,** in the tradition of Aristotle and Aquinas, hold that things are constituted by form, and that form is intrinsically ordered toward activity and toward other things.
- **Process philosophies** take events and their connections to be more basic than enduring substances.
- **Structural realism** holds that what our best theories most securely describe is structure, the pattern of relations.

These are serious positions held by serious people. Several of them converge, in important ways, with the view this book defends. The question is therefore not whether the alternatives can make room for relation. Many of them can. It is *where* relation enters, and what that costs.

## Where relation enters

Here is the difference that matters. In the relational picture this book proposes, relation is *constitutive* at the base. What a physical thing is cannot be specified apart from how it stands to others. Chapter 6 even defined the identity of a thing by what could distinguish it in relation to possible future interactions. The contrast that matters is with ontologies in which the fundamental entities are individuated wholly or substantially independently of constitutive relation, so that what a thing is can be fully specified before its relations are. In such ontologies relation is something already-complete bearers *have*, *exercise* or *enter into*. It is not what they *are*. Not every substance ontology is like this. A sophisticated powers theorist may hold that a thing's essential powers are other-directed from the start, and a hylomorphist may hold that form and its ordered capacities are constitutive from the beginning. Those views already share part of what this book defends, and the argument below is not aimed at them.

That difference may look slight in physics, where both kinds of ontology describe the falling stone equally well. It becomes significant when the account must cross into human life, because human life is pervaded by relation that is not merely exercised but constitutive. You are, in part, *who you are* through your parents, your promises, your friendships, your history with others and your standing before God. A person is not a self-contained individual who then enters relationships, as a billiard ball enters collisions. Personal identity is relational in a much stronger sense.

So an ontology whose fundamental entities are individuated independently of constitutive relation must, at some point on the way from stones to persons, introduce constitutive relation as something new: a kind of reality that its base does not contain in any form. That is not impossible. But it is a discontinuity. It adds a bridge assumption that has to be explained, or accepted without explanation, somewhere between physics and personhood.

A relation-first ontology does not need that bridge. Its base already has the *form* of what appears, transformed and deepened, in human life:

- constitutive relation (Chapter 3);
- identity as relational equivalence carrying its own history (Chapter 6);
- distinctions made available, carried and received as consequence (Chapter 7);
- wholes stabilised by the coordination of their parts (Chapter 8).

When relation becomes personal and moral, it does not appear as a stranger from a different reality. It appears in a world whose grain it already follows.

## The argument, stated plainly

The central argument of the book can now be stated in steps:

1. Human life, as we live it and as Scripture describes it, is pervaded by constitutive relation, identity through change, consequence that passes from one person to another, and responsibility.
2. A physical ontology whose fundamental entities are individuated independently of constitutive relation must introduce constitutive relationality for the first time at some higher level, as a new kind of reality.
3. A physical ontology in which relation is constitutive at the base already contains the form of these features, so that living, personal and moral relation are continuous in form with the physical order they arise in, while remaining distinct in kind.
4. Other things being equal, an account with fewer unexplained discontinuities and fewer ad hoc bridge assumptions between its domains is more globally coherent, by the tests of connection and faithful translation.
5. Therefore, *if* a relation-first picture of physical order is consistent with everything we observe, it is to be preferred on grounds of global coherence.

The argument does not claim that other ontologies make promises impossible or responsibility unintelligible. That would be stronger than the book needs, and probably false. It claims something more modest and more defensible: relation-first ontology offers a more *economical continuity of form* across physical, living and personal reality, provided the boundaries between categories stay intact. The conditional in step 5 matters. That is why Chapters 3 to 8 took such care over what physics does and does not say.

If the argument is sound, it shows why the physics of this book is not an optional technical appendix to a theological book. *How* the material world is real bears on whether the rest of reality hangs together.

## What the relational picture does not provide

It is as important to say what the argument does not claim, because it is easily inflated.

The common relational grounding of material things is a kind of glue. It allows physical causation, biological organisation, embodied action, consequence in the environment, communication and social dependence to belong to one created order. But it is continuity across the boundaries, not reduction across them:

- Moral obligation is not a field, and a promise is not a force.
- Love is not covariance.
- Sin is not entropy.
- Hearing and receiving the word of God is not receptor chemistry.
- The soul is not a carrier.
- Resurrection is not the substrate repairing itself.
- Divine action is not a wider set of physical permissions.

The relational picture does not tell you that you ought to keep your promise. Chapter 12 will distinguish what is physically possible from what is morally right, and insist that the first never settles the second. What the relational picture does is remove the strangeness of a world in which promises could bind at all.

## The strongest objection: emergence

The most serious reply runs like this. Nobody claims that promises exist among particles. They *emerge*. Complex arrangements display properties their parts lack: wetness emerges from molecules that are not wet, and life from chemistry that is not alive. Why should constitutive relation not emerge in the same way, from a base without it?

The book accepts emergence fully. Chapter 10 will argue that living organisation is genuinely new and genuinely explanatory at its own level. The disagreement is narrower. In familiar cases, what emerges is a new and higher expression of capacities the base already has. Wetness expresses the attraction molecules already have for one another. Life expresses chemistry that already includes catalysis, templating and self-assembly. The question is not whether new properties appear. It is whether the base already contains the *relational resources* that make the emergent structures intelligible.

If the base contains entities individuated independently of their relations, whose relations are always something added or exercised, then constitutive relation emerges from a base where nothing is constituted by its relations. That can be asserted, but it is precisely the kind of discontinuity step 2 names. A powers ontology narrows the gap, because powers are already other-directed, and the book counts that as convergence. It does not count it as refutation. The relational picture's claim is a claim of *continuity of structural capacity*: what emerges in living, personal and moral life is new in kind, and it emerges from a base that already has its form. It is not a claim to derive moral truth from physics. That is a more coherent position, though not one that can be proved.

## A second case: the poisoned river

A factory upstream discharges waste into a river. Downstream, fish die, and the people of a village who drink the water fall ill. Each link is physical: chemicals are released, carried in the water, and received as changes in the bodies of fish and people, a chain of transmissions crossing from chemistry to biology to human life. But the case is also moral. Someone is responsible, and the villagers have been wronged.

Apply the round-trip test. Begin with the physical chain, translate it into the moral question of responsibility, then into the action required (stop the discharge, compensate the village), and back into the physical world (cleaner water). In a relational creation, every step of that cycle concerns the same world. Responsibility answers to *real* consequences flowing through a *real* order of relation, and the remedy flows back through the same order. The moral layer is distinct, since responsibility is not a chemical fact. But it is not a separate story attached from outside.

Scripture speaks of exactly this reach, in which human wrongdoing is received by the land itself:

> `Hear the word of the LORD, ye children of Israel: for the LORD hath a controversy with the inhabitants of the land, because there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land. By swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing, and committing adultery, they break out, and blood toucheth blood. Therefore shall the land mourn, and every one that dwelleth therein shall languish, with the beasts of the field, and with the fowls of heaven; yea, the fishes of the sea also shall be taken away.`
> *Hosea 4:1-3*

> `For the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation of the sons of God. For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope… For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now.`
> *Romans 8:19-20, 22*

Hosea's land mourns because of the people's lying and killing. Paul's whole creation groans. Scripture consistently presents human moral life and the created world as bound together, so that wrong done by persons is received in the land, and the redemption of persons concerns the whole creation. The book does not claim that a relational physics *explains* these texts. Their explanation is theological. The claim is that a relational account of the material world sits naturally with the world they describe, while an account of sealed and indifferent objects makes them sound like poetry about a reality that is not really like that.

## Why this is a Christian question

This argument is not a covert attempt to derive Christianity from physics. A reader who accepted every word of it would not thereby believe in God. But it bears on Christian faith in a particular way.

Scripture describes a world in which relation is fundamental at every level. God makes humanity in his image and declares that it is not good for the man to be alone (Genesis 2:18). He binds himself to his people by covenant. He calls them to love, to keep faith and to bear one another's burdens. In Christ he reconciles "all things unto himself… whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven" (Colossians 1:20). If the physical world were, at bottom, a world in which nothing is constituted by its relations, this scriptural world would rest on a creation of a different kind, and the transition would need explaining. If the physical world is relational from its foundation, created by the God in whom "all things consist", then the relational life Scripture describes is the fulfilment, at its own level, of the grain of the whole creation. That does not prove the gospel. It removes one reason for thinking the gospel must be about a world other than the one we physically inhabit.

## Coherence is not the same as truth

A book that argues for coherence must face its limits here.

A coherent account can be wrong. A detective story can be perfectly coherent and accuse the wrong suspect. More subtly, an argument can become *more* coherent as it proceeds while moving *further* from the truth, if every step builds on an early mistake nobody questions. The research behind this project met this directly, though in exploratory work rather than in formally recorded experiments. While developing a method of reasoning that runs several independent lines of thought on one problem, the researchers observed that when the lines were repeatedly merged into a shared summary and fed back, agreement rose steadily, and an error present in one early line could harden into a confident consensus. They called this a *false attractor*. The lesson, which this book adopts, is that coherence is not the same as fidelity to the truth. Agreement can be produced by a shared hidden premise as easily as by a shared grasp of reality.

That applies to this book. Its argument is coherent, but that does not make it true, and its own coherence could be a false attractor. The safeguards are those already described. The argument stays accountable to observation wherever observation can reach (Chapter 8 named where it cannot). It keeps its levels of claim distinct. It keeps alternatives in view (Appendix A). And it submits its conclusions to Scripture, which is not one more input to the coherence but the frame within which coherence is sought.

There is also a reason this matters for global coherence in particular. Global coherence is the most demanding test there is, because it must hold across every domain at once, and because errors hidden in one domain tend to surface as drift on the round trip through the others. A false attractor in one place is likely, sooner or later, to produce a contradiction or a silent change of meaning somewhere else. That is exactly why global coherence is a stronger test than local success, and why the book is willing to be judged by it.

## Relation across categories

![Figure 5. Relation across categories. Material, living, personal, moral and spiritual realities are distinct kinds, not layers built one out of another; relation runs through each in its own way; God is not a category within creation but its Creator, who upholds all things.](v2/figures/fig-categories.png)

The chapter can now complete what Chapter 1 began. Creation contains distinct categories of reality, and relation appears in each of them differently.

- In the **physical** world, relation is constraint, equivalence, coordination and the carriage of distinctions (Chapters 3 to 8).
- In the **living** world, it becomes metabolism, signalling, templating and repair (Chapter 10).
- In the **personal** world, it becomes address and response, knowledge and love, promise and trust (Chapter 11).
- In the **moral** world, it becomes obligation, rightness and wrong (Chapter 12).
- In the **spiritual** world, it becomes communion with God.
- God's relation to creation is that of Creator to creature. It belongs to no category within creation, and every created relation depends on it.

The same word, *relation*, is used across all these categories, and it is used truly. What runs through them is a continuity of form, not a single nature. The remaining chapters take the categories one at a time, beginning with life, and show how each stands within a relational creation without being swallowed by it.
