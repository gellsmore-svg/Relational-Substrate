# Chapter 16: One World, Many Kinds

The stone is still on the path. Nothing about it has changed since the first page. What has changed, the book hopes, is how much can be seen in it.

It is a creature, made by God and held by him, not divine and not self-explaining. On the proposal this book has developed, it is a stable form within a created order of relation: real and distinct, but constituted within relation rather than sealed off from it. Its possible forms were bounded and weighted before any became actual, and those bounds were shaped by purpose, though the stone itself intends nothing. Its actual history unfolded by constrained chance. It persists because the changes it undergoes preserve what it is. It comes to you because a difference at its surface constrains the light, is preserved by a chain of dependency, and becomes a consequence in you. Its behaviour, and the light's, is regular enough to be written in exact mathematics, because countless open events, constrained and coordinated, add up to a reliable whole.

And you, who see it, are formed from the same dust, alive by the same gift that gives life to the woodlouse beneath it, and yet a person: known and called by God, answerable for what you do, capable of keeping or breaking a promise, wounded by corruption and offered restoration. You belong to the same world as the stone, and you are not the same kind of thing.

## The shape of the whole

Gathered together, the book's account runs like this.

God creates reality. Created physical order is real, and it is not self-grounding: it holds together because it is held. The deepest physical ontology the Relational Substrate proposes is relational rather than object-first. Actualisation is conditioned before anything happens, both by what is forbidden and by what is favoured. The book calls those conditions T0 and reads them as constrained possibility, while leaving open what kind of thing they finally are. What has already happened shapes what is currently reachable. Actualisation is, on the book's working hypothesis, fundamentally stochastic: consistent with quantum theory, though not established by it. Persistence, identity and regularity arise through structured relational continuity. A thing remains the same *kind* of thing by preserving its relevant equivalence class, judged by what reachable futures could tell apart, with invariants as its certificates and provenance as part of it where it still matters. Whether it is the same *individual* is a further question, of continuity and lineage, which remains open. Regularity is the stable behaviour of many chance events, constrained and coordinated in the right way. Transmit, carry and receive name the grammar by which any distinction becomes consequential to another state: a distinction capable of constraining beyond itself, a dependency that preserves it, and a consequence that depends on it. The book proposes that this is the form of consequential relation itself, and that cause, in a chancy world, is a distinction modifying what another state can become. Stable global behaviour does not require every underlying transition to be determined in advance. Mathematics describes that stable behaviour, often with astonishing precision, without thereby becoming the reality it describes. Material things are one achievement of this order: configurations that have acquired persistent, individuated identity that can be handled, as distinct from material organisation, such as a sound, that passes through them. Whether materiality itself emerges from an order that is not material is the book's central question, held as a hypothesis. Organised identities persist across changes of carrier when the relations that constitute them are preserved and a compatible receiver recovers them. And identities gather recursively, from distinction through relationship and organisation to higher-order wholes that act within the next order.

Because material reality is relational from its foundation, the physical world does not begin as a scatter of metaphysically isolated objects that must somehow acquire relation later. That matters for global coherence. Life, personhood, morality, consequence, corruption and restoration need not be bolted onto a physical world of a different kind. Yet material, biological, personal and spiritual categories remain genuinely distinct. Relation runs through all of them, and relation across categories does not erase category. The substrate is not the soul. Physical transmission is not divine speech. A law of physics is not a commandment. Creation is not God.

Observation, measurement and successful mathematics strongly constrain what can responsibly be claimed about the physical world. Empirical description alone does not exhaust ontology, and the metaphysical pictures that travel with it are proposals to be tested like any other. Scripture provides the governing frame within which this book seeks coherence. The whole project is an attempt to construct the most globally coherent account available, while remaining honest about observation, mathematics, experiment, metaphysics, uncertainty and revelation.

## What kind of confidence this deserves

The account contains claims of very different strength, and the reader is owed a final sorting of them.

Some of it the book asserts because Scripture asserts it: creation, the Creator–creature distinction, the reality of persons, corruption and restoration, the resurrection. These are held as commitments of faith, received rather than derived, and the book has tried not to make Scripture say more than it says.

Some of it is observation, measurement and well-tested mathematics. Physics describes the world with extraordinary precision. Quantum predictions are probabilistic. Deterministic large-scale laws can be the stable behaviour of countless chancy small-scale events. None of this is the book's own, and none of it is in dispute here. What the book does dispute is that any one interpretation of these findings, including the familiar picture of independent objects in a container of space, inherits their authority.

Some of it is philosophical argument. Description does not by itself settle ontology, and a successful description of consequences does not fix the nature of their cause. Every account starts from assumptions, and none may stretch its entities to fit whatever turns up. Plausibility can be compared rigorously without being turned into numbers. Structural analogy is not identity. And, most importantly, global coherence is a stronger test of a worldview than local success. These the book has argued for, and they stand or fall with the arguments.

And some of it is the Relational Substrate proposal itself: that relation is foundational, that actualisation is constrained chance, that relational equivalence can preserve sameness of kind through change (individual continuity being a further question of lineage), that T-C-R names something basic about how created order is connected, that materiality is emergent, and that identities are organised, carried and cohered through a recursive grammar of emergence. These are hypotheses. The project's models have shown them to be coherent and workable, and they do not show that nature works this way. The models have also marked limits: the simplest relational rules give no account of gravity's effect on clocks (Chapter 9). The book's case for them is the case of Chapter 10: that together they make the whole of what we know hang together better than the alternative, with no gulf between the physical world and the human one.

That combination calls for what the first edition called *disciplined confidence*: confidence where the grounds are strong, openness where they are not, and honesty about which is which. Appendix E records the current status of every major claim in one place, together with the questions that remain open and the readings the book rejects.

## The limits of creaturely knowing

There is a deeper reason for that discipline, and Scripture gives it.

> `Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.`
> *Job 38:4*

> `For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known.`
> *1 Corinthians 13:12*

Human beings know from inside creation. They cannot step outside it and see its foundations from the vantage point of its Maker. Whatever the deepest order of the world is, we meet it through its effects, through instruments and inference, through a glass. Like the caller of a private function in Chapter 2, we can learn its behaviour well without its inner nature being fixed by what we learn. That is not a defect to be overcome by enough research. It is part of being a creature. It is also why the warrant this book claims for its ontology is that of a coherence argument, held with disciplined confidence, and why Scripture, which comes to us from the One who laid the foundations, is the frame within which the argument is made.

But Job's question is not a rebuke to inquiry. The same book that asks "where wast thou?" catalogues the wonders of creation in loving detail, and the God who asks it invites Job to look. "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter" (Proverbs 25:2). Partial knowledge is still knowledge. To search out the order of creation, while knowing that we see in part, is one of the honours God gives to creatures made in his image.

Inquiry of this kind is a way, and Scripture's deepest word for it is Christ's own:

> `Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me.`
> *John 14:6*

The author reads the three words together: the way as a road actually walked, the truth as what grounds it, the life as participation in it, with the Father as the destination, who is one with the Son ("I and my Father are one", John 10:30). That is a theological reading, offered as one. It fits what this book has described: partial understanding refined, step by step, toward a coherence that only God knows perfectly. Truth is what is so. The way toward it is walked by creatures, and it is not the same as the truth it seeks.

The fitting response to such a world is **wonder**: the response of a creature to coherence that is intelligible and yet exceeds what it can now comprehend. Wonder does not end inquiry. It invites it. On the author's account, creation was made to be read this way. Its order represents something of the spiritual order that is prior to it (Chapter 1). And its beauty, the apprehensible expression of the patterns of goodness, which is spiritual in origin, can be met in created things without being exhausted by them, even where what is beautiful is not perfect in every part.

## Living in a relational world

If the world is as described here, some practical consequences follow. They are not deductions from physics, which would contradict Chapter 13, but ways of living that fit the grain of the world.

Science is to be honoured. It studies a real, ordered, intelligible creation, and its successes are possible precisely because creation is not chaos. It is also to be held in its place: it describes how the created order behaves, and the questions of what that order is, what it is for and how we should live in it are not answered by description alone.

Consequence is to be taken seriously. In a relational world, what we say and do goes out from us, is carried, and is received in others and in the creation around us. Words, promises, neglect and care all travel. Responsibility is not an arbitrary overlay. It answers to real effects flowing through a real order.

Wisdom is more than technique. A civilisation can learn to calculate brilliantly while forgetting what its calculations are about, and to build powerfully while forgetting what its buildings are for.

> `Through wisdom is an house builded; and by understanding it is established: And by knowledge shall the chambers be filled with all precious and pleasant riches.`
> *Proverbs 24:3-4*

Cleverness can build a tower against the heavens. Wisdom asks first what is being built and why.

And creation is to be cared for, because it is not a stage set for human drama but a creation that groans with us and waits with us for its deliverance. The land mourns when its people lie and kill (Hosea 4:3). It will rejoice when its Lord comes (Psalm 96:12-13).

## The end of the matter

A book about ontology can end in abstraction, and this one should not. The whole account, with all its distinctions and cautions, points beyond itself: to the One who made the stone and the light and the eye and the person who sees, who holds all things together, who has not abandoned what he made, and who has promised to make all things new.

> `Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil.`
> *Ecclesiastes 12:13-14*

The framework ends there, not because it is unimportant, but because that is where everything it describes finally rests.
