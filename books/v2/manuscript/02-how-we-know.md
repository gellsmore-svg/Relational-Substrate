# Chapter 2: How We Come to Know What Is There

Suppose you drop the stone from the path. It falls. Time the fall and measure the height, and you can calculate how fast it struck the ground. With Newton's law you can calculate the orbit of the Moon, and with Einstein's general relativity the corrections that keep satellite navigation accurate to within metres. Physics knows how gravity behaves with extraordinary precision.

Now ask a different question. What *is* gravity? Is it a force reaching across space, as Newton's equations seem to say? Is it the curvature of a spacetime that exists as a kind of thing in its own right? Or is spacetime itself a set of relations between events, with no existence apart from them? Physicists and philosophers who accept every one of those measurements still disagree about these questions, and nobody yet knows how gravity fits with quantum theory. The disagreement is not about whether the stone falls, or how fast. It is about what kind of reality the equations describe.

That gap, between describing how something behaves and knowing what it is, is the subject of this chapter. Seeing it clearly is the key to everything this book attempts.

## Six layers of knowing

When we say we "know" something about the stone, we can mean several different things, and they must be kept apart.

1. We have **observed** it: seen it, touched it, noticed that it falls.
2. We have **measured** it, attaching numbers by a defined procedure: its mass, its temperature, the time of its fall.
3. We have found a **mathematical relation** that predicts the measurements, such as distance fallen growing with the square of the time.
4. We have a **physical model** that organises the behaviour, such as Earth's mass pulling on the stone's mass, or the stone following a straight path through curved spacetime.
5. We hold an **ontological interpretation** of the model: what must actually be there for the behaviour to happen at all.
6. We live within a **worldview** in which that interpretation feels natural.

Each layer depends on the ones before it, but the later layers are not guaranteed by the earlier ones. An equation can predict perfectly while different pictures of the mechanism remain possible. A mechanism can be useful while the question of what fundamentally exists remains open. The history of physics shows this repeatedly. Newton's law of gravitation predicted superbly for more than two centuries, yet the picture of a force acting instantly across empty space, which Newton himself found troubling, was eventually replaced by a very different picture in general relativity. The *predictions* were largely preserved, and many of them still underpin practical calculation today. The *account of what is there* changed profoundly.

Quantum theory makes the point sharper. Its mathematical machinery is among the most precisely confirmed in science. Yet the same mathematics is read in strikingly different ways. On some readings the world is fundamentally a matter of chance at the smallest scales. On others it is entirely deterministic, with hidden variables or branching worlds. These readings disagree about what reality is while agreeing about every measurement made so far. (A further family of theories modifies the quantum dynamics itself and predicts slight differences, which experiments are now testing.) A successful description can be joined to more than one ontology.

This is simply what descriptions are. A description tells us how things behave. An ontology asks what kind of thing has to be there for that behaviour to be possible. Confusing the layers causes trouble in both directions. When descriptive success is treated as though it had settled ontology, metaphysical assumptions enter unexamined. When the gap is used as an excuse to ignore description, the result is fantasy.

## What the outside of a thing can tell you

Computing offers an exact picture of the gap. Programmers often work with a *private function*: code they can call but cannot read. They supply an input and receive an output. By testing systematically they can learn a great deal: which outputs follow which inputs, which conditions change the behaviour, what the function refuses to do, and what stays the same whatever they try. That is real knowledge, and reliable software is built on it. What they cannot learn this way is how the function is written. Quite different code can give the same output for every input anyone has tried, and the function may do things nobody has yet asked of it.

So knowledge of **consequential behaviour**, meaning what something does and under what conditions, is genuine knowledge. It is not knowledge of **intrinsic ontology**, meaning what that something is, and the second does not follow automatically from the first. Mathematics has a precise case. In 1966 Mark Kac asked whether one can "hear the shape of a drum": whether the notes an ideal drumskin can sound fix its shape. Some things can be heard; the notes reveal the drum's area, for example. But in 1992 Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert produced two differently shaped drums that sound exactly the same notes. The sound tells you much about the drum, and not everything.

Observability and understanding answer different questions. Something may be observed without its mechanism being understood, or understood relationally without a complete mechanism. Four respects in which a question may stand need to be kept apart. **First, mechanistic explanation:** we can account for how specified relations and processes produce an outcome. **Second, relational understanding without a complete mechanism:** we may genuinely understand what trust is, or why a promise binds, without possessing an exhaustive account of how that reality operates. **Third, present investigative limits:** some questions lie beyond our available instruments, evidence or methods, yet may become accessible. **Fourth, possible limits of exhaustive creaturely comprehension:** some matters may never be comprehended in full by a creature, although genuine partial understanding remains possible. These are respects in which we approach questions, not four kinds of reality or permanent boxes.

Observability cuts across these respects. Atoms were inferred for generations before they could be imaged, and single atoms can now be seen and moved one at a time. Other things may be unobservable in principle: quantum theory limits what can be learned from a single unknown quantum system. Yet unobservability does not forbid inference from consequences. Two errors are to be avoided. One declares something permanently inaccessible without reason. The other assumes that every underlying reality will eventually be observed as one more object, and that assumption is itself a metaphysical claim. Creaturely knowledge, as Scripture describes it, is genuine and partial at once (Romans 1:20; 1 Corinthians 13:12), and Chapter 16 returns to that. The principle is this: **a successful description of observable consequences does not, by itself, establish the ultimate ontological nature of their cause.**

## Every account starts somewhere

If description does not settle ontology by itself, then every ontology contains something more than measurement. It contains assumptions about what kind of world could produce the measurements. That is true of materialist accounts, of religious accounts, and of the account in this book.

Here it helps to distinguish two things often run together. **Methodological naturalism** is a working rule of scientific practice: in the laboratory, explain natural phenomena by natural causes and test the explanations against observation. It is a sensible rule, and Christians practise it every day in chemistry, geology and medicine. **Metaphysical naturalism** is a claim about all of reality: nothing exists except the natural world that science studies. The second does not follow from the first. A method that looks only for natural causes cannot discover that nothing else exists, any more than a metal detector can discover that there is no wood. When the method is treated as though it had proved the metaphysics, an assumption has entered without being named.

The same discipline applies to theology. Quoting Scripture does not make an argument rigorous if the step from the text to the conclusion is left unexplained. This book begins from Scripture openly. It also tries to show, at each important point, whether a claim is something Scripture teaches, something inferred from what Scripture teaches, or a proposal that Scripture permits but does not state. Metaphysical honesty means making these assumptions and inferences visible, so a reader can see where they are and decide what to make of them.

## Explanations that bend to fit

Assumptions go wrong in one characteristic way, and it needs a name. Imagine someone who explains every odd event in a house, a creaking stair, a lost sock, a flickering light, by an invisible resident with no other specified properties. Each explanation is logically possible, and none can be refuted, because whenever the evidence changes the resident acquires whatever power the new evidence requires. Nothing it could meet would count against it, and so it explains nothing.

The book calls this **metaphysical elasticity**: assigning additional, insufficiently justified properties to an explanatory entity whenever they are needed to preserve an interpretation, without acknowledging the added assumptions or the evidence they would require. The author's informal name for it is the *Harry Potter problem*: in a world of spells, an unseen cause can be produced for anything.

Elasticity is not confined to ghosts. It occurs when a successful mathematical description is taken as evidence that its mathematical entities are fundamental substances, without that identification being argued separately. It occurs when a word is widened to rescue a thesis. If "matter" is stretched to cover whatever physics finds, then materialism can never be wrong, and it has stopped saying anything. And it could occur in this book. The relational substrate would become exactly such an invisible resident if it gained a new power for every new problem. The book's defence is to fix its commitments in advance (Appendix E), to say what would count against them (Chapter 9), and to price every addition. The rule binds the Relational Substrate as strictly as its rivals.

## The status of each claim

Because of that, the book keeps track of what kind of claim it is making. It does not label every sentence, which would make it unreadable, but it uses one small, fixed vocabulary of status throughout:

| Status | Example from this book |
| --- | --- |
| Scriptural or theological constraint | God created the heaven and the earth. The substrate, if it exists, is a creature (an inference from Scripture, marked as one). |
| Empirical observation | A dropped stone falls. |
| Mathematical or physical constraint | Distance fallen grows with the square of the time. General relativity's predictions are precisely confirmed. |
| Ontological inference | Description does not by itself settle ontology. |
| Relational Substrate working hypothesis | Physical things are constituted within relations. |
| Mechanism candidate | A specific proposed structure for matter. The book names such candidates and does not rely on them. |
| Open question | How consciousness relates to physical order. Whether quantum events are fundamentally chancy. |
| Rejected | That a coherent account is thereby true. |

Two further labels say where a claim came from, not how firmly it is held. A **model result** happened in one of the project's computer models, built on stated assumptions. A model can show that an idea is possible, coherent and capable of doing work. It cannot show that nature works that way. An **exploratory** result comes from research sessions not yet published as formal records. Appendix E gives the status of every major claim in the book.

## Local, regional and global coherence

How, then, does one judge an ontology, if no measurement can display it directly? The answer this book gives is **coherence**. Freedom from contradiction is necessary but not enough: an elaborate story can avoid every contradiction while remaining a set of tidy compartments, none really connected to the others. Coherence in this book's sense also asks whether the parts are connected, and whether real distinctions survive the connecting. It comes in three degrees of scope.

**Local coherence** is achieved when a model explains a bounded phenomenon internally. An equation fits the measurements of falling stones. A theory accounts for the colours of a particular kind of glass. A model reproduces a pattern in one regime. Much of science operates, quite properly, at this level.

**Regional coherence** is achieved when several neighbouring domains integrate. Chemistry turns out to be grounded in the physics of atoms. Molecular biology joins chemistry to the life of the cell. The physiology of the nerve joins the cell to the behaviour of the organism. Regional coherence is harder to win than local coherence, and more valuable, because each domain now checks the others.

**Global coherence** is achieved when the whole account of reality remains mutually compatible. That means physical observation and mathematics, the material and the living, consciousness and personhood, morality and meaning, history and lived experience, and, for a Christian account, theology and Scripture: creation, corruption, redemption and restoration. A globally coherent worldview does not need one set of assumptions for the laboratory, another for the courtroom, another for the hospital bedside and another for prayer.

What makes global coherence demanding is not only that nothing may contradict anything else. Several further tests matter, and they can be stated in ordinary terms.

- **Real connection.** The domains must be joined by intelligible transitions, not merely placed side by side. It is not enough that an account says true things about physics and true things about persons. It must be possible to see how the one bears on the other.
- **Valid interfaces.** Where one kind of reality meets another, as when a physical consequence becomes a biological signal or a capacity to act becomes a moral responsibility, the meeting must be explained, not assumed. An idea crossing a boundary must keep what is relevant and must not quietly turn into something of a different kind.
- **Meaning kept through the layers.** A claim carried through several explanatory levels should still be recognisably itself at the end. If a word like "identity" or "cause" changes meaning a little at each step, the account can sound plausible paragraph by paragraph and still fail as a whole.
- **Stability under repair.** Solving a problem in one domain must not break what already held together in another.
- **The round trip.** Take a claim from one domain through others and back again, for example from bodily identity to the identity of an organism to the identity of a person and back to the body. It should return substantially the same. Hidden drift shows up most clearly on the way home.
- **Accountable kinds of knowing.** Observation, measurement, mathematics, philosophical inference, personal encounter and revelation are different kinds of knowing. Each contributes to inference in its own way. They must be kept distinct, and none may silently overrule the others.

And through all of these, the real distinctions must survive: Creator and creature, the material and the spiritual, description and ontology, probability and arbitrariness, analogy and identity, what is possible and what is right, a person and a configuration. Connection is not reduction. An account that achieves smooth connection by erasing these is not more coherent. It has simply lost something real.

The book's central methodological thesis can now be stated. **Local explanatory success is not sufficient evidence that an ontology is true if that ontology produces contradiction elsewhere.** An account of physical reality may explain falling stones perfectly. If it also makes promises, responsibility or love unintelligible, so that they must be explained away as illusions, then it has a global problem that its local success cannot cure. Conversely, an account that coheres across all these domains, while respecting every robust observation, has a strong claim on our attention even if its deepest commitments cannot be measured directly.

This is a philosophical thesis, and Chapter 10 argues for it. Most working scientists are rightly occupied with local and regional coherence. Global coherence asks what their successes jointly require.

## Coherence runs both ways

The test applies in both directions. The Relational Substrate must not contradict what has been observed and measured, or what successful mathematics predicts. Any proposal that does is finished, however elegant it is.

But the picture of reality that commonly travels with physics is also a proposal. Space is a container. The world is a collection of independent objects. Fields and particles are the furniture of reality. Laws are rules things obey. These belong to layers four to six above, not to layers one to three. They are often well motivated, and some may be right. They gain no evidential weight from being familiar, mainstream, widely published or taught in every school. Consensus is a fact about people, not a measurement of the world. So the conventional metaphysics of physics is held to the same standard of global coherence as the proposal of this book, and it receives no exemption for being physics. That is a refusal to confuse what was measured with a picture of what was measured.

## Comparing without numbers

Judging between accounts needs one more tool. It corrects a common mistake, the assumption that unless a probability can be calculated, talk of likelihood is mere opinion. Three judgments are involved.

1. **Logical possibility.** An account contains no identified contradiction.
2. **Qualitative plausibility.** Given the relevant observations, constraints, assumptions and alternatives, one account is more or less plausible than another.
3. **Quantitative probability.** A number is assigned under a specified mathematical model.

The absence of the third does not invalidate the second. Suppose you must pass your hand along an electric fence twice, once a centimetre away and once a metre away, in comparable circumstances where a slip is possible. Contact is more likely on the first pass. Nobody needs a model of your breakfast, your sleep or every possible distraction to know this. Those things bear on both passes alike, and only the distance differs, so the ordering holds across every reasonable variation of them. What must stay explicit are the conditions it depends on: that the circumstances are comparable, and that contact is possible at all. **A justified qualitative ordering of likelihoods does not require an exhaustive quantitative model of reality.** Mathematicians have studied such orderings directly. Their theory of comparative probability shows that "more probable than" can be reasoned about rigorously without numbers, and that some perfectly consistent orderings cannot be turned into numbers at all.

So the book sorts its comparisons. A **robust** comparison survives every reasonable variation of its assumptions, as the fence comparison does. A **conditional** one holds only under assumptions that must be stated. Sometimes **no defensible ordering** is available, and then none is claimed. The converse mistake matters too: logical possibility carries no number, and an account is not improbable merely because it is complicated. Accounts are compared by what each assumes and what each leaves unexplained.

This gives coherence a sharper edge. **Global coherence concerns not only what is logically possible, but how naturally an account explains the observed relationships and regularities of created reality without unnecessary independent assumptions.** An account can be possible and still need a long run of unexplained coincidences. That it can be imagined does not make it comparatively plausible. Chapter 3 puts this to work.

## Human knowing is already like this

The appeal to coherence is not a special method invented for this book. It is how human beings come to know almost everything they know.

Consider a doctor diagnosing an illness. She has a set of symptoms, some test results and a patient's history, none of which is conclusive alone. She forms the diagnosis that best accounts for all of them together, and remains ready to revise it if a new result does not fit. A jury does the same with testimony and evidence. So does a historian reconstructing what happened in a century no living person saw. So, on a smaller scale, do you, when you recognise a friend's voice on a crackling telephone line. Philosophers call the pattern *inference to the best explanation*. **Human understanding normally depends on assembling the most coherent account available from incomplete evidence.** We rarely possess exhaustive, certain access to the facts. We judge, compare, anticipate and revise, and our comparisons are mostly qualitative, of exactly the kind just described.

If that is how ordinary knowing works, then asking for the most globally coherent account of reality is not an eccentric substitute for evidence. It is the same activity, carried out at the largest scale.

Behind this ordinary practice lies a distinction the book will need. **Logic** is the necessary structure of consistency, implication, exclusion and possibility. **Reasoning** is its application, which can be done well or badly. There is, first, the complete coherence of all that is, perfectly comprehended and perfectly applicable by the One who made it. The author calls this **true logic**. It belongs inseparably to God's own nature, and no creature possesses it exhaustively. And there is the way finite minds move toward it. The author's definition is exact: "Inferential logic is the recursive refinement of partial understanding towards true logic through the discovery and application of relational coherence within the boundaries of created runtime order." *Created runtime order* is the operating created world, which Chapter 3 describes. **Status:** theological premise (true logic); working definition (inferential logic).

Two words in that definition carry the weight. *Partial*: the doctor's diagnosis is genuine knowledge, and it is not everything. *Recursive*: a later finding can revise a conclusion, the assumptions behind it, and sometimes the question itself. Observation, measurement, experiment, mathematics, philosophical argument and the reading of Scripture are not routes running beside inference. They are what inference works with. And because revision can reach even the book's own foundational commitments, one distinction stays fixed: revising an account of coherence is never revising the coherence of what God has made.

## What observation decides, and what it cannot

Coherence is not truth. A worldview can be internally coherent and false; a detailed and consistent story about a flat Earth is still wrong. The book says this once, here, and takes it as read from now on. Observation constrains ontology powerfully, and in three ways.

Robust observation rules out accounts that contradict it, however elegant they are. It reveals regularities that any account must respect, even before it can explain them. And it tests specific proposals: several of the Relational Substrate project's have been tested, some survived, some failed, and the failures are recorded in the project's public research history.

What observation cannot do is display the deepest structure of created reality as one more visible object. It narrows the field and rarely, if ever, picks out a single winner. So the warrant this book offers for the Relational Substrate is not that of experiment. It is a **global coherence argument**, and it holds itself to a plain standard: what it proposes, why, what supports it, what would count against it, and what remains unknown. A hypothesis stated clearly enough to be wrong can be improved. One hedged until it says nothing cannot. Chapter 9 names observations that would count against particular claims.

What, then, is proof? In this book the word has a bounded sense. **Proof** is the achievement of enough warrant, under stated premises, scope and constraints, for an inferred relationship to be relied on in further reasoning: to become what the book calls a **coherence factor**. A mathematical proof has this character in its strongest form: within its axioms and rules its conclusion is settled. An experimental result has it within what its procedures can show. Philosophical and theological arguments have warrant of their own kinds. A coherence factor keeps its scope, its source, its uncertainties and its dependencies in view, so that it can be revised if later work requires. Proof in this sense is real, and it is never the possession of the whole truth of what it concerns. The aim of inquiry is accordingly **inferential precision**: discriminating well between candidate relationships, tracing the warrant for each, and keeping what remains uncertain open to correction.

The posture this asks for is what the first edition called *disciplined confidence*, and it cuts both ways. Confidence must not outrun its warrant. Nor should justified understanding be discounted because it falls short of exhaustive knowledge. Overclaiming and underclaiming are both failures of the same discipline.

With these tools in hand, the book can return to the stone and ask the first ontological question. The stone appears to be a separate thing, sitting on the path, distinct from the light, the air and you. Is that what it fundamentally is?
