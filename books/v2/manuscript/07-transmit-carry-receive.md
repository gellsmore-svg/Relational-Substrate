# Chapter 7: Transmit, Carry, Receive

Return to the path in the morning light. Sunlight falls on the stone. Its grey surface returns some of that light and absorbs the rest, and its lighter vein returns a little more. Some of the returned light reaches your eye and is absorbed in the cells at the back of it. Molecules change, then cells, then nerves, and in the end you see a grey stone with a pale vein, there on the path, and you know it is there.

Something about the stone made a difference in you. The stone did not move. Nothing from its surface flew into your head. Yet the distinction between its grey body and its pale vein became a distinction in what you see.

This chapter asks what is required for that to happen, and its answer reaches far beyond light. The project names the answer **Transmit, Carry, Receive**, often written **T-C-R**. This book proposes that T-C-R is the grammar of consequential manifestation: the logical structure by which any distinction in created order becomes consequential to another state. The seeing of the stone is one instance. The chapter builds the grammar from that instance, then states the stronger claim.

## The grammar in its minimal form

Stated as simply as possible, the grammar is:

```text
distinction → preserved dependency → consequence
```

Each part is a role, not a component.

- **Transmit.** A distinction in one relational condition is capable of constraining something other than itself.
- **Carry.** The dependency between that distinction and its consequence preserves enough of the distinction for the consequence to depend on it. The carrier need not be a separate object. At the most primitive level, the relation itself may be the carrier.
- **Receive.** Another state becomes actual, or the range of what it can actualise is constrained, because of the source distinction. Reception is consequence, not arrival.

There is a simple test for whether a distinction has been carried. If two different source states could never make any difference to any consequence, the distinction between them has not been carried, however much else has happened.

In this minimal form the grammar presupposes very little. It does not presuppose that anything travels, or that there is a measurable space for it to travel through, or that some intermediate object passes from one place to another. It does not presuppose encoding and decoding, or a stack of hidden and visible layers. The source need not be used up. Reception need not happen exactly once. Each of these does occur in particular cases, and the cases below show several of them. None belongs to the grammar itself.

## Steps one and two: a distinction, and its capacity to constrain

What reaches you from the stone? "Light" is true as far as it goes. But light falls on everything on the path, and you do not see everything as the same. What reaches you is the *distinction* the stone makes in the light: its grey body returns light differently from its pale vein, and both differently from the soil. If the stone were exactly like its surroundings in every way light can register, you would not see it at all. So T-C-R concerns a **source distinction**: a distinguishable state or relation that could make a difference to something else, such as a dark mark on white paper, the pitch of a struck bell or the sequence of bases in a strand of DNA. Distinctions are everywhere. Most make no difference beyond themselves.

For one to matter, it must be capable of constraining something other than itself. That is what **transmit** means at the most general level. Sometimes the source pays for it. A struck bell grows quieter as the energy of the strike leaves it as sound, and an atom that emits light drops to a lower energy state. But that is a subcase, not the definition. When you tell someone your name, you do not forget it. A lighthouse makes its distinction available to every ship in range, night after night, without becoming any less a lighthouse. Transmission may involve emission, copying, exposure, the modulation of something already in motion, or simply standing in a relation on which something else depends. And it is governed by admissibility (Chapter 4). An atom can emit light only at the energies its structure allows, which is why each element has its own fingerprint of colours. A distinction can constrain something else only in the ways the created order makes available.

## Step three: carriage

In the daylight scene there is a gap between the stone and your eye. What preserves the stone's distinction across it?

It is tempting to imagine a little object crossing the gap, a pellet carrying the stone's greyness. Physics describes light as coming in discrete packets, called photons, but what a photon is between emission and absorption is one of the deep questions of quantum theory. In any case, the pellet picture misses what matters. Some simpler cases make it clearer.

Drop a pebble into a still pond, and a ring of waves spreads to the bank. The water at the bank rises and falls because of the pebble, but no water travelled from the centre. Each small parcel moved only a little. What crossed the pond was a *pattern*, borne by a succession of different water, none of which made the journey.

Or consider the chain of signal fires that ancient peoples used to send news across a country. A fire is lit on one hill. On the next, a watcher sees it and lights a second fire, and so on for a hundred miles. No fire travelled. Each was a different fire, burning different wood. Yet the news arrived, because each new fire was lit *in dependence on* the one before.

Or consider a telephone call. Your voice, a pattern in air pressure, becomes a pattern in an electrical current, then in radio waves, then in pulses of light in a glass fibre, then in current again, then in air pressure at your friend's ear. The physical vehicle changed completely, several times. Your friend still hears what you said.

In each case, what must persist is not a particular material thing. It is **the relevant distinction, preserved by a chain of dependency**. At each stage, the carrier's state still depends on the source's state closely enough that the receiver, at the end, can be changed in a way that depends on what was at the source. In these manifest cases the carrier may be an object, a wave, a chain of local interactions, a record or a changing medium. *Carrier* is the name for whatever bears the dependency at a given stage, and carriers can be substituted. A carrier can preserve consequence without preserving its implementation. That is why a conversation survives being routed through copper, radio and glass.

These are cases where carriers are easy to see. At the deepest level the book proposes something simpler. Where two relational conditions are constitutively related, the relation between them is the dependency. Nothing further needs to cross between them. The carrier is the relation itself.

This is where Chapter 6's account of identity does real work. What a carrier must preserve is not everything about the source, and not merely some summary number. It must preserve the source distinction's **relevant equivalence class**: whatever the receiver's possible responses can tell apart. A telephone line can drop the fine texture of a voice and still carry the words, because nothing your friend does next depends on that texture. It cannot drop the difference between "yes" and "no".

**Provenance** can matter here too. A letter's words may arrive intact while the question of who sent it is lost. If the receiver will act differently depending on the sender, as a bank does with a signature or a court with a chain of custody, then the provenance is part of what must be carried. If nothing downstream depends on it, it can be dropped without loss.

Carriage is also **constrained**. Dependency can degrade. Ripples spread and fade. A watcher on a hill falls asleep. A telephone line hisses with noise. Carriage holds only along the dependencies created order supports, and only against disturbances they can absorb. Like every kind of persistence in Chapter 6, it is persistence of something, against some kind of disturbance, over some length of time.

## Step four: reception

Light from the stone reaches many things besides your eye. It strikes the window of a passing car, the leaves of a hedge, the grass. Some of it passes through the car window almost untouched. Did the window "receive" the stone?

In one sense, yes: the light arrived there. But nothing at the window was changed in a way that depended on the stone's particular pattern. The window would have been in the same state if the stone had been a different colour. In the sense that matters for this grammar, the window did not receive the distinction. It was passed through.

**Reception** is not arrival. It is **consequence**: another state changed, or constrained, in a way that depends on the source distinction. At the back of your eye, light is absorbed by a molecule that changes shape when it absorbs it. That change starts a cascade that alters cells and nerves. The distinction between grey and pale has become a distinction in you.

A letter makes the point in human terms. A letter delivered and left unopened on the mat has *arrived*. A letter opened, read and acted on has been *received*. The words are the same in both cases. What differs is whether the distinction they carry has made a difference in someone.

What a receiver can receive, and how, is set by the receiver itself. This is **receiver compatibility**, and it has two sides.

First, every receiver has a range of distinctions it can register. Your eye responds to visible light, not to the radio waves passing through you at this moment. That is not because radio waves fail to arrive. The receiving molecules simply admit only certain changes. Reception, like transmission, is governed by admissibility, now on the receiving side.

Second, receivers differ in how they respond to repeated or continuing arrivals:

- A thermostat receives continuously, responding to the temperature as it changes.
- An ear integrates many arrivals into one sound.
- A light switch already "on" is unchanged by being switched on again; the repeat is harmless.
- A choir rehearsing a phrase is changed more by each repetition.
- A bank ledger must record each payment exactly once. Record it twice and the account is wrong.

Only the last kind of receiver needs exactly-once reception. For a receiver whose consequence is a count, duplicated reception is error. That is a property of transactional receivers, not a universal law of reception. What is universal is that reception has a definite character. How a receiver responds to what reaches it is part of what that receiver is.

A broadcast radio programme shows how much compatibility involves. An FM station on 91.7 megahertz does not send its music *at* 91.7 megahertz. That figure is the carrier frequency, a radio wave oscillating nearly ninety-two million times a second. The programme is carried in the way that frequency is made to vary, slightly and continuously, in step with the sound: the *modulation*. A receiver must be able to tune to the carrier, and it must also have the right arrangement for recovering the variation, a demodulator matched to frequency modulation. A medium-wave set cannot tune to that band at all, and a receiver built only to follow changes in a signal's strength cannot properly recover a programme carried in changes of its frequency. The music never was the carrier frequency. Five things need to be kept apart: the carrier frequency; the modulation; the preserved organisation of the programme; the receiver's compatible arrangement; and the programme as reconstructed at the loudspeaker. The general principle is plain: **consequential reception depends on compatibility between the organisation in which a distinction is preserved and the organisation of the receiver.**

## When what is carried is an identity

The radio programme is not one distinction but an organised whole: a voice, a melody, a sequence of words, many distinctions held together in relation. When what passes through T-C-R is an identity of this kind, two functions become visible that a single distinction does not show.

On the transmitting side, something must establish and constrain the distinctions so that they can take part in one coherent identity. The station's modulator does this, laying the sound's variations into the carrier in a fixed and orderly way. A cello does it for a melody: its strings and body admit some vibrations and not others. The book calls this an **identity organiser**: a relational organisation or process that establishes, structures or constrains distinctions so that they can participate in a coherent, consequential identity.

On the receiving side, something must recover the identity from what arrives, which is never exactly what was sent. The radio's demodulator does this. So, far more remarkably, do a human ear and brain. The book calls this an **identity coherer**: a relational organisation capable of preserving or reconstructing consequentially relevant identity from variations received through a compatible interface.

Three cautions keep these terms honest. Neither is a substance, and neither is a field or a vibration by definition: a modulator, an ear and a lock are organisers or coherers because of what they do. Cohering is not confined to the receiver. The choice of frequency modulation itself makes a programme robust, because electrical crackle mostly disturbs a signal's strength and leaves the frequency variation that carries the music largely intact. And organising and cohering are complementary, so one process can do both. Neither role belongs to the minimal grammar. A single distinction can be transmitted and received with no organising or cohering at all, and modulation and demodulation, like encoding and decoding, are particular cases. The working reading is that organiser and coherer are T-C-R's transmitting and receiving roles, specialised to identities. Whether they are anything more fundamental is left open. **Status:** RS working hypothesis; developing research concepts.

A distinction may be transmitted, and even carried, without ever reaching consequential reception: light from a distant star that falls on nothing able to register it, or a letter lost in the post. The propagation is real. The T-C-R chain is simply incomplete. A distinction becomes *consequential* only when it is received.

## The whole grammar

In the manifest world each part usually has a visible vehicle: something emitted, a chain of carriers, a receiver changed by what arrives.

The dependency persists while the vehicles change. At substrate depth the carrier may simply be the relation.](v2/figures/fig-tcr.png)

The morning scene shows three further features. T-C-R **nests**: the nerve impulse from eye to brain is its own chain, a pulse of electrical change passed from segment to segment of a fibre, which travels the length of the nerve while no particle does. It **composes**: light from every point of the stone reaches the retina at once and forms one image, while Sun to stone, stone to eye and eye to brain run in sequence. And chains are **coordinated**. Your two eyes receive slightly different images, and depth is seen in how they are related, not in either alone. Your ears receive sound at slightly different times, and direction is heard in the difference. Chapter 9 will show why relations among many chancy events can matter more than the events taken one by one.

## A grammar that recurs

The same three-part form turns up wherever one thing is consequential to another: in light and sound, in the copying of DNA, in nerves, in speech, in law and in liturgy. One explanation is coincidence, many different mechanisms happening to share an engineering pattern. This book proposes a deeper one.

If A and B are genuinely related in a consequential sense, something about A must be capable of making a difference to B. That difference must be preserved well enough for B to depend on it. And B must actually be different because of it. That is already T-C-R. On this proposal T-C-R is not a mechanism added to a relational world. It is the operational form of consequential relation itself. It recurs from the substrate to speech because anything consequentially relational must instantiate some version of it.

**Status:** an RS working hypothesis, and one of the book's central ones. It is supported by the recurrence just described and by its economy: one grammar explains why consequence has the same shape across domains that share no mechanism. It would be weakened by a clear case of genuine consequence that cannot be described as a distinction preserved by a dependency, or by a demonstration that the recurrence is better explained as convergent design. Its limits matter as much as its reach. A shared grammar across domains is a structural analogy, not an identity of nature. Light, a gene and a sentence share the form of consequence. They do not thereby become the same kind of thing.

The grammar is also **recursive**. A receiver at one stage is, at once, a source relative to another relation: the molecule in your eye that receives light transmits to the cell, which receives and transmits to the nerve. Consequential relation is therefore not a set of separate pipelines but a network, in which every present state is the reception of earlier distinctions and a source of later ones. This gives a sharper picture of the relation between present actuality and what can happen next, which Chapter 4 raised. Actuality carries its history because past distinctions, received, have become present conditions. They shape what is currently reachable because those present conditions are, in turn, transmitted.

## Cause as consequential relation

The grammar also suggests a precise account of cause and effect, suited to a world in which actualisation is chancy.

In a deterministic picture, a cause *produces* its effect: given the cause, the effect follows. In a stochastic world that cannot be the whole story, because what happens next is open. But a distinction can still make a difference. It can exclude some next states. It can make others possible. It can change how likely they are. It can constrain how events are correlated, preserve an invariant through change, or alter what will be accessible later. Each of these is a way of being causal without producing a fixed result.

The book's working formulation is this:

> **A causal relation exists where a distinction in one relational condition modifies the admissible actualisation, or the consequential state, of another through a distinction-preserving dependency.**

The formulation was checked against the project's simplest relational models before being adopted. In them, a three-way condition changes the likelihood of the next two-way event: a present distinction modifies what happens next by weighting it, without determining it. The initial value of that same condition is soon forgotten: within a short run, nothing in the visible state depends on it any longer. On the formulation, the initial distinction has stopped being causal for that state, because no dependency preserves it, while the condition's *current* value goes on mattering. That is the T-C-R test at work. Rules that forbid a change alter what can be reached at all, and rules that merely favour it alter only how likely it is. Those are two distinct ways of being causal. **Status:** working hypothesis. It fits the models. Whether it is the right account of causation in nature is a question of coherence, not of those models.

The account also disciplines a common word. To say that one change "enabled" another can mean four different things: that it came first (*precedence*); that it removed competitors, so the other became the likelier next event (*opportunity*); that it changed the other's own tendency (*rate*); or that it made the other possible at all (*support*). Only the last two are changes of admissibility or tendency, and only support is the work of a forbidding rule. In the project's later models the senses came apart cleanly. Most soft "enabling" turned out to be opportunity.

## Manifestation

There is one further reason T-C-R matters, and it is the deepest. The book has proposed a created relational order beneath the manifest physical world of stones and light. For that order to be manifest at all, as material and experienced reality, there must be a **consequence-preserving relationship** between distinctions in the underlying order and the actual world we meet. If distinctions at the deepest level could make no difference to anything manifest, they would be idle.

That is a minimal claim, and it should be kept minimal. It is tempting to picture something invisible being *transformed* into something visible, as though a hidden object rose through layers and emerged as a stone. That is only one possible architecture, and the book does not assume it. The underlying order and the manifest world might be two descriptions of one relational fact. They might be different resolutions of one order. They might be related by a mapping not yet understood, or in a way not yet conceived. The question of whether the substrate is itself spatial, or whether space arises from it, also remains open. What the book does claim is that, whatever the architecture, consequence must be preserved between them. That is the T-C-R insight applied at its deepest point.

## How T-C-R gathers what came before

T-C-R is not a separate principle added to the previous chapters. It draws them together. It is what mediation requires, if nothing acts across a gap of non-relation (Chapter 3). It is shaped by **admissibility** (Chapter 4) in what a source can transmit, what dependencies exist and what a receiver can register. It is shot through with **constrained chance** (Chapter 5): which molecule in your retina absorbs light from the stone at a given instant is a matter of chance, and the image is steady because many such events compose. And it depends on **identity** (Chapter 6): the carried distinction persists as an equivalence class across changing carriers, with its provenance where something downstream depends on it.

Together these show that a relational world is not a frozen lattice in which everything is related and nothing happens. Relation becomes history: a distinction here makes a difference there, which can in turn be transmitted, carried and received. Life is built from such chains (Chapter 11), and so is human communication (Chapter 12). The theological chapters use the grammar, with care, as a lens on some forms of corruption and restoration (Chapters 14 and 15).

**Status.** Emission, propagation and absorption are established physics, and T-C-R organises that physics without replacing it. It has not been shown to predict anything the physics does not. That T-C-R is the form of consequential relation itself, reaches to the substrate, is recursive and supplies the right account of cause are working hypotheses. Two things are open. In every model the project has built, the dependencies that carry distinctions were designed, and whether created order generates its own carriers from minimal relations is not known. Nor is the architecture of manifestation.

## What T-C-R does not mean

The same three-part form appears in physics, in biology, in human conversation and in Scripture. Scripture presents God's word as going out and accomplishing its purpose in those who receive it:

> `For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud, that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater: So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it.`
> *Isaiah 55:10-11*

> `How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher?… So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God.`
> *Romans 10:14, 17*

Isaiah's image is physical (rain that waters the earth), and its subject is God's word that accomplishes his purpose. Paul traces a sequence of sending, preaching, hearing and believing. The form is unmistakably that of something made available, carried and received. In a creation made by the Word, that likeness is not surprising. But Chapter 1 has already given the governing principle: shared form across categories does not mean shared nature. God's word is not a physical signal, and faith is not the absorption of light. The soul is not a pattern carried by a disturbance. Information transfer is not consciousness. The physical case does not explain the spiritual one, and the spiritual one is not secretly physical.

If anything, the direction of illumination runs the other way. A world made by a God who speaks, and whose word goes out and accomplishes what he purposes, is a world in which it is fitting that even light and sound should be consequential in this way. Physical T-C-R may be one of the ways created order bears the likeness of its Maker. But likeness is the most that can be claimed.

## The question that remains

Seeing the stone is the reception of one fairly simple distinction. Much of what reaches us is richer: a voice recognised through a wall, a melody heard on a different instrument from the one it was written for, a bird known only from its bones and from drawings by people long dead. These are organised identities, and they survive changes of carrier that might seem bound to destroy them. How they hold together, and how relations become organisations and organisations become new wholes, is the question of Chapter 8.
