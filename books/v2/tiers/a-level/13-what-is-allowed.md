# Chapter 13: What Is Allowed

A surgeon and an assailant may make physically near-identical cuts; one heals and the other commits a crime, and nothing in the physics of the cut tells you which. Persons now enter the world of constrained possibility as agents. When we say something is "allowed", what kind of allowing is meant?

## Three registers of admissibility

Chapter 4 promised that *admissible* would carry only its physical sense until now. Three questions must be distinguished:

- **Physical admissibility**: is it possible at all within created order? If not, it is impossible for anyone.
- **Operational admissibility**: is it within this agent's power? If not, it is possible in principle but inaccessible to this agent.
- **Moral admissibility**: is it right? If not, it may be possible and within power, and still wrong.

The registers are independent. Lying is physically possible, within ordinary power, and wrong. Sustaining creation in being occurs, but is within no creature's power. Creating something beautiful is possible, within power and good. Scalpel and knife are equally possible and equally within power; the moral register divides them.

> `And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.`
> *Genesis 3:6*

The fruit was within reach and eating within their power; what was forbidden was the means, wisdom seized by taking what God had withheld. Admissible in the first two registers, not the third.

Collapsing the registers yields three characteristic mistakes, named in the first edition:

- **The naturalist mistake** collapses the moral into the physical: if nature produces it, it cannot really be wrong.
- **The pietist mistake** collapses the physical into the moral: if it is wrong, God will not let it happen.
- **The technological mistake** collapses the physical into the operational: if we can now do it, we should.

## Why the physical cannot settle the moral

This is the book's firmest safeguard against misreading Chapter 10. That chapter's argument **does** imply that moral acts are real events: a lie is sent, carried and received as a change in someone who now believes falsely; a kept promise honours a commitment carried across days. Moral life is not a ghostly overlay on sealed objects it cannot touch.

It **does not** imply that any physical fact settles moral admissibility. The physics of a lie and of a truth is the same physics. A relational account can tell you your words will have consequences; it cannot tell you to tell the truth. The moral question belongs to persons answerable to one another and to God, and is answered by a different authority.

> `He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?`
> *Micah 6:8*

> `Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness.`
> *Romans 2:15*

What is good is *shown*, revealed by God and written on the heart, not deduced from the behaviour of matter. Moral admissibility is a real layer of created order whose source is the will and character of God.

## Alignment

**Alignment** is fitting the order a thing or person is made for. A bone set straight heals aligned; a string tuned to pitch is aligned with the instrument's design. For persons, alignment is a moral and relational reality, not a measurement: right relation to God, others and creation. Justice, mercy, faithfulness, truthfulness and love are its shape in persons.

The first edition proposed that beauty, order, truth and goodness are "four perspectives on the same structural property". That went too far: it risked turning moral and aesthetic realities into physical configuration, the very collapse this chapter rejects, and it contradicted the technical volume's statement that structural alignment is not a synonym for goodness. This edition withdraws it. Goodness, truth and beauty are related, and one in God, but in creation not reducible to a single physical measure. The book defines **beauty**, in the author's terms, as the apprehensible expression of the relational patterns of goodness, spiritual in origin, within created organisation; a beautiful thing need not be perfect in every part (Chapter 16).

## Agency and responsibility

Moral admissibility presupposes genuine agency; without it, love would be mechanism and praise or blame category mistakes. Christian traditions differ on what agency requires: some hold responsibility requires that one could have done otherwise; others that it is compatible with God's sovereign ordering, provided persons act willingly from their own desires and reasons. The book does not decide between them.

> `I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life.`
> *Deuteronomy 30:19*

One point falls within the book's subject. A world whose actualisation is not fixed in advance (Chapter 5) does not settle human freedom in either direction. **Constrained chance is not freedom.** A coin toss is not a choice, and the book makes no claim that agency is molecular chance. Physics cannot settle whether the will is free, and the ontology does not try. Agency belongs to persons, in their own category.

## Time as moral opportunity

> `So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.`
> *Psalm 90:12*

The days are counted as opportunity, given and limited, for wisdom. **Time as moral opportunity** is not a second kind of time and not a clock unit; it is what succession (Chapter 6) means for a person who must act within it. **Status:** the author's interpretation, within a scriptural constraint.

Opportunity is relative to the person: a nanosecond is no occasion for decision, an hour can be decisive, yet duration is not the measure. An athlete's two minutes rest on months of preparation. Earlier choices change capacity, disposition and even which options are visible, so a moral life is a trajectory in which much is open and nothing begins from nowhere. In this chapter's terms, preparation changes operational admissibility. Responsibility follows the same shape: "unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required" (Luke 12:48), a difference of responsibility, not of worth.

## Boundaries that free

Chapter 4 argued that physical constraint is generative. Scripture says the same of the moral world, emphatically:

> `The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple.`
> *Psalm 19:7*

> `And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts.`
> *Psalm 119:45*

Liberty is found *in* seeking God's precepts. A boundary rightly placed does not shrink the space of good action; it gives it shape. This is a **structural analogy**: the constraint that shapes a crystal and the commandment that shapes a life share a form, a boundary that enables. They do not share a nature: the commandment is spoken by a Person to persons and received in conscience, trust and obedience, while the crystal's constraint is received by nothing.

## What this chapter establishes

- Physical, operational and moral admissibility are independent; collapsing them yields naturalist, pietist and technological mistakes.
- Moral acts are real events in a relational world, but no physical fact settles what is right; the source of moral order is God.
- Alignment is moral and relational; the first edition's "four perspectives" claim is withdrawn.
- Constrained chance is not freedom; time is moral opportunity; moral boundaries are generative, by structural analogy.
