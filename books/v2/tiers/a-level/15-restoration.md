# Chapter 15: Restoration

Optimism, distraction, denial and private consolation are not hope. Hope in the strong sense requires that reality contain more promise than corruption can destroy. If disorder is ultimate, hope is theatre; if creation is God's, hope may be the most realistic stance there is. This chapter's first claim is a boundary on which the rest depends.

## Restoration is God's act

Chapter 6 showed that preserving is not repairing: when information is destroyed, conservation keeps what remains and restores nothing lost. Living things partly overcome this with templates (Chapter 11), but that capacity fails; every living thing dies. The healing of creation cannot be expected from creation's own resources. If restoration came only from the persistence of the world's invariants, there would be no raising of the dead, no righting of wrongs, no new creation.

> `For it pleased the Father that in him should all fulness dwell; And, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself; by him, I say, whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven.`
> *Colossians 1:19-20*

The One through whom all things were made and in whom they consist is the One through whom they are reconciled. Redemption is not alien to creation, but it is *his* answer: not a process within the substrate, a self-correction of physical order, or a law of nature. Hence Chapter 4's insistence that God is not one more cause among causes, and that the book offers no account of the manner of God's action. The resurrection of Christ was not an unusually improbable configuration of the substrate but an act of the Creator. Miracles are received as Scripture records them, neither explained as hidden physics nor explained away.

## The scope of hope

> `Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God.`
> *Romans 8:21*

> `And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new.`
> *Revelation 21:4-5*

The hope is not souls escaping a ruined world but creation delivered, all things reconciled, new heavens and a new earth (2 Peter 3:13), the wolf dwelling with the lamb (Isaiah 11:6). On an ontology of sealed objects with no intrinsic bearing on persons, the redemption of persons and the renewal of creation would be two unrelated projects, and some forms of Christianity have drifted toward exactly that. On the relational account, persons are formed from the dust of a creation relational from its foundation, so their wounding and healing are bound up with it. The ontology does not *explain* redemption. It shows why Scripture's redemption is of *this* world.

## Restoring reception

Chapter 14 described corruption partly as failed reception; Scripture describes restoration partly as reception restored.

> `A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh.`
> *Ezekiel 36:26*

The gospel that comes "in word only" has arrived; the gospel that comes "in power, and in the Holy Ghost" (1 Thessalonians 1:5) has been received. This is **structural analogy**: the Spirit's work is not a physical process, faith is not a receptor state, and hearing the gospel is not an exactly-once transaction; Scripture speaks of a word received, rehearsed, remembered and received again. What is shared is only the outline of something sent, carried and received as change.

## The same person, raised

> `Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have.`
> *Luke 24:39*

> `It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:42-44*

The risen Christ is recognisably himself, wounds included. Resurrection raises the same persons, transformed, not replacements, and it concerns all, to life or to judgment (John 5:28-29).

How can the same person be raised when the body has returned to dust? Chapter 6 distinguished equivalence from individual continuity: a reconstruction may belong to the same class without being the same individual, and conservation cannot restore destroyed carriers of identity. Chapter 12 added that personal identity is grounded in the inner person and in God's knowing and calling. So resurrection is **not** the substrate preserving a person's invariants through death and **not** the reconstruction of an indistinguishable replacement. Death destroys the configuration; the spirit "shall return unto God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7); the identity of the person raised is held by God. The book makes no claim about mechanism: "it doth not yet appear what we shall be" (1 John 3:2). Its contribution is negative clarity: it shows why resurrection cannot be a natural process of created order, and so must be, as Scripture says, the act of God.

## Human death and the Cross

Human death is bound up with sin (Romans 5:12) and is an enemy to be destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26); it is not humanity's intended end. The author's further interpretation, that animal death entered with the Fall, carries the open fossil-record problem named in Chapter 14. The narrower scriptural claim stands on any reading.

> `No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again.`
> *John 10:18*

In the ordinary case death is corruption prevailing over a creature that cannot resist. Christ's death is presented as yielded: "he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost" (John 19:30). The suffering and death were real; corruption did not overtake him: "neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption" (Psalm 16:10).

## Restored alignment can be higher than before

Offered as theological and pastoral reflection, not binding doctrine and not a claim that evil was necessary: untested innocence is real goodness but unowned. Disorder exposes what was hidden; truthfully faced, repented of and repaired, it can yield alignment that is conscious, tested, chosen and humbler, as in a marriage rebuilt or a faith come through doubt. Restoration does not erase history: forgiveness changes what can follow from a wrong without undoing every effect, and wound and repair remain part of the story (Hebrews 12:11; Romans 5:3-4).

Four clarifications. First, disorder is not good in itself; God redeems evil that occurred, a greater claim than that evil was needed. Second, restoration is costly; at creation's scale it cost the Cross, foreordained "before the foundation of the world" (1 Peter 1:20), which the author reads as a real commitment made before creation concerning an event within its history, a decision rather than a forecast. Third, freedom is for love and trust, not a licence; faith must be a genuine, willing response, however the traditions describe the freedom involved. Fourth, freedom never required actualised evil: before the Fall the command and the possibility of disobedience were real (Genesis 2:16-17), yet creation was "very good" (Genesis 1:31). **Status:** scriptural constraint (inference from the text).

## Consummation and judgment

The hope is consummation, not mere return to Eden: death "swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). Judgment belongs to it; without judgment redemption would be sentimental (Matthew 25:46). Such judgment does not depend on what a damaged record can establish. Created order carries every act's consequences but not every act's provenance; God knows the whole history of each act, its intention, circumstances, effects and responsibility, which is why his judgment can be just where human courts fail. Greater material damage is not by itself greater guilt.

Between Christ's resurrection and the renewal of all things, the gathered people of God, in concrete local assemblies, can be real if imperfect places where restored relation becomes visible: truth spoken and received, wrongs confessed and forgiven, remembrance kept. The book attempts no doctrine of the church.

The final answer to corruption is not a better ontology but the Lord who creates, judges, redeems and renews. What the ontology can show is that this hope concerns the world we inhabit.

## What this chapter establishes

- Creation cannot heal itself; restoration is God's act, not a natural process.
- The hope is the renewal of this world, which the relational account shows to be one work with the redemption of persons.
- Resurrection raises the same person, held by God; it is neither preservation of invariants nor reconstruction.
- Restored alignment can exceed untested innocence, but evil was never necessary; judgment rests on God's complete knowledge.
