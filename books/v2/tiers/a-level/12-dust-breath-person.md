# Chapter 12: Dust, Breath, and Person

Chapter 7 followed the seeing of the stone as far as physical description reaches: light, retina, nerve. But the chain ended in *someone* who saw a stone, recognised it, found it beautiful or dull and decided to pick it up. Every earlier link is a transmission between configurations. The last is not. The one who sees is a person, and here the distinction between categories matters most.

## Formed from the dust

> `And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness... So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.`
> *Genesis 1:26-27*

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

A body is *formed* from *dust*, the same material as the earth; *breath* is imparted directly by God; the result is a *living soul*. Humanity is not made of a special heavenly substance: "dust thou art" (Genesis 3:19); God "remembereth that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14). The human body belongs fully to the relational physical order of Chapters 3 to 9 and the living order of Chapter 11. But dust alone is not a person. What distinguishes humanity lies in what God has made it to be: an image-bearing, conscious, responsible person called into relation with him.

## Embodiment is good

Genesis does not present dust and breath as opposed principles yoked together, but as jointly making one living creature. The body is part of what a human person is, not a prison. Hence Christian hope is resurrection of the body (Chapter 15), and bodily life carries moral weight: limitation, vulnerability, place, dependence, the capacity to give and to be wounded are the conditions under which creaturely persons love and keep faith.

A human moral act is therefore not a spiritual event with a bodily sign attached. When someone lies, the words, the speaking body and the changes produced in others belong to the act. Its moral reality, a wrong before God and against a neighbour, is not identical with those events and not separate from them. **Within one person the two are causally connected, not identical** (Chapter 1). **Status:** the author's interpretation of a scriptural constraint; the mechanism is open. This is the sense in which created order is the theatre and language of moral consequence (Chapter 10).

## Personhood is deeper than embodiment

> `God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.`
> *John 4:24*

Reasoning only from human cases would define the person by the body. Christian faith rules this out. The Father, the Son and the Spirit are persons; the Father and the Spirit have no bodies; the Son was a person eternally before the Incarnation. If personhood were constituted by embodiment, the doctrine of the Trinity would be incoherent. Since that doctrine is foundational, personhood is the deeper category.

- A **person** is a centre of awareness, relation, agency, address and responsibility: one who can know and be known, speak and be spoken to, act and answer for the act.
- **Embodiment** is the mode in which creaturely persons exist and act within created order. For humans it is not optional, but personhood as such is not a property of bodies. In the Incarnation the eternal Son took the creaturely, embodied mode to himself, permanently.

This is Scripture shaping ontology, and the result is more coherent: it explains why persons cannot be reduced to configurations of matter while honouring the body as the God-given mode of human personhood.

## The inner person

> `For what man knoweth the things of a man, save the spirit of man which is in him?`
> *1 Corinthians 2:11*

> `Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.`
> *Ecclesiastes 12:7*

> `And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul.`
> *Matthew 10:28*

> `For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.`
> *2 Corinthians 4:16*

These texts offer no technical theory of soul and spirit, and the book does not settle the debates. Together they establish that the inner person is real, self-known as nothing else knows it, given by God and returning to him, not killed with the body, and renewed while the outward person wastes. The human person has depth, and the inward is not reducible to the outward.

Here the book applies its guard at full force. **The soul is not a configuration of the relational substrate.** Nothing in Chapters 3 to 9 describes, explains or contains it. When the book says relation runs through every category, it means persons are relational beings in a creation relational from its foundation, not that persons are made of the same relations as stones.

## Consciousness

Consciousness depends on body and brain: damage the brain and awareness changes or ceases. Yet why there is anything it is like to see grey, the *hard problem*, remains unsolved by any physics, mainstream or relational, and may exceed creaturely comprehension. **The Relational Substrate does not explain consciousness.** It claims something smaller: a relational world, in which differences already become consequences for something else, is a more fitting setting for conscious persons than a world of sealed objects. The step from a changed retinal molecule to the experience of seeing is not taken by the substrate. **Status:** open question.

## Knowing and choosing as events

Knowing is itself something that happens. In recognising the stone, a received distinction is related to what you already know, and you know something new: observation supplies a distinction, inference receives it, often without deliberate attention. Persons can also hold the not-yet-actual in view and choose among alternatives, and a choice actualises one configuration rather than another: the stone is now somewhere it would not otherwise be. **Knowing and choosing are actualisations in persons.** **Status:** ontological inference. Reasoning about represented relationships at this level the book calls **relational inference**.

None of this makes created order a knower. It is not conscious and did not become capable of consciousness by complexity. Creaturely personhood is where two kinds of reality meet by design: a non-conscious created runtime order fitted to bear consequence, and a conscious capacity for relation to God given with the breath of Genesis 2:7.

## Image and calling

The image of God has been read as reason, moral capacity, dominion, relationship and representative calling. Genesis joins image to dominion. The book's broad reading: the image is not a material feature but a status and calling, to know God and be known, to answer to him and to represent his care within creation. Animals are not called to account in Scripture; humans are.

> `Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine.`
> *Isaiah 43:1*

Addressed to Israel, the verse reveals a pattern for personal identity: what makes a person *this* person is not finally a configuration of dust but being known, called and named by God.

## The identity of a person

Chapter 6 proposed that physical things remain the same kind through equivalence and the same individual through lineage. Persons resemble this: bodily material is replaced while you remain, and character and memory carry history. The resemblance explains why replacing atoms does not threaten identity. But it is a **structural analogy**, not a definition. Personal identity is grounded more deeply, in the continuity of the inner person and ultimately in God's knowing and calling. Hence death does not end a person, and resurrection raises the same person rather than producing a copy (Chapter 15).

## What this chapter establishes

- Humans are formed dust and imparted breath: fully within physical order, not reducible to it.
- An embodied act and its moral reality are causally connected, not identical.
- Personhood is deeper than embodiment (from the Trinity); the soul is not a substrate configuration.
- RS does not explain consciousness; knowing and choosing are actualisations in persons; personal identity rests on God's knowing and calling.
