# Before You Begin

This book has two hard words in its title. Let's deal with them first.

**Ontology** is a long word for a simple question: *what is something, really?* Not what it looks like, and not only what it does, but what it actually is. This book asks that question about the whole world.

**Biblical** means the book starts with the Bible. It does not begin by asking science for permission to talk about God. It begins where the Bible begins, with God making a world that is really there.

So *Coherent Biblical Ontology* means a clear picture of what the world really is, one that hangs together and starts from the Bible.

## Four kinds of claim

The book makes different kinds of claim, and it tries to tell you which is which. Watch for these four:

- **The Bible says so.** For example, God made the world.
- **We can see or measure it.** For example, a dropped stone falls.
- **This is the book's best idea.** It might be right, and it might be wrong. The book will tell you why it thinks the idea is worth holding, and what would count against it.
- **Nobody knows yet.** Some questions are open, and the book says so.

The most important thing to remember is this: the book's big new idea, which it calls the *Relational Substrate*, belongs to the third kind. It is a careful guess about how God's world works. It is not something the Bible teaches, and the book will never pretend that it is.

## One ordinary scene

Every chapter comes back to one scene: a clear morning, and a stone on the path in front of you. Almost every question in this book is hiding there: what the stone is, how you see it, why it is the same stone tomorrow, and who *you* are, the one who sees it.

## A note for readers of earlier editions

Earlier versions of this book described particular mechanisms, such as tiny "knots" that make up matter, as if they were settled. This edition does not rely on them. They remain ideas worth testing, and nothing more.
