# Chapter 9: Right and Wrong in a Real World

On Monday you promise a friend you will help her move house on Saturday. On Saturday morning you would rather stay in bed. You get up and go.

That ordinary scene holds a lot. The person who gets up on Saturday is the same person who promised on Monday. Your words went out from you and became something in your friend: a plan she is counting on. And the promise *binds* you. If you stayed in bed, she would not just be surprised. She would be let down. You *owed* it.

## Why the kind of world matters

If the world were made of completely separate objects, like snooker balls, relationships would be something added on top of a world with no room for them. If the world is made of relationships from the very bottom, then a life full of promises, families, friendships and trust is not a stranger in it. It fits the grain of the world God made. That is one of the book's main reasons for its big idea: it helps everything we know, from stones to promises, hang together.

That does not mean promises are physics. A promise is not a force. Love is not a wave. The book is showing that the world has room for these things, not that they are made of the same stuff as stones.

## Three kinds of "allowed"

A surgeon cuts a patient's skin with a scalpel. An attacker cuts someone's skin with a knife. The two cuts might look almost the same. One is healing, and the other is a crime. Nothing in the cut itself tells you which is which.

So when we say something is "allowed", we can mean three different things:

- **Can it happen at all?** Is it possible in the world God made?
- **Can *I* do it?** Is it within my power?
- **Should it be done?** Is it right?

These are different questions. Lying is possible. Almost anyone can do it. And it is wrong. Holding the whole world in being is possible, since it happens, but no creature can do it. Only God can.

The Bible's first story of wrongdoing turns on this. The fruit was within reach, and eating it was within Adam and Eve's power. But it was not right.

The most important point is this: **knowing what is possible never tells you what is right.** The science of a lie is the same as the science of the truth. What is right is shown to us by God:

> `He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?`
> *Micah 6:8*

## Chance is not choosing

Chapter 5 said the world might be open, with chance working inside its rules. Does that make you free? No. A coin toss is not a choice. The book does not claim that your choices come from tiny chance events. Science cannot settle whether your will is free, and this book does not try. Choosing belongs to persons.

## Time as a gift

The Bible speaks of time in a way a clock does not:

> `So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.`
> *Psalm 90:12*

Our days are an **opportunity**, given and limited, to grow wise and do good. What you did yesterday shapes what you are able to do today. An athlete's two minutes in a race rest on months of training. That is why the Bible says that more is asked of those who have been given more (Luke 12:48).

## Rules that free

People often think every rule is a loss of freedom. But remember the river and its banks in Chapter 4. The Bible says God's commands work the same way:

> `And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts.`
> *Psalm 119:45*

A good boundary gives life its shape, so that you can grow into what you were made to be. (This is a likeness of form: a command is spoken by God to persons, and a riverbank is not.)

## What this chapter has shown

- A world of relationships is a natural home for promises and love, without turning them into physics.
- Possible, within my power and right are three different questions, and the first never settles the third.
- Chance is not choosing, and time is an opportunity.
