# Chapter 1: Scripture and the World That Is There

Every account of reality starts somewhere. This book starts with Scripture, and says so openly.

> `All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness.`
> *2 Timothy 3:16*

## Scripture speaks first

Scripture is authoritative in the form it actually takes. Genesis is narrative and worship. It is not a laboratory report, and it is not a hidden code for physics.

So "Scripture first" means this:

- Scripture sets the **boundaries** within which an account of reality may be built.
- Inside those boundaries there is room for **patient inquiry** into how the world works.
- A proposal that **contradicts** Scripture is ruled out, however elegant.
- A proposal Scripture **neither teaches nor forbids** may be explored, but never presented as if the Bible taught it.

When the Bible and science seem to collide, the fault usually lies in a human extension: a text pressed beyond what it says, or a conclusion drawn beyond what was measured.

The Relational Substrate is a modern proposal, so the book keeps four relations carefully apart:

1. **Scripture teaches** something (the world is created, held, good and distinct from God).
2. The proposal **is consistent with** what Scripture teaches.
3. The proposal offers **a possible mechanism** for something Scripture says, without Scripture teaching the mechanism.
4. Scripture **provokes a question** the proposal explores.

Only the first carries Scripture's authority. The others never borrow it.

## A world that is really there

> `In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.`
> *Genesis 1:1-3*

Notice the order. The world is created in verse 1. In verse 2 the earth, the deep and the waters are already there, unformed. Only then is light spoken. So Genesis 1:1 is a real beginning, and what follows is forming and filling *within* a real world.

That shapes how we read the darkness of verse 2. It is not nothing, because the earth and waters are there with it. It is not called evil. The book infers, and labels it as an inference, that this darkness is a real condition of a real world not yet made manifest.

If darkness were nothing, then whatever cannot be seen would seem less real than what can. Genesis forbids that. **Visibility is not the measure of reality.**

> `Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear.`
> *Hebrews 11:3*

## Creator and creature

The most important boundary in the book is this: God made the world, and the world is not God.

> `For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible... and he is before all things, and by him all things consist.`
> *Colossians 1:16-17*

The boundary cuts both ways:

- **Created things are not divine.** Nothing in creation, however vast or orderly, is God or part of God.
- **Created things are not self-explaining.** Nothing holds itself together by its own power. "By him all things consist."

This matters because the book will propose a deepest created order of the physical world. Such an order could easily be mistaken for something more: a cosmic mind, a world-soul or a quiet substitute for God. Scripture closes that door in advance. The substrate, if it exists, is a **creature**. It is not divine, not intelligent and not the source of its own order.

## Kinds of reality

Genesis describes distinct kinds of reality. The Hebrew verb *bara*, "create", used only with God as subject, marks three thresholds: the beginning of everything (Genesis 1:1), animal life (1:21) and humanity (1:27). Genesis 2 shows the inner structure of the last:

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

The book calls these kinds of reality **categories**: the material, the living, the personal and the spiritual. God is not a category within creation. He is its Creator.

## The same word is not the same thing

In Genesis 1:3 God creates light. Christ says, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). John writes, "God is light" (1 John 1:5). Nobody should conclude that God is made of sunlight. Yet the word is not used by accident. Light discloses and makes things visible, and Christ does something like that with the truth about God.

The two share a *form* of relation. They do not share a *nature*. The book names this distinction:

- A **structural analogy** is a likeness of form between different kinds of thing.
- **Ontological identity** would mean they are the same kind of thing.

The book uses four ways of linking categories, and says which it means each time:

| Relation | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Structural analogy** | Created light and Christ's making-manifest share a form. |
| **Representation** | Creation was designed to show something of God: "the invisible things of him... are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made" (Romans 1:20). |
| **Causal connection** | Within one person, a lie spoken with the tongue is also a wrong. The two are connected, not the same. |
| **Ontological identity** | Rejected. Different categories are never the same kind of thing. |

## What Scripture leaves open

Scripture rules out a good deal: a self-made or eternal world, and matter as evil or illusory. But it leaves much open, such as how electricity works or what the deepest structure of matter is, and it invites the search:

> `It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter.`
> *Proverbs 25:2*

## Chapter recap

- Scripture sets the boundaries; the book's proposal never borrows Scripture's authority.
- The world is real from the start; God upholds it, and it is not God. The substrate, if it exists, is a creature.
- Creation contains distinct categories, and structural analogy is not identity.
