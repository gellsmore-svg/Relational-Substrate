# Preface

A discipline can be exact in its own domain and still leave the deepest questions about reality unanswered. Physics is the clearest case. It predicts with astonishing precision, and nothing in this book disputes that. But a theory can predict well without telling us why its entities are compatible with one another at all, why the same grammar of interaction holds in every place we look, why the world is mathematically describable, how living things and persons belong to the same order, or why the weight of a promise or a betrayal is not alien to the stuff of which bodies are made. These are not questions physics has failed at. They are questions its success leaves open.

This book exists to ask them together. Its question is not whether modern science works. It does. The question is what kind of created reality can make everything we know, physical, living, personal, moral and revealed, belong coherently to one world.

The book's answer is that created physical reality is *relational* from its foundation. Things are not first made as sealed, self-contained objects that are later connected. They are constituted within an order of relation that God made and upholds. The project calls that order the Relational Substrate. Two further proposals give the answer its working shape:

- what actually happens is drawn, with real chance, from what the created order makes possible and favours;
- a difference anywhere becomes consequential somewhere else through one recurring grammar: something distinguishes, the distinction is carried by a dependency that preserves it, and another state is changed because of it.

The book calls that grammar transmit, carry, receive, and comes to treat it as a candidate form of consequential relation itself.

Scripture governs the whole. The book begins where the Bible begins, with God creating a world that is really there, and it lets the biblical distinctions between Creator and creature, dust and breath, body and person, corruption and restoration set boundaries the ontology must respect. A relation-first world does not turn morality into physics or persons into patterns. It does mean that the moral and personal life Scripture describes is not bolted onto a world that has no room for it.

## How the argument is judged

The standard is *global coherence*. A proposal about what exists must hold together across every domain it touches. It must not change the meaning of its terms as it crosses from physics to life to persons. It must not need unexplained interfaces where one kind of reality meets another. It must not break one domain to fix another. That standard runs in both directions. The book must not contradict what has been observed and measured, or what successful mathematics predicts. Equally, the picture of reality commonly carried along with physics, a world of independent objects in a container of space, is itself a proposal and is tested in the same way. Observation and measurement constrain every ontology. Familiarity, consensus and habit constrain none.

The book makes several kinds of claim and labels them. Some things are stated because Scripture states them. Some are observed and measured. Some are inferences. Some are working hypotheses of this project, and some questions are simply open. Chapter 2 sets out these levels. Appendix E gathers the book's current ontology in one place, with the status of each claim. A reader who rejects one claim can see exactly which others depend on it.

The book does not prove the Relational Substrate. No set of measurements could display the deepest structure of created reality directly. What the book does is state a hypothesis clearly enough to be wrong, show why it is believed, show what supports it and what would count against it, and follow its consequences to the end.

## About this edition

The book is written for an intelligent reader with no specialist training in physics, philosophy or theology who is willing to think carefully. Each technical term is explained where it is first needed. The opening chapter introduces one ordinary scene, seeing a stone in daylight, which returns throughout as a common reference point.

This is revision 2.5 of the second edition. The first edition rested on specific mechanisms that later work did not support. The second rebuilds the book on more foundational ideas, and Appendix B records what changed and why. The research behind the book is the work of one author, carried out with extensive computational and AI-assisted exploration. Its records are public, and Appendix B notes what they do and do not show.

A free electronic edition of this book is available, offered as an open intellectual contribution to encourage reading, sharing and honest discussion. Updated versions and related materials can be found through the project website at <https://relational-substrate.blogspot.com/> and the public research repository at <https://github.com/gellsmore-svg/Relational-Substrate>.

# Chapter 1: Scripture and the World That Is There

Begin with something ordinary. It is a clear morning, and a stone lies on the path in front of you. You see its grey surface, a vein of lighter mineral, the shadow it casts. You could pick it up, feel its weight, put it in your pocket, and find it there tomorrow, still the same stone.

Nothing could be more familiar, and almost nothing about it is simple. What is the stone, at the deepest level we can reach? What is the light that brings it to you, and how does that light become your seeing? Why is it the same stone tomorrow, when much about it and about you will have changed? Why does light behave so regularly that we can calculate exactly how it will bend through glass? And who is the one who sees, recognises, names and decides to pick it up?

The stone will return many times in what follows. It was chosen because every idea the book needs is present in the scene, not because the scene proves anything. Before any of those questions can be asked well, though, a prior decision has to be made: where does the inquiry begin?

## Scripture speaks first

This book begins with Scripture. That is a decision with consequences, and it should be made openly.

> `All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.`
> *2 Timothy 3:16-17*

Scripture claims its own authority, and this book receives that claim. Scripture is authoritative in the form it actually takes. Genesis is narrative, theological and liturgical, not a laboratory report, and it is not a hidden vocabulary for physics. Scripture does not overstep its own truthfulness. Human interpretation can overstep, on both sides. When the Bible and a scientific account seem to collide, the fault almost always lies in a human extension: a reading pressed beyond what the text says, or a metaphysical conclusion drawn beyond what the measurements show. It does not lie in revelation, or in careful measurement as such.

What Scripture-first does mean is this: Scripture sets the boundaries within which an account of reality may be built. It tells us what kind of world exists, who made it, what has gone wrong with it, and what God is doing about it. Inside those boundaries there is room for patient inquiry into how the world works. A proposal that contradicts what Scripture teaches about creation is ruled out, however elegant it is. A proposal that Scripture neither teaches nor forbids may be explored, but it must never be presented as though the Bible taught it.

That last point will govern the whole book. The Relational Substrate is a modern proposal. Scripture never mentions it. So the book keeps four relations between Scripture and its proposal carefully apart:

- *Scripture teaches* something: the world is created, held, good, and distinct from God.
- The proposal *is consistent with* what Scripture teaches.
- The proposal offers *a possible mechanism compatible with* a scriptural claim, without Scripture teaching the mechanism.
- Scripture *provokes a question* that the proposal explores.

The first sets real boundaries. The others never borrow its authority.

## A world that is really there

Genesis does not begin with an idea, a possibility or a meditation. It begins with an act.

> `In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.`
> `And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.`
> `And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.`
> *Genesis 1:1-3*

The order of the verses matters. The heaven and the earth are created in the first verse. In the second, the earth is already there, though unformed and empty. The deep is there. The waters are there. Darkness is upon the face of the deep. Only then is light spoken into being. Genesis 1:1 is therefore best read as a real beginning, the creation of a world that exists. What follows is formation, separation, filling and manifestation *within* that world, not the gradual appearance of reality out of nothing.

This has a quiet but important consequence for the darkness of verse 2. In ordinary speech, darkness can mean the mere absence of light, or something threatening, or moral evil. Later Scripture uses darkness in all those senses, and rightly. But in Genesis 1:2 darkness belongs to created reality before light is spoken. The text says only that darkness was upon the face of the deep, and that light then came. Two things are plain in the text: the darkness of verse 2 is not nothing, since the earth, the deep and the waters are already there with it, and it is not presented as evil. From the sequence the book draws an inference, and labels it as one: darkness here is a real condition of a real world, not yet manifest in the visible order that follows. Scripture elsewhere speaks of darkness as having its own created place:

> `Where is the way where light dwelleth? and as for darkness, where is the place thereof.`
> *Job 38:19*

This matters for everything that follows. If darkness were nothing, then light would be the arrival of existence itself, and whatever cannot be seen would seem less real than what can. Genesis forbids that. Visibility is not the measure of reality. The New Testament says the same thing directly:

> `Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear.`
> *Hebrews 11:3*

The visible world rests on what does not appear. That sentence will matter again when we ask what the stone is made of. Our instruments meet the world through its effects. Whatever is deepest in created order is likely to be known indirectly, through what it does, rather than displayed as one more visible object.

## The Creator and the creature

The second thing Scripture establishes is the most important boundary in the book. God made the world, and the world is not God.

> `Know ye that the LORD he is God: it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture.`
> *Psalm 100:3*

> `Who changed the truth of God into a lie, and worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator, who is blessed for ever. Amen.`
> *Romans 1:25*

The distinction cuts both ways. Created things are not divine, so nothing in creation, however vast, orderly or mysterious, may be treated as God or as part of God. And created things are not self-explaining, so nothing in creation exists by its own power or holds itself together by its own right. It is held:

> `For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him: And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.`
> *Colossians 1:16-17*

> `God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands… For in him we live, and move, and have our being.`
> *Acts 17:24, 28*

"By him all things consist" means that all things hold together in him. Creation is coherent because it is held. This does not mean God is a part of creation, or that creation is a part of God. It means creation's coherence is never self-generated. Whatever the deepest physical order turns out to be, it is upheld, moment by moment, by the One who made it.

This boundary will be tested later, because the Relational Substrate is proposed as the deepest created order of the physical world. A deepest created order can easily be mistaken for something more than created. It can be imagined as a cosmic mind, a world-soul, or a quiet substitute for God. Scripture closes that door before the proposal is even stated. The substrate, if it exists, is a creature. It is not divine essence, not a rival principle, not intelligent in itself, and not the source of its own order.

## Kinds of reality

Genesis does more than say that God made a world. It describes a world with distinct kinds of reality in it.

The opening chapter is marked by three uses of the Hebrew verb *bara*, "create", the verb used only with God as subject. It appears at the beginning of everything (Genesis 1:1). It appears again when animate creaturely life appears: "God created great whales, and every living creature that moveth" (Genesis 1:21). And it appears a third time, three times in one verse, when humanity is made:

> `So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.`
> *Genesis 1:27*

The book does not rest its argument on a single Hebrew word. But the pattern the text shows is clear. Creation is not one flat category in which everything is the same sort of thing at different sizes. There is the material world. There is living creaturely life. There is humanity, made in the image of God. Genesis 2 shows the inner structure of that last threshold:

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

Dust, breath and living soul: a body formed from the same material as the rest of creation, life imparted by God, and a living person who is more than the dust and yet not separable from it in this life. Chapter 11 will return to this verse at length. For now the point is simpler. Scripture distinguishes kinds of reality, which the book will call **categories**. The material is one category. The living is another, built on the material but not reducible to it. The personal is another. The spiritual is another. And God is not a category within creation at all, but its Creator.

Categories matter because the argument of this book has a particular risk. It will claim that physical reality is relational at its foundation, and later that relation also runs through life, persons, morality and redemption. A careless reader, or a careless author, could slide from "relation appears everywhere" to "everything is the same kind of thing". That slide would be a serious error, and Scripture itself shows why.

## The same word is not the same thing

Consider light. In Genesis 1:3 God says, "Let there be light", and there is created light. In the Gospel of John, Christ says:

> `I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life.`
> *John 8:12*

And John's first letter says:

> `This then is the message which we have heard of him, and declare unto you, that God is light, and in him is no darkness at all.`
> *1 John 1:5*

The same word is used for created light, for Christ and for God. It would be absurd to conclude that God is made of the light of Genesis 1:3, or that Christ is a kind of radiation. Yet the word is not used by accident either. Created light discloses, makes visible, brings what was hidden into view, and allows sight. These are structural features, and they are shared by what Christ does in bringing truth, life and the knowledge of God. The physical and the spiritual share a *form* of relation: one thing making another manifest. They do not share a *nature*.

This is the distinction the whole book depends on, and it deserves a name. A **structural analogy** is a likeness of form between things of different kinds. **Ontological identity** would mean they are the same kind of thing. Scripture is full of structural analogies, such as light, bread, water, seed, word and body, and they are real. Creation was made by the Word, and it bears likenesses of its Maker and of the spiritual realities it serves. But likeness of form across categories never erases the categories. When this book later shows the same relational pattern in a beam of light, in a living cell, in a conversation and in the history of redemption, it will be pointing to analogies of form, not claiming that redemption is a physical process. The reader is entitled to hold the book to that promise.

## What Scripture leaves open, and what it rules out

Scripture's boundaries are real, and they exclude a good deal. If God created the world, the world is not eternal and did not make itself. If the world is not God, it is not divine in whole or in part. If creation is good, matter is not evil or illusory. If darkness is a created condition, it is not a co-eternal rival to light. Historically, each of these exclusions answers a serious position that thoughtful people have held. They are gathered in Appendix A for readers who want to see the points of divergence. The chapters that follow do not dwell on them, because their purpose is to build rather than to refute.

At the same time, Scripture leaves a great deal open. It does not tell us how electricity works, why the speed of light has the value it does, or what the deepest structure of matter is. It tells us the world is real, ordered, intelligible, dependent and good. It tells us the world is meant to be searched out:

> `It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter.`
> *Proverbs 25:2*

Some of Scripture's language provokes questions without answering them. The prophet speaks of the One

> `that stretcheth out the heavens as a curtain, and spreadeth them out as a tent to dwell in.`
> *Isaiah 40:22*

Language like this sits uneasily with the habit of imagining space as an empty container that simply exists, and it invites the question of what extension itself is. It does not supply a geometry for the deep order of creation, and this book does not extract one from it. Scripture constrains and provokes. It is not a hidden physics textbook.

Nor is beginning here a cage on inquiry. An inquiry that treats every conceivable account of origins as equally likely has no way to focus; beginning from one Creator and one created order narrows the field to what can actually be investigated.

## The questions the book will answer

Return to the stone on the path. Scripture has told us that the stone and the light and the one who sees them all belong to a real, created, ordered, dependent world. It has told us that the world is not God, that it holds together in Christ, and that its deepest order is not necessarily visible. It has told us that creation contains distinct kinds of reality, which may share forms of relation without sharing a nature.

That is the frame. It does not yet tell us what the stone is, how the light reaches us, or why the stone will still be a stone tomorrow. Those questions belong together, because the same world answers them all, and any account of the stone must also leave room for the one who sees it and for everything Scripture says about that person. To ask them well, one more thing is needed first. We need to be clear about the difference between describing how something behaves and knowing what it is, and about how human beings come to know anything at all when the deepest things cannot simply be seen. That is the work of the next chapter.

# Chapter 2: How We Come to Know What Is There

Suppose you drop the stone from the path. It falls. Time the fall and measure the height, and you can calculate how fast it struck the ground. With Newton's law you can calculate the orbit of the Moon, and with Einstein's general relativity the corrections that keep satellite navigation accurate to within metres. Physics knows how gravity behaves with extraordinary precision.

Now ask a different question. What *is* gravity? Is it a force reaching across space, as Newton's equations seem to say? Is it the curvature of a spacetime that exists as a kind of thing in its own right? Or is spacetime itself a set of relations between events, with no existence apart from them? Physicists and philosophers who accept every one of those measurements still disagree about these questions, and nobody yet knows how gravity fits with quantum theory. The disagreement is not about whether the stone falls, or how fast. It is about what kind of reality the equations describe.

That gap, between describing how something behaves and knowing what it is, is the subject of this chapter. Seeing it clearly is the key to everything this book attempts.

## Six layers of knowing

When we say we "know" something about the stone, we can mean several different things, and they must be kept apart.

1. We have **observed** it: seen it, touched it, noticed that it falls.
2. We have **measured** it, attaching numbers by a defined procedure: its mass, its temperature, the time of its fall.
3. We have found a **mathematical relation** that predicts the measurements, such as distance fallen growing with the square of the time.
4. We have a **physical model** that organises the behaviour, such as Earth's mass pulling on the stone's mass, or the stone following a straight path through curved spacetime.
5. We hold an **ontological interpretation** of the model: what must actually be there for the behaviour to happen at all.
6. We live within a **worldview** in which that interpretation feels natural.

Each layer depends on the ones before it, but the later layers are not guaranteed by the earlier ones. An equation can predict perfectly while different pictures of the mechanism remain possible. A mechanism can be useful while the question of what fundamentally exists remains open. The history of physics shows this repeatedly. Newton's law of gravitation predicted superbly for more than two centuries, yet the picture of a force acting instantly across empty space, which Newton himself found troubling, was eventually replaced by a very different picture in general relativity. The *predictions* were largely preserved, and many of them still underpin practical calculation today. The *account of what is there* changed profoundly.

Quantum theory makes the point sharper. Its mathematical machinery is among the most precisely confirmed in science. Yet the same mathematics is read in strikingly different ways. On some readings the world is fundamentally a matter of chance at the smallest scales. On others it is entirely deterministic, with hidden variables or branching worlds. These readings disagree about what reality is while agreeing about every measurement made so far. (A further family of theories modifies the quantum dynamics itself and predicts slight differences, which experiments are now testing.) A successful description can be joined to more than one ontology.

This is simply what descriptions are. A description tells us how things behave. An ontology asks what kind of thing has to be there for that behaviour to be possible. Mathematics can describe regularity without being identical to the reality it describes, and a model can predict without settling what fundamentally exists. Confusing the layers causes trouble in both directions. When descriptive success is treated as though it had settled ontology, metaphysical assumptions enter unexamined. When the gap is used as an excuse to ignore description, the result is fantasy.

## Every account starts somewhere

If description does not settle ontology by itself, then every ontology contains something more than measurement. It contains assumptions about what kind of world could produce the measurements. That is true of materialist accounts, of religious accounts, and of the account in this book.

Here it helps to distinguish two things often run together. **Methodological naturalism** is a working rule of scientific practice: in the laboratory, explain natural phenomena by natural causes and test the explanations against observation. It is a sensible rule, and Christians practise it every day in chemistry, geology and medicine. **Metaphysical naturalism** is a claim about all of reality: nothing exists except the natural world that science studies. The second does not follow from the first. A method that looks only for natural causes cannot discover that nothing else exists, any more than a metal detector can discover that there is no wood. When the method is treated as though it had proved the metaphysics, an assumption has entered without being named.

The same discipline applies to theology. Quoting Scripture does not make an argument rigorous if the step from the text to the conclusion is left unexplained. This book begins from Scripture openly. It also tries to show, at each important point, whether a claim is something Scripture teaches, something inferred from what Scripture teaches, or a proposal that Scripture permits but does not state. Metaphysical honesty means making these assumptions and inferences visible, so a reader can see where they are and decide what to make of them.

## The status of each claim

Because of that, the book keeps track of what kind of claim it is making. It does not label every sentence, which would make it unreadable, but it uses one small, fixed vocabulary of status throughout:

| Status | Example from this book |
| --- | --- |
| Scriptural or theological constraint | God created the heaven and the earth. The substrate, if it exists, is a creature (an inference from Scripture, marked as one). |
| Empirical observation | A dropped stone falls. |
| Mathematical or physical constraint | Distance fallen grows with the square of the time. General relativity's predictions are precisely confirmed. |
| Ontological inference | Description does not by itself settle ontology. |
| Relational Substrate working hypothesis | Physical things are constituted within relations. |
| Mechanism candidate | A specific proposed structure for matter. The book names such candidates and does not rely on them. |
| Open question | How consciousness relates to physical order. Whether quantum events are fundamentally chancy. |
| Rejected | That a coherent account is thereby true. |

Two further labels say where a claim came from, not how firmly it is held. A **model result** happened in one of the project's computer models, built on stated assumptions. A model can show that an idea is possible, coherent and capable of doing work. It cannot show that nature works that way. An **exploratory** result comes from research sessions not yet published as formal records. Appendix E gives the status of every major claim in the book.

## Local, regional and global coherence

How, then, does one judge an ontology, if no measurement can display it directly? The answer this book gives is **coherence**. Freedom from contradiction is necessary but not enough: an elaborate story can avoid every contradiction while remaining a set of tidy compartments, none really connected to the others. Coherence in this book's sense also asks whether the parts are connected, and whether real distinctions survive the connecting. It comes in three degrees of scope.

**Local coherence** is achieved when a model explains a bounded phenomenon internally. An equation fits the measurements of falling stones. A theory accounts for the colours of a particular kind of glass. A model reproduces a pattern in one regime. Much of science operates, quite properly, at this level.

**Regional coherence** is achieved when several neighbouring domains integrate. Chemistry turns out to be grounded in the physics of atoms. Molecular biology joins chemistry to the life of the cell. The physiology of the nerve joins the cell to the behaviour of the organism. Regional coherence is harder to win than local coherence, and more valuable, because each domain now checks the others.

**Global coherence** is achieved when the whole account of reality remains mutually compatible. That means physical observation and mathematics, the material and the living, consciousness and personhood, morality and meaning, history and lived experience, and, for a Christian account, theology and Scripture: creation, corruption, redemption and restoration. A globally coherent worldview does not need one set of assumptions for the laboratory, another for the courtroom, another for the hospital bedside and another for prayer.

![Figure 1. Three scopes of coherence, nested: local coherence (a model explains one bounded phenomenon), within regional coherence (neighbouring domains such as physics, chemistry and biology integrate), within global coherence (the whole account of reality, from physics to Scripture, remains mutually compatible).](v2/figures/fig-coherence.png)

What makes global coherence demanding is not only that nothing may contradict anything else. Several further tests matter, and they can be stated in ordinary terms.

- **Real connection.** The domains must be joined by intelligible transitions, not merely placed side by side. It is not enough that an account says true things about physics and true things about persons. It must be possible to see how the one bears on the other.
- **Valid interfaces.** Where one kind of reality meets another, as when a physical consequence becomes a biological signal or a capacity to act becomes a moral responsibility, the meeting must be explained, not assumed. An idea crossing a boundary must keep what is relevant and must not quietly turn into something of a different kind.
- **Meaning kept through the layers.** A claim carried through several explanatory levels should still be recognisably itself at the end. If a word like "identity" or "cause" changes meaning a little at each step, the account can sound plausible paragraph by paragraph and still fail as a whole.
- **Stability under repair.** Solving a problem in one domain must not break what already held together in another.
- **The round trip.** Take a claim from one domain through others and back again, for example from bodily identity to the identity of an organism to the identity of a person and back to the body. It should return materially the same. Hidden drift shows up most clearly on the way home.
- **Accountable kinds of knowing.** Observation, mathematics, philosophical inference and revelation are different kinds of knowing. They must be kept distinct, and none may silently overrule the others.

And through all of these, the real distinctions must survive: Creator and creature, the material and the spiritual, description and ontology, probability and arbitrariness, analogy and identity, what is possible and what is right, a person and a configuration. Connection is not reduction. An account that achieves smooth connection by erasing these is not more coherent. It has simply lost something real.

The book's central methodological thesis can now be stated. **Local explanatory success is not sufficient evidence that an ontology is true if that ontology produces contradiction elsewhere.** An account of physical reality may explain falling stones perfectly. If it also makes promises, responsibility or love unintelligible, so that they must be explained away as illusions, then it has a global problem that its local success cannot cure. Conversely, an account that coheres across all these domains, while respecting every robust observation, has a strong claim on our attention even if its deepest commitments cannot be measured directly.

This is a philosophical thesis, and Chapter 9 argues for it. Most working scientists are rightly occupied with local and regional coherence. Global coherence asks what their successes jointly require.

## Coherence runs both ways

The test applies in both directions. The Relational Substrate must not contradict what has been observed and measured, or what successful mathematics predicts. Any proposal that does is finished, however elegant it is.

But the picture of reality that commonly travels with physics is also a proposal. Space is a container. The world is a collection of independent objects. Fields and particles are the furniture of reality. Laws are rules things obey. These belong to layers four to six above, not to layers one to three. They are often well motivated, and some may be right. They gain no evidential weight from being familiar, mainstream, widely published or taught in every school. Consensus is a fact about people, not a measurement of the world. So the conventional metaphysics of physics is held to the same standard of global coherence as the proposal of this book, and it receives no exemption for being physics.

This is not a rejection of modern physics. It is a refusal to confuse what was measured with a picture of what was measured.

## Human knowing is already like this

The appeal to coherence is not a special method invented for this book. It is how human beings come to know almost everything they know.

Consider a doctor diagnosing an illness. She has a set of symptoms, some test results and a patient's history, none of which is conclusive alone. She forms the diagnosis that best accounts for all of them together, and remains ready to revise it if a new result does not fit. A jury does the same with testimony and evidence. So does a historian reconstructing what happened in a century no living person saw. So, on a smaller scale, do you, when you recognise a friend's voice on a crackling telephone line, or decide that a noise downstairs is the cat. Philosophers call the pattern *inference to the best explanation*. Studies of reasoning describe how people weigh competing hypotheses by how much of the evidence each explains and how well the pieces hang together.

Perception itself may work in a related way: on one influential family of accounts, the brain continually anticipates its incoming signals and corrects its expectations against them. The models are debated, and the book does not depend on them. The modest point is enough: **human understanding normally depends on assembling the most coherent account available from incomplete evidence.** We rarely possess exhaustive, certain access to the facts. We judge, compare, anticipate, and revise. Our sense of certainty is often the felt result of a coherence we have not noticed ourselves constructing.

If that is how ordinary knowing works, then asking for the most globally coherent account of reality is not an eccentric substitute for evidence. It is the same activity, carried out at the largest scale.

## What observation decides, and what it cannot

Coherence is not proof of truth. A worldview can be internally coherent and false; a detailed and consistent story about a flat Earth is still wrong. The book says this once, here, and takes it as read from now on. Observation constrains ontology powerfully, and in three ways.

First, robust observation rules out accounts that contradict it. Any ontology of the physical world must account for the measured speed of light, the stability of atoms and the precise predictions of quantum theory, or it fails, however elegant it is. Second, observation reveals regularities that any account must respect, even when it cannot yet explain them. Third, observation can test specific proposals. If a framework predicts something definite, measurement can confirm it or reject it. Several proposals of the Relational Substrate project have been tested in exactly this way. Some survived, some failed, and the failures are recorded in the project's public research history.

What observation cannot do is display the deepest structure of created reality as one more visible object. It narrows the field and rarely, if ever, picks out a single winner. So the book does not offer an empirical proof of the Relational Substrate. It offers a **global coherence argument**, and holds itself to a plain standard. Here is what the book proposes. Here is why. Here is what supports it. Here is what would count against it. Here is what remains unknown. Then it follows the consequences. A hypothesis stated clearly enough to be wrong can be improved. One hedged until it says nothing cannot. Chapter 8 names observations that would count against particular claims.

With these tools in hand, the book can return to the stone and ask the first ontological question. The stone appears to be a separate thing, sitting on the path, distinct from the light, the air and you. Is that what it fundamentally is?

# Chapter 3: Relation Before Things

Look again at the stone and list what you know about it. It is grey. It is hard. It is heavy for its size. It is cold to the touch this morning. It has a definite shape, which casts a definite shadow.

Now notice something about each item on the list. Its greyness is how it answers daylight: which colours of light it returns and which it absorbs, as registered by an eye. In the dark it has no visible colour at all, though it remains disposed to answer light in that way. Its hardness is how it resists being pressed or scratched by other things. Its weight is the pull between it and the Earth; on the Moon the same stone would weigh about a sixth as much. Its coldness is the direction in which heat flows between it and your hand. Even its shape is the boundary at which it stops admitting things into its space and begins to resist them.

Every property on the list turns out to be a way the stone stands in relation to something else: light, pressure, the Earth, your hand, the region around it. Strip away all those relations and ask what is left. It is surprisingly hard to say.

This chapter asks whether that difficulty is telling us something about what the stone, and the physical world, fundamentally is.

## Two pictures of the world

There are two broad ways of picturing the physical world, and most people hold one of them without ever having chosen it.

On the first picture, reality is basically a collection of **separate objects**. Each object is complete in itself and possesses its own properties. The objects sit in an empty space that contains them and move through a time that flows independently of them. Relations between them, such as attraction, collision, causation or communication, are real enough, but they are secondary. They are added to objects that would exist just as they are without them. Think of billiard balls on a table: each ball is what it is, and the collisions happen to it from outside.

On the second picture, reality is fundamentally **relational**. Things are real and distinct, but they are constituted *within* relations rather than existing first and relating afterwards. What a thing is cannot be separated from how it stands to everything else. Space is an order of relations among things rather than an empty container waiting to be filled. Change is the reorganisation of relations rather than a sequence of objects being pushed around.

The first picture has been enormously useful, and in many practical situations it is perfectly adequate. Engineers build bridges with it. But it carries an assumption that is rarely stated: that the basic furniture of the world is mutually independent and self-contained, and that relation is an afterthought.

## Why the object picture strains

There are reasons to doubt that assumption at the deepest level. Two come from physics, and one comes from coherence.

The first reason is that physics has found no truly isolated thing. Everything physics describes interacts, and its properties are specified by how it interacts. An electron's charge is not a little label it carries; it is its capacity to interact in a particular way. In quantum field theory, the mathematics treats particles as excitations of fields rather than as self-standing pellets in a void. What that mathematics describes is debated, but the pellet picture no longer sits comfortably at the base.

The second reason is that the deepest successful theories describe relations more naturally than things. General relativity describes gravity in terms of the geometry relating events. Quantum theory describes systems whose joint state cannot be broken down into separate states of the parts. This is entanglement, and it has been confirmed many times. Some have drawn relational conclusions: Leibniz, that space is nothing over and above the order of relations among things; Carlo Rovelli, that quantum properties are always relative to other systems; the structural realists, that our best theories most securely describe structure, the pattern of relations. These positions are contested, and the argument does not rest on any of them. They show that the same mathematics is compatible with relation-first readings. The description underdetermines the ontology.

The third reason is the one this book cares about most, and it comes from coherence rather than from physics. If the basic furniture of reality is self-contained objects, then every relation has to come from somewhere outside the objects. Causation, influence, communication, dependence, belonging, responsibility: each has to be added on top of a world that does not, in itself, contain any of them. This becomes a serious difficulty when we reach life and persons, where relation is central. Chapter 9 will press the point in detail. Here it is enough to notice that the object picture makes relation secondary all the way down. The rest of the book tests whether that makes the world harder to understand as a whole.

## One grammar everywhere

There is a fact so ordinary that it is easy to miss. Pick up a piece of granite in Hampshire and another in Western Australia. They look alike, and that is not surprising: similar processes made them. The striking thing is something else. The two stones, formed half a world apart with no shared history that anyone arranged, are *mutually intelligible*. Bring them together and they press, scratch, warm and cool each other according to the same relations. Light from either is received by the same eye. A grain of one could be built into the crystal lattice of the other.

This holds everywhere. Independently located physical things can affect one another. Each has a receptivity compatible with what the other does. The same interaction relations hold in places that have never been in contact. Physical quantities are commensurable, so that the mass of a stone here and the mass of a star there are the same kind of thing. Matter everywhere composes into stable higher structures. Something newly encountered, a meteorite or a sample from another world, turns out to join the same causal grammar at once.

Call this **universal interoperability**. It is an observation, and an ontology has to account for it.

An ontology of wholly self-contained objects cannot take it for free. If each thing is complete in itself, nothing in its own nature guarantees that it will have an interface compatible with every other thing. Something common must supply the interface. Serious accounts supply it in serious ways: common laws of nature, common fields, shared symmetries, common forms, universal dispositions, or a common generative history. Each of these does real work, and some come close to the view of this book. But each is a common structure doing exactly the work that the objects, taken as self-contained, could not do. The question to put to every ontology, this one included, is what that common structure is and what it costs.

A relation-first ontology answers directly. Things are constituted within one created relational order, so they inherit their compatibility from it. Interoperability is not a coincidence to be explained after the objects are in place. It is what being constituted within the same relations means. An object-first ontology has to recover that common grammar somewhere else.

This is an argument from explanatory coherence, not a calculation of odds. No defensible sample space exists from which one could compute how surprising interoperability would be. Its force is comparative: one picture treats the common interface as foundational, and the other must add it.

## The claim: relation is foundational

The Relational Substrate project makes a definite proposal in response. It can be stated in one sentence. **What is most fundamental about created physical reality is relation.** Whatever appears as a stable, distinct unit, such as an atom, a stone or a star, appears as a configuration within relation, never as an item that first exists independently and then participates in relations secondarily.

This is an **ontological thesis**, the book's central one. It is not an observation, since relation as such cannot be put on a laboratory bench, and it is not a theorem. It is a claim about what kind of reality best accounts for everything else we know. It is supported by the strain in the object picture, by the underdetermination of physical description and by universal interoperability. It would be weakened by an object-first account that explained interoperability, life and personhood with equal economy and fewer unexplained interfaces. The following chapters develop it and test it.

Three clarifications prevent common misunderstandings.

First, relation-first does not deny that things are real or distinct. The stone is really there, and it really is not the path. Distinctness is real. It is simply *derivative*: the stone's distinctness is carried by a stable pattern of relations, not by some separating gap between it and everything else. A knot in a rope is a useful picture here. The knot is real: it can be pointed to, counted and untied. Yet it is not a separate object added to the rope. It is a configuration of the rope itself. The picture breaks down in one place. A rope is made of fibres that exist before any knot is tied, whereas the relational proposal imagines no pre-existing material into which relations are then tied. The picture shows only how a real, distinct thing can be a configuration rather than an addition.

Second, relation in this sense is not a mental association. When we say two things are related, we sometimes mean only that someone thinks of them together. Here relation means a real standing-between in which each relatum is conditioned by the other, whether or not anyone is thinking about it. The Earth and the stone stand in such a relation whether or not anyone weighs the stone.

Third, relation-first is not the same as "everything interacts". Two things that are already fully what they are can interact without either one's identity depending on the interaction: two billiard balls collide and remain the same balls. That is *interactional* relation, and every ontology recognises it. The Relational Substrate proposal is stronger. It holds that physical things are *constituted* within relation, so that relation partly belongs to what they are, and is not only something that happens between them.

## The relational substrate

If relation is foundational, it is natural to ask what bears it. Relations of what, in what?

The project's answer is the **relational substrate**: the created relational order within which physical things are constituted and through which they act. The word *substrate* means "what lies beneath". It names the deepest created level of physical order: what must already be there for stable things, change, influence and persistence to be real.

Because the word can mislead, it helps to say plainly what the substrate is not.

- It is not matter. Matter, as Chapter 6 will argue, is a stable form *within* the substrate, not the substrate itself.
- It is not a field of physics under another name. A field is a mathematical representation that assigns values to points. Whatever such representations describe, the substrate is a proposal about that relational order, not a rival to the mathematics.
- It is not the old luminiferous ether, a medium filling an otherwise empty space. The relational proposal does not begin with a space to be filled.
- It is not assumed to be spatial, nor assumed not to be. Its structure might correspond closely to the space we experience; space might arise from an order that is not itself spatial; or the substrate might have adjacency and ordering from which measurable space emerges. The book leaves this open.
- It is not information or code, detached from anything physical.
- It is not a hidden second world behind the visible one. It is the deep order of this world.
- It is not a mind. It thinks nothing and intends nothing.
- Above all, it is not God, and it is not a soul.

The language of a *language* can help, with its own limits. The words of a language have their meanings only within the whole order of the language: in their contrasts with other words, the grammar that admits some combinations and excludes others, and the history of use. A word torn out of its language becomes a sound. The relational proposal says something similar about physical things: they have what they are within an order of relation. The comparison fails in a way worth noticing. A language is made and sustained by speakers who mean things by it, while the substrate is not a medium of meaning and has no speakers inside it. Its order is given by its Creator, not negotiated by its parts.

## Influence through continuity

One consequence follows directly. If relation is foundational, nothing acts on anything else across a gap of non-relation. Influence is always **mediated**: a consequence depends on a relation that connects it to its source. In the manifest world mediation often looks like something travelling. The stone affects your eye through light, the Earth affects the stone through gravitation, and your hand affects the stone through the contact of surfaces. But the claim is not about travel or distance. It is that no effect is produced across literally nothing.

Physics has moved in a consonant direction, away from instantaneous action at a distance and toward influences propagated at finite speed. The relational proposal goes deeper than any particular picture of propagation. Chapter 7 develops how a distinction becomes consequential to another state, which is the heart of mediated influence.

## Created order as operating

A second term needs introducing before the next chapter. The book speaks of **created runtime order**, and the phrase needs explaining because it borrows from computing.

A computer program can be considered in two ways: as written, and as running. The second is called its "runtime". Creation can likewise be considered in two ways: in its origin, the creative act of God by which it exists at all, and in its ongoing operation, the ordinary unfolding of physical process within the order God made. *Runtime* names the second. Falling stones, travelling light, chemical reactions, the growth of crystals: these belong to created runtime order. The phrase keeps two levels apart that are often confused. Asking how the stone falls is a question about runtime order. Asking why there is a world in which stones fall at all is a question about creation.

The analogy has a sharp limit, and the reader should hold it firmly. Creation is not a computer. The world is not code, and God is not a programmer who wrote software and stepped away. A better image might be a piece of music, distinguishing its composition from its performance, except that in creation the Composer also upholds every note as it sounds. The borrowed word earns its place only by naming a real distinction, between the order as given and the order as operating. Nothing more should be read into it.

## What Scripture requires here

Scripture does not mention a relational substrate. What it does is set requirements that any account of the deep physical order must meet, and the relational proposal is shaped by them.

> `Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power…`
> *Hebrews 1:3*

> `And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.`
> *Colossians 1:17*

These texts teach that created order holds together because it is upheld by Christ. They do not say how. But they rule out any account in which the deepest physical order is self-sufficient, self-explaining or divine. The relational proposal accepts that boundary without reservation. If there is a relational substrate, it is a creature. Its coherence is received, not generated. Placing relation, rather than self-standing substance, at the base of created order fits a world whose every part depends on its Creator. Scripture does not teach this ontology. The ontology is consistent with what Scripture teaches about dependence, and bounded by it.

## From relation to possibility

The stone, then, is not a self-contained lump sitting in a void. On the proposal developed here, it is a stable configuration within a created order of relation, and its familiar properties are the ways it stands toward everything else. That raises the next question at once. If things are configurations within relation, what decides which configurations there can be? Stones exist, but not every arrangement one could imagine exists. Water freezes into ice, not into anything one likes. Something bounds what the relational order can bear. The next chapter asks what that bound is, and how anything actual comes out of what is merely possible.

# Chapter 4: What Can Be and What Comes to Be

Water, cooled enough, becomes ice. It does not become glass, or iron, or a hexagonal crystal one day and a cubic one the next at random. Under ordinary conditions it freezes into the same characteristic lattice, whose six-fold symmetry shows in every snowflake. Snowflakes differ endlessly in their details, so no two are quite alike, yet every one of them is six-sided. Something leaves room for enormous variety and at the same time draws firm limits around it.

The stone on the path is like this too. Its minerals are arranged in crystal forms that are possible for those elements and not others. Its grains could have settled in countless slightly different ways, but not in any way at all. The previous chapter proposed that physical things are configurations within a created order of relation. This chapter asks what bounds the configurations that order can bear, and how a particular configuration, this stone rather than another, comes to be actual.

## Possibility is not a blank cheque

Begin with a distinction everyone uses without naming it: the difference between what *could* happen and what *does*. A coin could land heads or tails; it lands tails. A seed could grow into an oak; this one is eaten by a squirrel. The possible is wider than the actual, and the actual is drawn from the possible.

What is less often noticed is that the possible is itself structured. It is not "anything whatever". A coin cannot land on a colour. An acorn cannot grow into a sunflower. The range of what can happen is bounded before anything happens at all. Physics describes these bounds in many ways: conservation laws that no process violates, symmetries that constrain which interactions can occur, quantum rules that permit only certain energy levels in an atom and not the values between them. The bounds are not added after the fact to tidy up the results. They are part of what the world is.

The project uses a short label, **T0**, for whatever conditions actualisation: the constraints, potential, admissibility or relational organisation according to which an actual state can come to be at all. That is the minimal commitment, and the book is confident of it. Something is logically prior to any particular happening and bounds it.

What kind of thing T0 is remains an open question. This book's working reading is **constrained possibility**: the shape of what created physical order can bear, meaning the configurations and transitions that can occur, and how they are weighted. That reading does real work in the chapters that follow. But it is one reading among several live ones. T0 might be better understood as a created potential order, as a generative architecture of constraint, or as the relational state of the substrate itself considered before a particular actualisation. Other parts of the project's work have used the label in that last sense. It might be something not yet formulated. "Possibility space" is a useful way of speaking, not a realm, a store or a second world. The book uses "constrained possibility" where that reading is meant and "T0" where only the minimal commitment is needed. Appendix E records the alternatives.

## Forbidding and favouring

There are two quite different ways a rule can shape what happens, and the difference is worth getting exactly right, because the rest of the book depends on it.

A rule can **forbid**. It can make some transition impossible, so that the door simply is not there. A rule can also **favour**. It can make some transition more or less likely than another, while leaving every option open, like a path that is well worn rather than a door that is locked. The two are easily run together, since both "shape" outcomes. But they shape outcomes in different ways.

The difference can be made exact in the simplest relational setting one can write down. Imagine a handful of points between which links form and dissolve one at a time. Left alone, every link toggles with equal chance, and the system wanders through every pattern of links. Now add rules. A rule might say "never form the link between these two points", or "when two links are present, the third is four times as likely to form". The project's constraint laboratory listed every rule of this kind, and every combination of up to three, for the smallest such worlds, and worked out *exactly*, by calculation rather than simulation, how each rule set changed the system's long-run behaviour.

The result was clean. Rules that **forbid** were the only rules that changed which patterns could be reached at all. Only they could create dead ends, change the basic rhythm of movement, or cut the space of possibilities in two. Rules that merely **favour** never did. They shifted how much time the system spent in each pattern, sometimes substantially, but every pattern reachable before stayed reachable. The same held when rules were allowed to respond to how many links were present in the whole system: every genuinely new pattern of possibility came from a forbidding rule.

These are mathematical facts about a model, not discoveries about nature. What the model exhibits is a distinction that can be named:

- **Admissibility** is whether a transition is possible at all.
- **Tendency** is how likely an admissible transition is compared with the others.

Admissibility decides the shape of the possible. Tendency weights the possible without changing its shape. Tendency is not a probability that anyone, or anything, assigns. It names how created order is disposed among the admissible options: more often this, less often that. The book proposes that the same distinction runs through created physical order: whatever conditions actualisation has both a shape and a weighting. The model shows that the distinction is sharp and fruitful. It does not show that the created world uses it.

Later work in the same models located the difference more exactly. In averages, a ban behaves like the far end of ever-stronger disfavouring. What only a ban changes is the *shape of the histories that are possible*: some changes become one-way, and some states can be regained only by first undoing something else. So a persisting absence is either slow, where return is possible but unlikely, or *gated*, where return is impossible until another relation changes. A ban on forming links never made anything permanently unrecoverable, but hard rules could leave a system where nothing at all can happen. Whether anything in created order corresponds to that total arrest is open (Appendix E.8).

One caution about the word. In this book, *admissible* means "physically possible within created order". It never means "morally permitted". Chapter 12 will distinguish physical, operational and moral admissibility explicitly. Until then the word carries only its physical sense.

## Constraint makes things possible

It is natural to think of constraint as negative: a limit, a fence, a restriction on freedom. The previous section suggests otherwise. Forbidding gives the possible its shape, and favouring gives it its character. They are not the only sources of structure. How transitions are coupled to one another, and how relations of more than two things enter in, can change what a system does even where nothing new is forbidden. Later chapters show both. But constraint, in all these forms, makes things possible rather than merely limiting them. A river is a river because of its banks. Remove them and the water does not become freer; it becomes a marsh. The rules that bound which crystal forms water can take are the reason ice exists at all, rather than a formless slush that never settles into anything.

Scripture presents created order in exactly these terms. Its account of creation is an account of division and bounding:

> `Who shut up the sea with doors, when it brake forth, as if it had issued out of the womb?… And said, Hitherto shalt thou come, but no further: and here shall thy proud waves be stayed?`
> *Job 38:8, 11*

> `Thou hast set a bound that they may not pass over; that they turn not again to cover the earth.`
> *Psalm 104:9*

> `He hath also stablished them for ever and ever: he hath made a decree which shall not pass.`
> *Psalm 148:6*

Light is divided from darkness, waters from waters, land from sea, kind from kind. Each division is a constraint, and each constraint makes room for a richer world. The theme reaches further in Scripture than physical order. Law, wisdom and discipline are presented throughout as the conditions of flourishing rather than its enemies, and Chapter 12 will return to that. Here it is enough that boundary belongs to how the world is made, not to how it is restricted.

## From the possible to the actual

Possibility, however well shaped, is not yet anything. A space of admissible configurations does not contain a single actual stone. Something must happen. Among all that can be, something comes to be.

The project calls this **actualisation**, and uses the shorthand **T1** for actual order: the configurations that have actually come to be, and the transitions actually occurring. If T0 conditions what can be, T1 is what is. What is actual is not a bare snapshot. As Chapter 6 will show, it carries its own history: what has happened leaves present relational traces.

Those traces matter for the relation between T0 and T1, which is not a one-way pipeline from a hidden store of possibilities to a finished output. Distinguish two things. There is **total admissibility**: everything the created order could ever bear. And there is what is **currently reachable**: what can actually happen next, given the present state and the history it carries. A stone that has cracked can now split along that crack. A seed that has germinated can no longer stay dormant. Actual history changes what is presently reachable without changing whatever T0 ultimately is. In that sense actuality takes part in the conditions of its own next actualisation. This is a working hypothesis of the book, and model systems show the pattern: what a system has done can change which of its futures remain open.

An analogy helps here, and it must be used with its limits stated. Think of chess. The rules of chess define which moves are legal from any position. They are the admissibility of the game, and they are strict: a bishop cannot move like a knight. Among legal moves, some are commonly preferred and some rarely played, and that is something like tendency. And there is the game actually played: this sequence of moves, on this evening, ending in this position. That is actualisation.

The analogy captures three things well. Possibility is structured but not determining, since the rules never tell you which legal move will be made. The actual is always drawn from the admissible. And the same rules allow an endless variety of actual games. But the analogy fails at one point, and the failure is the most important part of it. In chess, the moves are chosen by *players*: minds with intentions, who want to win. In the relational substrate there are no players. Nothing inside created physical order is choosing which admissible transition to make, and nothing inside it wants anything. If the analogy is pressed to supply a player, it turns the substrate into a mind. That is precisely the error Scripture forbids.

So how does one admissible transition, rather than another, come to be actual, if nobody inside the substrate selects it? That is the question of the next chapter, and the answer the project proposes is *constrained chance*. Before reaching it, one boundary has to be set, because it governs how the answer must be understood.

## Purpose shapes what is possible

If nobody inside created order chooses, is the world purposeless? Scripture says no. The world is made with purpose, and its order serves ends:

> `All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.`
> *John 1:3*

> `While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.`
> *Genesis 8:22*

> `Thus saith the LORD, which giveth the sun for a light by day, and the ordinances of the moon and of the stars for a light by night… If those ordinances depart from before me, saith the LORD, then the seed of Israel also shall cease from being a nation before me for ever.`
> *Jeremiah 31:35-36*

The order of the heavens is called *ordinances*, and God binds his own faithfulness to it. The regularity of seedtime and harvest is promised. Creation's order is purposed through and through.

The question is *where* the purpose sits. The book's answer is a theological inference:

- **Purpose shapes what is possible.** God's intention for creation is expressed in the shape of constrained possibility, in what the created order is capable of bearing and what it is not.
- **Possibility shapes what can be actualised.** Every actual configuration is drawn from that purposed space.
- **Actualisation proceeds without the substrate itself intending anything.** The substrate does not aim, want or plan. It is ordered without being an agent.

This arrangement protects two truths at once. It protects purpose: the world is not an accident, because its very possibilities were given with intent. And it protects the creatureliness of the world: physical order is lawful and real in its own right, not a puppet whose every twitch must be pulled, and not a mind in disguise. A purposive creation does not require a purposive substrate. Purpose belongs to the Creator, and the creature bears it in its form.

Two older mistakes are avoided. One is the view that God acts in every event as a fresh, bespoke intervention, so that created things have no real operations of their own; this makes nonsense of the stable regularity Scripture promises. The other is the view that God made the world and withdrew; this contradicts the continuous upholding of Colossians 1:17 and Hebrews 1:3. The position here is the mainstream Christian one: created order operates lawfully and really, under God's continual upholding, within a range of possibility that his purpose has shaped. The book calls this **non-agentic runtime**. The physical order is purposed without being purposeful in itself.

One more caution belongs here, and Chapter 14 will develop it. To say that physical order runs without the substrate intending anything is not to say that God's action is confined to the substrate's possibilities. God is not one more cause among the causes of the world. He is the Creator of all its possibilities. The book makes no claim to describe how God acts in miracles, in providence or in redemption. It describes the ordinary operation of what he has made.

## The stone, again

The stone on the path, then, is a configuration drawn from a structured space of possibility: the crystal forms its elements admit, the ways its grains could settle, the shapes erosion could leave. That space was shaped by its Creator's purpose. Within it, the stone's actual history unfolded through admissible transitions, none of which the substrate chose. Some transitions were weighted more than others, and one history among the admissible ones became actual, with no hidden chooser selecting it. How that can be is the subject of the next chapter, which turns to a word that is very easily misunderstood: chance.

# Chapter 5: Constrained Chance

Take a lump of carbon from an old campfire and consider a single atom of radioactive carbon-14 inside it. When will that atom decay? On the standard understanding of quantum physics, nobody can say, and not because we lack information. (Some interpretations dispute this; the chapter returns to them below.) The atom has no internal clock counting down. It might decay in the next second or in fifty thousand years. Yet in a sample of many such atoms, half will have decayed in about 5,730 years, with a precision that lets archaeologists date the campfire. The individual event is open. The collective behaviour is as reliable as a law.

This pairing, open in the particular and exact in the aggregate, is one of the most important facts in this book. It also requires one of the most easily misunderstood words in the English language. This chapter is about chance: what it is, what it is not, and what the Relational Substrate project proposes about it.

## Three meanings of "random"

When people say that something happened "at random", they can mean at least three different things.

They might mean that it was **lawless or arbitrary**: anything could have happened, with no pattern and no reason. They might mean that it was **unpredictable to us**: there was a cause, but we did not know enough to foresee the result. Or they might mean that it was **probabilistic**: governed by definite chances over a definite set of possibilities, so that individual outcomes are open while their frequencies are fixed.

The third meaning is the one that matters here, and it is precisely not the first. A fair die is the simplest example. Each throw is open: any face can come up. But it cannot come up seven, and over thousands of throws each face appears very close to one time in six. The die is not lawless. It is governed by a probability distribution, and the distribution is exact.

To keep this meaning clear, the book uses the word **stochastic**, from a Greek word for aiming at a target. A stochastic process is one whose outcomes are governed by probabilities over defined possibilities. Stochastic does not mean lawless. It does not mean that anything at all can happen. And it does not mean chaotic, which is a different idea again. A *chaotic* system, in the technical sense, is fully deterministic: each state follows from the last by fixed rules, but the system is so sensitive to its starting point that tiny differences grow into large ones, as in the weather. Chaos makes the future hard to predict even though nothing in it is chancy. A stochastic description makes the future a matter of definite probabilities, without being lawless.

Notice what the word does not yet say. Calling a process stochastic says how it is *described*: by probabilities over defined possibilities. It does not by itself say why. The probabilities might express our ignorance of hidden details in a process that is deterministic underneath. Or they might express genuine openness in the world itself, so that the outcome is not fixed until it occurs. The first is called *epistemic* probability, the second *ontic* indeterminacy. The same stochastic description fits both.

## Chance in physics

Radioactive decay is not an isolated curiosity, and the layers of Chapter 2 sort it cleanly. *Observation and measurement:* repeated quantum measurements produce outcomes whose frequencies are stable and exact. *Mathematical relation:* quantum theory gives its predictions as probabilities. It does not say where an electron will be found, only how likely each place is.

*Interpretation:* whether nature itself is fundamentally chancy is a different question, and a contested one. As Chapter 2 noted, some interpretations of quantum theory read the probabilities as genuine openness in the world, and others as ignorance of a reality that is fully determined, while all agree about every measurement made so far. The probabilities are established. Whether nature is fundamentally indeterministic is not.

## The proposal: constrained stochastic actualisation

Recall the question left by the previous chapter: if nothing inside the substrate chooses which admissible transition becomes actual, what makes one rather than another actual? This book proposes that **actualisation is fundamentally stochastic**, in the ontic sense. Among the transitions currently reachable, which one occurs is genuinely open until it occurs, a matter of chance weighted by tendency. The future is really open within its conditions. That openness is the book's claim, not part of the meaning of the word "stochastic".

The word *constrained* carries most of the weight in that sentence. **Unconstrained** stochasticity, where every conceivable outcome is equally open, would produce a world without stable form: a static of meaningless flicker. **Constrained** stochasticity is chance operating inside the shape of the possible. Admissibility rules out whole regions of possibility entirely. Tendency weights what remains. And, as the next chapters will show, what has already happened can change what is admissible or likely next. Chance, on this proposal, never acts on an open field. It acts within a structure that was there before it.

**Status:** an RS working hypothesis. It fits the probabilistic character of quantum theory without following from it, since deterministic interpretations remain viable and agree with every measurement so far. A demonstration that the probabilities are wholly epistemic, reflecting only ignorance of a deterministic process beneath, would count against it. Its positive support is coherence. It explains how a non-agentic creation can actualise without a hidden chooser, and it fits the observed pattern of openness in the particular with exactness in the aggregate. Stable, exact behaviour does not require that every underlying event be determined in advance. The carbon-14 atoms show this in nature. A simple model shows it vividly.

## An illustration: exact answers from chance

One of the project's computer models makes the point concrete. It is an illustration, not evidence about nature. It is a calculator that does arithmetic without calculating. To add 17 and 28, it builds two small populations of relations, one standing for each number, each relation carrying a positive or negative orientation. It pours them together and lets them change at random under a few local rules: relations may be re-attached, opposite pairs may cancel, and neutral pairs may appear or vanish without changing the total. When nothing further can cancel, the surviving relations are counted. From one identical starting arrangement, thousands of runs followed thousands of different paths, no two alike, and every one arrived at the correct answer.

**An exact, stable outcome can coexist with a great diversity of chance-driven paths.** The limits of the illustration matter as much as its point. The exactness did not come from chance. It came from rules built in to conserve the total. Chance supplied the variety of paths, and the constraint supplied the identity of the result. A fixed order of steps would have been exact too, and ordinary computer science explains everything the model did. What it shows is how chance and exactness relate, not that nature computes this way.

A second lesson came from weakening the rules. When a forbidden change, the outright deletion of a relation, was occasionally let through, accuracy fell smoothly and predictably, and it fell further the longer the system ran. Stability is always stability *of something*, *against some kind of disturbance*, *over some length of time*. When later chapters speak of things persisting, those three qualifiers stay in view. Appendix B records the details.

## Chance and providence

A Christian reader may feel uneasy at this point. If actualisation is at bottom a matter of chance, is the world slipping out of God's hands? Scripture speaks to this directly, and its answer is unexpectedly calm:

> `The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.`
> *Proverbs 16:33*

The casting of lots was the ancient world's paradigm of a chance event, and Israel used it in matters as weighty as the division of the land. Scripture does not deny that the lot falls by chance, as far as anything within the lap and the hand can tell. It says that the whole disposing of it belongs to the Lord. Chance is a real feature of how created events unfold, and it is not outside God's governance. Elsewhere the Preacher observes plainly that "time and chance happeneth to them all" (Ecclesiastes 9:11), and the observation is not treated as a threat to God's rule.

The book draws a theological inference here, and it marks it as one. If created actualisation is constrained-stochastic, then chance is a *created mode of actualisation*. It is part of how a non-agentic creation unfolds within possibilities God has shaped. It is not a rival power, and it is not God's uncertainty. God is not waiting to see how the dice fall. He is the Creator of the dice, of the table, of the probabilities, and of the purposes the whole serves. How divine sovereignty relates in detail to created chance is a question Christian thinkers have pondered for centuries, and this book leaves it where they have. What it affirms is that constrained chance does not compete with the God of Proverbs 16:33.

## What chance leaves unexplained

The stone on the path has a history in which chance played a part: the settling of grains, the paths of eroding water, the cracks that happened to open. Its present form is one actual history among many admissible ones. Yet it is not a flicker of static. It is a stone, and it will be the same stone tomorrow. If the history beneath it is open at every step, what keeps it the same? The illustration gave a hint: the result survived every path because something was preserved by every allowed change. The next chapter asks what such preservation can explain about sameness through change, and where it stops short of being the same individual.

# Chapter 6: Identity Through Change

The ancient Greek thinker Heraclitus is remembered for saying that you cannot step into the same river twice. The water you stepped into is gone downstream, and new water has taken its place. Yet everyone who lives beside a river calls it by one name and knows it is the same river this year as last. A candle flame is the same: the burning gas is replaced from moment to moment, while the flame stays steady and recognisable. Your own body is similar. Over the years much of its material is replaced, yet you are not a different person from the child in your old photographs.

What, then, makes a thing the same thing through change? The old answer, rarely stated but deeply assumed, is that a thing stays the same because its stuff stays the same: some core of unchanging material carried along through time. The river, the flame and the body show that this cannot be right in general. Their stuff changes and they persist. The previous chapter added a sharper puzzle. If what happens at the deepest level is constrained chance, so that the history beneath every thing is open at each step, what keeps the stone on the path a stone?

## What every allowed change preserves

A first answer is that a thing stays the same because every change available to it preserves something. In the illustration of Chapter 5, the relations changed constantly and no two histories were alike, yet the *total* never moved. The number did not live in any particular relation or arrangement. It lived in what every allowed change preserved.

In mathematical language, such a preserved quantity is an **invariant**: something a set of transformations leaves unchanged. Physics describes the world this way at its foundations. The great conservation laws, of energy, momentum and electric charge, say that certain quantities are left unchanged by every physical process. Early in the twentieth century the mathematician Emmy Noether proved that such conservation laws are tied to symmetries: each conserved quantity corresponds to a way the laws of physics stay the same under some transformation. Physicists classify particles by such invariants. An electron is identified by properties that every electron shares, and that no allowed interaction alters without turning it into something else. It is not identified by being a particular bit of stuff.

There is also a more visual kind of invariant. Tie a knot in a loop of string, then pull, twist and squash the loop without cutting it. The knot can be made to look very different, but it cannot be undone. The *kind* of knot is invariant under every deformation that does not cut the string, and mathematicians measure it with whole numbers that can only stay the same or jump. The knot illustrates how identity can be carried by organisation rather than by stuff. It is not a claim about what particles are.

So the first lesson is that things persist through change because the changes available to them leave something invariant. That lesson is sound. But it is not the whole story, and the rest of the chapter is about what it leaves out.

## The same in what respect?

Imagine two coins in your palm. They are the same size, the same weight, stamped with the same design. For every purpose you have this morning they are the same. Then one is passed to a bank, tested, and found to be a counterfeit. The difference was there all along, in its metal and its making. Nothing you did with it this morning could find it. A bank's test could.

Or imagine two keys, cut by eye to look alike. Laid side by side they are indistinguishable. Try them in the lock, and one turns and the other does not.

These cases show something an invariant alone does not capture. Whether two things are "the same" always depends on *what could tell them apart*. Sameness of one visible summary, such as size, weight or design, is not sameness in every respect that might later matter. What decides whether a difference is real and relevant is whether some admissible future interaction could detect it: the bank's test, or the lock.

The same thing appears, exactly, in the simplest relational models. Rule systems sorted into one family by their coarse behaviour turned out, when examined exactly, to be many distinct systems, some of them sharing every recorded summary while differing in how unpredictably they moved. Observable equality is not equality of the underlying law. A description can map many different underlying things onto one visible picture, and the differences it hides can still matter for what those things do next.

![Figure 3. Same appearance, different identity. Two states that look identical under one description can differ in a hidden relational detail. If some reachable future interaction draws on that detail, the two states behave differently. They were never fully the same. If nothing reachable ever draws on it, treating them as one loses nothing.](v2/figures/fig-identity.png)

That is the problem of **projection**, or coarse-graining. Every description summarises. Summaries are indispensable, and science could not proceed without them. But a summary can hide distinctions, and whether the hidden distinctions matter depends on whether anything that can actually happen will draw on them.

The principle can be stated simply. **A distinction can safely be ignored only where no reachable future consequence can draw on it.** If nothing that can happen will ever tell two states apart, treating them as one loses nothing. If something can, they were never fully the same.

## Identity as relational equivalence

With these cases in hand, the account of identity can be stated more carefully.

**A thing continues to be the same kind of thing, in the respect that matters, when the changes it undergoes preserve its relevant equivalence class: the set of states that no admissible future interaction, of the kind that matters for that thing, could tell apart from it.**

"Equivalence class" is a technical phrase for a plain idea. It means all the states that count as "the same" for a given purpose. The coins were in the same class for shopping this morning and in different classes for a bank.

An invariant, then, is one way of *carrying* or *certifying* such a class. A conserved total and a knot's whole number are excellent certificates. They mark, in a single quantity, what every admissible change preserves. But an invariant is not necessarily the whole of identity. Two states can share every invariant one has thought to measure and still differ in ways a later process will draw on, as the counterfeit coin shows.

This is not a slide into "sameness is in the eye of the beholder". The criterion is not what a human happens to notice or measure. It is what admissible interactions *within created order* could discriminate, whether or not anyone is watching. The counterfeit was different whether or not it was ever tested. Sameness of this kind is relational, because it concerns what can distinguish one state from another. It is not arbitrary.

## Two questions about sameness

Everything so far answers one question: *what kind of thing, or what state, is this*, judged by what any reachable future interaction could tell apart? There is a second question, and it must not be folded into the first: *is this the same individual*? That is, is it the same continuing thing, and not merely an equivalent replacement?

The two can come apart. Suppose a thing is destroyed, and later something is built that no defined future interaction could distinguish from it: same structure, same behaviour, same invariants. The new thing belongs to the same equivalence class as the old. That does not settle whether it *is* the old thing. Two coins struck from the same die can be indistinguishable in every test and still be two coins. A second coin minted to replace a lost one is not the lost coin found again.

What answers the second question is not equivalence alone but continuity: an unbroken lineage, a history carried forward, the provenance described below. A system can rebuild structure that works, and even match a lost organisation, without recovering the particular lineage that was lost. The project's exploratory models met exactly that. Four achievements need to be kept apart:

- restoring *function*, so that the thing does the job again;
- restoring *organisation*, so that it has the same pattern again;
- restoring the *equivalence class*, so that nothing reachable could tell it from the original;
- restoring the *same individual*, the same lineage carried on.

The first three can each happen without the fourth. What carries individual continuity in physical things is an open question. For the rest of this chapter, *identity* means the first kind of sameness unless the second is named, and *individual continuity* means the second.

## Provenance: history in the present

There is a further consequence. Sometimes what distinguishes two present states is how they came to be. A coin and its counterfeit may differ in nothing but their making. A bent and straightened paper clip looks like a new one but is weaker, because its bending is still present in its crystal structure. A healed bone looks whole but carries the remodelling of its fracture.

The name for this is **provenance**: past distinctions that survive in present relational structure. Provenance is not a separate record kept somewhere else, a ledger attached to the thing. It is embodied in the thing's present configuration: in residual stresses and orientations, in correlations among its parts, in what is accessible to it now and what is not. And it belongs to the thing's identity exactly where it remains consequential, where some future interaction could draw on it. Where nothing could, it can be set aside.

This matters for how the book understands what is actual. Chapter 4 called the actual order of things **actuality** (T1): what has come to be from among what was possible. Actuality is not a succession of memoryless snapshots, each owing nothing to the last. A present state can carry the relational consequences of its own past: its provenance, its accumulated constraints, what has become accessible to it and what has been closed off. What happens next depends on all of that, not merely on how things look at this instant.

## Persistence

**Persistence** can now be described more fully. It is the continued re-actualisation of a thing within its relevant equivalence class, and, for a continuing individual, along an unbroken history. The river persists because, moment by moment, flowing water keeps re-establishing a river-shaped pattern within its banks, carrying with it its channel's history. The flame persists because combustion keeps re-establishing a flame-shaped region of light and heat. On the relational proposal the stone persists in the same way, though far more slowly: its crystal lattices and the bonds that hold its grains keep re-establishing themselves through the small, ceaseless fluctuations of its atoms.

Chapter 5's three qualifiers apply: stability is of something, against some disturbance, for some time. The stone persists against rain for centuries and against a hammer for a moment. No created thing is stable against everything forever. To say that something persists is to say that the changes it actually undergoes stay within the class that preserves what it is.

The project's later models added a discipline to the word. *What is likely* and *what lasts* are different questions: two model systems agreeing on how often each state occurs, how dense and how active they are, differed more than twentyfold in how long their patterns survived. So a claim of persistence must say what it was compared with, must separate *reaching* a state from *surviving* in it, and should show that a structure outlasts its own parts before treating it as a point of reference (Appendix E.8).

## Preserving is not repairing

Preservation has a firm boundary. When a relation in the Chapter 5 model was destroyed rather than rearranged, the system never recovered the original number. It settled cleanly, and confidently, on a different one.

An equivalence class survives every change that keeps a thing inside it. It does not survive changes that move the thing outside it, and conservation alone does not restore what is lost. Identity, on this account, is robust but not indestructible. And as the previous section showed, even rebuilding something into the same equivalence class would not by itself restore the same individual. Where lineage was recovered in the project's explorations, it sometimes depended on help from the surrounding context, and sometimes it was not recovered at all. If anything is to be recovered after damage, the distinctions needed for recovery must survive somewhere: in redundancy, in a template, in a wider order, or in an agent able to restore them. The point will matter later. Living things repair themselves because they carry such surviving constraints (Chapter 10). Restoration in the theological sense is not the world's invariants reasserting themselves unaided (Chapter 14).

## Memory without a memory substance

Provenance also gives the clearest sense of what memory is at the physical level. Bend a paper clip back and forth and it does not return to its first condition; eventually it breaks. A piece of iron, magnetised and released, stays magnetised. Its present state depends on its history, not only on its present surroundings. Physicists call this **hysteresis**, and it is well understood in many materials. That much is mainstream science.

Three further pieces of support, of different strengths, point the same way.

- **Directional evidence.** In published metal-fatigue data, the *order* of loads matters: gentle cycles first, then harsh, leaves a part more durable than the reverse at equal totals, though the simplest standard model says order makes no difference. The same direction appears in three unrelated domains. The project's model predicted the size of the effect badly, so this is a directional finding only.
- **Memory without a history variable.** When a genuinely three-way relation in a small relational model was hidden from view, the visible two-way links behaved as though they remembered their recent past, although nothing in the rules recorded history. Apparent memory can come from present structure that a description leaves out (Chapter 8).
- **History as present state.** In later models, one visible arrangement of links faced different futures depending on how it had come about. Counting the carried history as part of the present state made the future depend on the present alone again. That history stayed predictive after exact details were forgotten, and a coarse description could hide it entirely. These are model results, not a law of memory in nature.

The book's proposal unites these. **Memory, at the physical level, is past distinctions embodied in present relations so that they alter what is currently reachable, how likely it is, or how a thing responds.** No separate memory substance is needed. The bent paper clip remembers its bending in its bent crystal structure and nowhere else. **Status:** RS working hypothesis. It will be useful later, when the book speaks of habits, of character, and of the slow hardening Scripture describes (Hebrews 3:13), always as a structural analogy and never as an explanation of moral life.

## Matter as stable form

We can now say what the project proposes the stone *is*. On the relational proposal, **matter is stable form within the relational substrate**: configurations whose relevant equivalence classes are preserved by the changes they ordinarily undergo, so that they persist, can be encountered, and act on other things in consistent ways. Atoms, on this view, are very stable relational forms rather than tiny self-existing pellets. Molecules are larger stable forms built of coupled atomic forms. The stone is an immense, coherently coupled configuration that has stayed stable long enough to lie on a path and be picked up.

This does not make matter less real. It makes its reality a matter of form held stable rather than of brute stuff. Mainstream physics already undermines the picture of mass as simply the sum of tiny lumps. Most of the mass of ordinary matter arises from the energy of the interactions that bind quarks inside protons and neutrons, not from the masses of the quarks themselves. Inertial mass is measured as resistance to acceleration. The Relational Substrate reading is that what we measure as matter's properties are, all the way down, properties of stable relational form. **Status:** ontological interpretation.

Scripture speaks of creaturely form as given and shaped:

> `But God giveth it a body as it hath pleased him, and to every seed his own body.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:38*

> `For ever, O LORD, thy word is settled in heaven. Thy faithfulness is unto all generations: thou hast established the earth, and it abideth. They continue this day according to thine ordinances: for all are thy servants.`
> *Psalm 119:89-91*

The earth "abideth", it persists, and it does so "according to thine ordinances". Created persistence is real and reliable, and it is held.

## Two kinds of sameness

The psalms also guard against a confusion. Scripture speaks of another kind of sameness, of a different order entirely:

> `They shall perish, but thou shalt endure: yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment; as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they shall be changed: But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end.`
> *Psalm 102:26-27*

Created things persist by remaining within an equivalence class through change, and they can wear out like a garment. God does not persist in this way at all. His sameness is not the preservation of anything through admissible transitions, because God is not a configuration, does not undergo transitions, and has no admissibility imposed on him from beyond. The word "same" is used of both, truly, but across the deepest category boundary there is. Creaturely identity is received and sustained. God's is underived.

The same caution applies, at a different boundary, to human persons. Persons have bodies, and bodies persist in the way described here. But whether a *person's* identity is simply an equivalence class of physical configuration is a different question, and the book's answer, given in Chapters 11 and 14, is no. Personal identity resembles both kinds of sameness described here, and the resemblance helps. It is not the same thing.

## A word about time

If change is the actualisation of admissible transitions, then time, on the relational proposal, is the **ordering of actualisation**: the before-and-after of change itself, rather than an independent container that would still be ticking if nothing happened. This is an old view, held in different forms by Leibniz and others, and it is an ontological interpretation. It must sit with what is measured: clocks run at different rates at different heights and speeds, exactly as general relativity predicts. General relativity's mathematics treats time geometrically, as part of spacetime. Whether spacetime is a substance or an ordering of relations among events is a question of interpretation, not of measurement. The book's reading is that clocks are physical processes, and the time they keep is the ordering of the changes they undergo.

Ordering is not yet *direction*. The book calls a settled, directed order of change **succession**: this, then that, and not the reverse. In the project's simplest models succession does not come free. Rules that merely make some states more likely give preferred states but no direction. Direction needs a tendency that favours going round a cycle of changes one way, or a rule that makes some change one-way. Its characteristic pattern is a handover: a link forms, and the link that supported it dissolves. This is not a claim about the arrow of time in nature, and no model claim about time is free of the modeller's clock: counting steps and measuring continuous time can disagree (Appendix E.8).

## The stone, made manifest

The stone is a stable, history-bearing configuration. It is also *manifest*: it presents itself to other things, scattering light, resisting pressure, making itself detectable and encounterable. Manifestation in this sense belongs to physical order. It is something a stone does whether or not anyone is looking. It is not the same as anyone's *awareness* of the stone, which belongs to the category of persons, as Chapter 11 will insist. For the stone to make any difference to you, some distinction in it must become consequential to you. In the daylight scene, light is how that happens. What is required for any distinction to become consequential to another state is the subject of the next chapter.

# Chapter 7: Transmit, Carry, Receive

Return to the path in the morning light. Sunlight falls on the stone. Its grey surface returns some of that light and absorbs the rest, and its lighter vein returns a little more. Some of the returned light reaches your eye and is absorbed in the cells at the back of it. Molecules change, then cells, then nerves, and in the end you see a grey stone with a pale vein, there on the path, and you know it is there.

Something about the stone made a difference in you. The stone did not move. Nothing from its surface flew into your head. Yet the distinction between its grey body and its pale vein became a distinction in what you see.

This chapter asks what is required for that to happen, and its answer reaches far beyond light. The project names the answer **Transmit, Carry, Receive**, often written **T-C-R**. This book proposes that T-C-R is the grammar of consequential manifestation: the logical structure by which any distinction in created order becomes consequential to another state. The seeing of the stone is one instance. The chapter builds the grammar from that instance, then states the stronger claim.

## The grammar in its minimal form

Stated as simply as possible, the grammar is:

```text
distinction → preserved dependency → consequence
```

Each part is a role, not a component.

- **Transmit.** A distinction in one relational condition is capable of constraining something other than itself.
- **Carry.** The dependency between that distinction and its consequence preserves enough of the distinction for the consequence to depend on it. The carrier need not be a separate object. At the most primitive level, the relation itself may be the carrier.
- **Receive.** Another state becomes actual, or the range of what it can actualise is constrained, because of the source distinction. Reception is consequence, not arrival.

There is a simple test for whether a distinction has been carried. If two different source states could never make any difference to any consequence, the distinction between them has not been carried, however much else has happened.

In this minimal form the grammar presupposes very little. It does not presuppose that anything travels, or that there is a measurable space for it to travel through, or that some intermediate object passes from one place to another. It does not presuppose encoding and decoding, or a stack of hidden and visible layers. The source need not be used up. Reception need not happen exactly once. Each of these does occur in particular cases, and the cases below show several of them. None belongs to the grammar itself.

## Step one: a distinction at the source

What reaches you from the stone? The natural answer is "light", and that is true as far as it goes. But light falls on everything on the path, and you do not see everything as the same. What reaches you is the *distinction* the stone makes in the light: the pattern by which its grey body returns light differently from its pale vein, and both differently from the soil around them. If the stone were exactly like its surroundings in every way light can register, you would not see it at all, however much light reached your eye.

So T-C-R concerns a **source distinction**: a distinguishable state or relation that could make a difference to something else. A dark mark on white paper is one. So is the warmth of a stove compared with a cold room, the pitch of a struck bell, a name known to one person, or the sequence of bases in a strand of DNA. Distinctions are everywhere. Most make no difference beyond themselves. The question is how some of them come to matter.

## Step two: transmission, or the capacity to constrain

For a distinction to matter beyond itself, it must be capable of constraining something other than itself. That is what **transmit** means at the most general level.

How this happens varies enormously, and the variety matters. Sometimes the source is changed by transmitting. A struck bell rings more quietly with every second, because the energy of the strike is leaving it as sound. An atom that emits light drops to a lower energy state, and the light carries away exactly the energy the atom lost. In these cases transmission really does cost the source something.

But that is a subcase, not the definition. When you tell someone your name, you do not forget it. A lighthouse makes its distinction (this light, at this place, flashing in this rhythm) available to every ship within range, night after night, without becoming any less a lighthouse. A cell's DNA takes part, again and again, in shaping the proteins the cell makes, without losing its sequence. Transmission may involve emission, copying, exposure, the modulation of something already in motion, or simply standing in a relation on which something else depends. Whether the source is depleted depends on the case.

Not every distinction can be transmitted, and not by every route. An atom can emit light only at the particular energies its structure allows, which is why each element has its own fingerprint of colours. A bell can ring only at the pitches its shape admits. Transmission is governed by admissibility (Chapter 4). A distinction can constrain something else only in the ways the created order makes available.

## Step three: carriage

In the daylight scene there is a gap between the stone and your eye. What preserves the stone's distinction across it?

It is tempting to imagine a little object crossing the gap, a pellet carrying the stone's greyness. Physics describes light as coming in discrete packets, called photons, but what a photon is between emission and absorption is one of the deep questions of quantum theory. In any case, the pellet picture misses what matters. Some simpler cases make it clearer.

Drop a pebble into a still pond, and a ring of waves spreads to the bank. The water at the bank rises and falls because of the pebble, but no water travelled from the centre. Each small parcel moved only a little. What crossed the pond was a *pattern*, borne by a succession of different water, none of which made the journey.

Or consider the chain of signal fires that ancient peoples used to send news across a country. A fire is lit on one hill. On the next, a watcher sees it and lights a second fire, and so on for a hundred miles. No fire travelled. Each was a different fire, burning different wood. Yet the news arrived, because each new fire was lit *in dependence on* the one before.

Or consider a telephone call. Your voice, a pattern in air pressure, becomes a pattern in an electrical current, then in radio waves, then in pulses of light in a glass fibre, then in current again, then in air pressure at your friend's ear. The physical vehicle changed completely, several times. Your friend still hears what you said.

In each case, what must persist is not a particular material thing. It is **the relevant distinction, preserved by a chain of dependency**. At each stage, the carrier's state still depends on the source's state closely enough that the receiver, at the end, can be changed in a way that depends on what was at the source. In these manifest cases the carrier may be an object, a wave, a chain of local interactions, a record or a changing medium. *Carrier* is the name for whatever bears the dependency at a given stage, and carriers can be substituted. A carrier can preserve consequence without preserving its implementation. That is why a conversation survives being routed through copper, radio and glass.

These are cases where carriers are easy to see. At the deepest level the book proposes something simpler. Where two relational conditions are constitutively related, the relation between them is the dependency. Nothing further needs to cross between them. The carrier is the relation itself.

This is where Chapter 6's account of identity does real work. What a carrier must preserve is not everything about the source, and not merely some summary number. It must preserve the source distinction's **relevant equivalence class**: whatever the receiver's possible responses can tell apart. A telephone line can drop the fine texture of a voice and still carry the words, because nothing your friend does next depends on that texture. It cannot drop the difference between "yes" and "no".

**Provenance** can matter here too. A letter's words may arrive intact while the question of who sent it is lost. If the receiver will act differently depending on the sender, as a bank does with a signature or a court with a chain of custody, then the provenance is part of what must be carried. If nothing downstream depends on it, it can be dropped without loss.

Carriage is also **constrained**. Dependency can degrade. Ripples spread and fade. A watcher on a hill falls asleep. A telephone line hisses with noise. Carriage holds only along the dependencies created order supports, and only against disturbances they can absorb. Like every kind of persistence in Chapter 6, it is persistence of something, against some kind of disturbance, over some length of time.

## Step four: reception

Light from the stone reaches many things besides your eye. It strikes the window of a passing car, the leaves of a hedge, the grass. Some of it passes through the car window almost untouched. Did the window "receive" the stone?

In one sense, yes: the light arrived there. But nothing at the window was changed in a way that depended on the stone's particular pattern. The window would have been in the same state if the stone had been a different colour. In the sense that matters for this grammar, the window did not receive the distinction. It was passed through.

**Reception** is not arrival. It is **consequence**: another state changed, or constrained, in a way that depends on the source distinction. At the back of your eye, light is absorbed by a molecule that changes shape when it absorbs it. That change starts a cascade that alters cells and nerves. The distinction between grey and pale has become a distinction in you.

A letter makes the point in human terms. A letter delivered and left unopened on the mat has *arrived*. A letter opened, read and acted on has been *received*. The words are the same in both cases. What differs is whether the distinction they carry has made a difference in someone.

What a receiver can receive, and how, is set by the receiver itself. This is **receiver compatibility**, and it has two sides.

First, every receiver has a range of distinctions it can register. Your eye responds to visible light, not to the radio waves passing through you at this moment. That is not because radio waves fail to arrive. The receiving molecules simply admit only certain changes. Reception, like transmission, is governed by admissibility, now on the receiving side.

Second, receivers differ in how they respond to repeated or continuing arrivals:

- A thermostat receives continuously, responding to the temperature as it changes.
- An ear integrates many arrivals into one sound.
- A light switch already "on" is unchanged by being switched on again; the repeat is harmless.
- A choir rehearsing a phrase is changed more by each repetition.
- A bank ledger must record each payment exactly once. Record it twice and the account is wrong.

Only the last kind of receiver needs exactly-once reception. For a receiver whose consequence is a count, duplicated reception is error. That is a property of transactional receivers, not a universal law of reception. What is universal is that reception has a definite character. How a receiver responds to what reaches it is part of what that receiver is.

A distinction may be transmitted, and even carried, without ever reaching consequential reception: light from a distant star that falls on nothing able to register it, or a letter lost in the post. The propagation is real. The T-C-R chain is simply incomplete. A distinction becomes *consequential* only when it is received.

## The whole grammar

In the manifest world each part usually has a visible vehicle: something emitted, a chain of carriers, a receiver changed by what arrives.

![Figure 4. Transmit, Carry, Receive, as it appears in manifest cases. A distinction at the source becomes capable of constraining something beyond itself; a chain of dependency preserves the relevant distinction across substitutable carriers; a compatible receiver is changed in a way that depends on it. The dependency persists while the vehicles change. At substrate depth the carrier may simply be the relation.](v2/figures/fig-tcr.png)

Read the morning scene again with this in hand. Sunlight is emitted by the Sun, which is changed by emitting it, and received by the stone, which is warmed. The stone's interaction with that light makes its own distinction transmissible: the light it returns is now patterned by the difference between grey body and pale vein. That pattern is carried to your eye by a succession of electromagnetic disturbances, none of which is "the stone". At your eye it is received, as a definite change in molecules able to admit it. That change is then transmitted to cells and nerves. In the end, at a level the coming chapters approach carefully, it reaches *you*, as the seeing of a stone.

The chain shows three further features of T-C-R.

It **nests**. Within one act of seeing there are transmissions inside transmissions. The nerve impulse from eye to brain is its own T-C-R: a pulse of electrical change in one segment of a nerve fibre, carried by charged particles that each cross only a tiny distance of membrane, received by the next segment, which passes it on. The impulse travels the length of the nerve. No particle does.

It **composes**. Many transmissions run in parallel: light from every point of the stone reaching every part of the retina at once, together forming one image. Others run in sequence: Sun to stone, stone to eye, eye to brain. You may then *tell* someone what you saw, and the scene has become part of a conversation.

And multiple chains are **coordinated**. Your two eyes receive slightly different images, and depth is seen in how they are related, not in either alone. Your ears receive sound at slightly different times, and direction is heard in the difference. Chapter 8 will show why relations among many chancy events can matter more than the events taken one by one.

## A grammar that recurs

The same three-part form turns up wherever one thing is consequential to another: in light and sound, in the copying of DNA, in nerves, in speech, in law and in liturgy. One explanation is coincidence, many different mechanisms happening to share an engineering pattern. This book proposes a deeper one.

If A and B are genuinely related in a consequential sense, something about A must be capable of making a difference to B. That difference must be preserved well enough for B to depend on it. And B must actually be different because of it. That is already T-C-R. On this proposal T-C-R is not a mechanism added to a relational world. It is the operational form of consequential relation itself. It recurs from the substrate to speech because anything consequentially relational must instantiate some version of it.

**Status:** an RS working hypothesis, and one of the book's central ones. It is supported by the recurrence just described and by its economy: one grammar explains why consequence has the same shape across domains that share no mechanism. It would be weakened by a clear case of genuine consequence that cannot be described as a distinction preserved by a dependency, or by a demonstration that the recurrence is better explained as convergent design. Its limits matter as much as its reach. A shared grammar across domains is a structural analogy, not an identity of nature. Light, a gene and a sentence share the form of consequence. They do not thereby become the same kind of thing.

The grammar is also **recursive**. A receiver at one stage is, at once, a source relative to another relation: the molecule in your eye that receives light transmits to the cell, which receives and transmits to the nerve. Consequential relation is therefore not a set of separate pipelines but a network, in which every present state is the reception of earlier distinctions and a source of later ones. This gives a sharper picture of the relation between present actuality and what can happen next, which Chapter 4 raised. Actuality carries its history because past distinctions, received, have become present conditions. They shape what is currently reachable because those present conditions are, in turn, transmitted.

## Cause as consequential relation

The grammar also suggests a precise account of cause and effect, suited to a world in which actualisation is chancy.

In a deterministic picture, a cause *produces* its effect: given the cause, the effect follows. In a stochastic world that cannot be the whole story, because what happens next is open. But a distinction can still make a difference. It can exclude some next states. It can make others possible. It can change how likely they are. It can constrain how events are correlated, preserve an invariant through change, or alter what will be accessible later. Each of these is a way of being causal without producing a fixed result.

The book's working formulation is this:

> **A causal relation exists where a distinction in one relational condition modifies the admissible actualisation, or the consequential state, of another through a distinction-preserving dependency.**

The formulation was checked against the project's simplest relational models before being adopted. In them, a three-way condition changes the likelihood of the next two-way event: a present distinction modifies what happens next by weighting it, without determining it. The initial value of that same condition is soon forgotten: within a short run, nothing in the visible state depends on it any longer. On the formulation, the initial distinction has stopped being causal for that state, because no dependency preserves it, while the condition's *current* value goes on mattering. That is the T-C-R test at work. Rules that forbid a change alter what can be reached at all, and rules that merely favour it alter only how likely it is. Those are two distinct ways of being causal. **Status:** working hypothesis. It fits the models. Whether it is the right account of causation in nature is a question of coherence, not of those models.

The account also disciplines a common word. To say that one change "enabled" another can mean four different things: that it came first (*precedence*); that it removed competitors, so the other became the likelier next event (*opportunity*); that it changed the other's own tendency (*rate*); or that it made the other possible at all (*support*). Only the last two are changes of admissibility or tendency, and only support is the work of a forbidding rule. In the project's later models the senses came apart cleanly. Most soft "enabling" turned out to be opportunity.

## Manifestation

There is one further reason T-C-R matters, and it is the deepest. The book has proposed a created relational order beneath the manifest physical world of stones and light. For that order to be manifest at all, as material and experienced reality, there must be a **consequence-preserving relationship** between distinctions in the underlying order and the actual world we meet. If distinctions at the deepest level could make no difference to anything manifest, they would be idle.

That is a minimal claim, and it should be kept minimal. It is tempting to picture something invisible being *transformed* into something visible, as though a hidden object rose through layers and emerged as a stone. That is only one possible architecture, and the book does not assume it. The underlying order and the manifest world might be two descriptions of one relational fact. They might be different resolutions of one order. They might be related by a mapping not yet understood, or in a way not yet conceived. The question of whether the substrate is itself spatial, or whether space arises from it, also remains open. What the book does claim is that, whatever the architecture, consequence must be preserved between them. That is the T-C-R insight applied at its deepest point.

## How T-C-R gathers what came before

T-C-R is not a separate principle added to the previous chapters. It draws them together. It is what mediation requires, if nothing acts across a gap of non-relation (Chapter 3). It is shaped by **admissibility** (Chapter 4) in what a source can transmit, what dependencies exist and what a receiver can register. It is shot through with **constrained chance** (Chapter 5): which molecule in your retina absorbs light from the stone at a given instant is a matter of chance, and the image is steady because many such events compose. And it depends on **identity** (Chapter 6): the carried distinction persists as an equivalence class across changing carriers, with its provenance where something downstream depends on it.

Together these show that a relational world is not a frozen lattice in which everything is related and nothing happens. Relation becomes history: a distinction here makes a difference there, which can in turn be transmitted, carried and received. Life is built from such chains (Chapter 10), and so is human communication (Chapter 11). The theological chapters use the grammar, with care, as a lens on some forms of corruption and restoration (Chapters 13 and 14).

**Status.** Emission, propagation and absorption are established physics, and T-C-R organises that physics without replacing it. It has not been shown to predict anything the physics does not. That T-C-R is the form of consequential relation itself, reaches to the substrate, is recursive and supplies the right account of cause are working hypotheses. Two things are open. In every model the project has built, the dependencies that carry distinctions were designed, and whether created order generates its own carriers from minimal relations is not known. Nor is the architecture of manifestation.

## What T-C-R does not mean

The same three-part form appears in physics, in biology, in human conversation and in Scripture. Scripture presents God's word as going out and accomplishing its purpose in those who receive it:

> `For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud, that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater: So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it.`
> *Isaiah 55:10-11*

> `How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher?… So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God.`
> *Romans 10:14, 17*

Isaiah's image is physical (rain that waters the earth), and its subject is God's word that accomplishes his purpose. Paul traces a sequence of sending, preaching, hearing and believing. The form is unmistakably that of something made available, carried and received. In a creation made by the Word, that likeness is not surprising. But Chapter 1 has already given the governing principle: shared form across categories does not mean shared nature. God's word is not a physical signal, and faith is not the absorption of light. The soul is not a pattern carried by a disturbance. Information transfer is not consciousness. The physical case does not explain the spiritual one, and the spiritual one is not secretly physical.

If anything, the direction of illumination runs the other way. A world made by a God who speaks, and whose word goes out and accomplishes what he purposes, is a world in which it is fitting that even light and sound should be consequential in this way. Physical T-C-R may be one of the ways created order bears the likeness of its Maker. But likeness is the most that can be claimed.

## The question that remains

The light from the stone arrives at your eye as a vast number of individual events, each one a matter of chance, and yet you see a steady image. Light bends through glass by exactly the amount an optician can calculate in advance. If what happens at the deepest level is constrained chance, where does such exact regularity come from? And what is the mathematics that describes it so well? Those are the questions of Chapter 8.

# Chapter 8: Regularity, Coordination, and the Place of Mathematics

Pump up a bicycle tyre and feel it grow firm. The pressure you feel is the combined effect of a vast number of air molecules striking the inner wall. Each moves erratically, its path impossible to follow. Yet the pressure is steady, and it obeys a simple law: squeeze the air into half the volume at the same temperature and the pressure doubles. Out of the tumult of individual molecules comes a regularity you can write in a single line.

The previous chapters proposed that what happens at the deepest level of created order is constrained chance, and that distinctions reach us through chains of transmission, each made of many chancy events. This chapter asks how such a world can be so reliably regular. Light bends through a lens by exactly the amount the optician calculates. The stone is the same weight tomorrow. And the chapter asks what mathematics, which describes this regularity so well, is in relation to the world it describes.

Several different problems are hidden inside the question of regularity, and it helps to separate them at the start. First, many chance events can simply average into a stable result. Second, the way those events are related to one another can change the behaviour of the whole, even when the chance of each event stays exactly the same. Third, a simple mathematical law can describe the whole accurately at one scale without describing what happens beneath it. And fourth, such a description may leave out lower-level distinctions that do not matter for the question it answers but do matter for others. These are different problems. The chapter takes them in turn, and the examples that follow (coins, rings, fluids and hidden structure) each belong to one of them.

## Regularity from many chances

Part of the answer has long been understood, and it belongs to mainstream science rather than to the Relational Substrate project. When very many chance events are combined, their total can be highly predictable even though each event is open. Flip a coin ten times and you might get seven heads. Flip it ten million times and the proportion of heads will almost certainly lie within a tenth of a per cent of a half. This is the **law of large numbers**. In its simplest familiar form it concerns independent events. Mathematicians have proved versions of it for events that are dependent on one another, provided the dependence fades enough over time or distance.

Physics is built on this in many places: the gas laws; the jittering of small particles in water, which Albert Einstein traced in 1905 to the random jostling of molecules; the precise half-lives of Chapter 5. In each case a deterministic-looking law at the large scale is the stable behaviour of many chancy events at the small scale.

Physicists call such laws **effective**: they hold to great precision at the scale where they apply, without describing what happens underneath. The book uses the phrase **effective determinism** for behaviour that is regular, reliable and predictable to the precision we can observe, whatever the underlying process. It is a fact about regularity, not a metaphysical claim that nothing could have happened otherwise.

Two neighbouring ideas need to be kept apart from it. **Mathematical determinism** is a property of equations: given the state at one moment, the equations fix the state at every later moment. Many of the equations of physics are deterministic in this sense, including the fluid equations discussed below, even when what they describe is the aggregate of very many chancy events. A deterministic equation can therefore describe excellently a process that is not deterministic beneath it. And an **attractor** is a precisely defined idea in the mathematics of changing systems. It is a state, or set of states, toward which a system tends from a wide range of starting points and to which it returns when slightly disturbed. The word is easily used loosely for any outcome that turns up often. But a reliable outcome is not by itself evidence of an attractor: the illustration in Chapter 5 reached the right answer every time because of conservation, not because anything drew it there.

## Same odds, different behaviour

Averaging answers the first problem: independent chance events are enough to keep the pressure in a tyre steady. But an average is only one feature of a whole. The second problem begins with a simple puzzle.

Imagine two rooms, each with a hundred people tossing fair coins at the same moment, again and again. In the first room everyone tosses independently. In the second, people are paired, and each pair agrees in advance that whatever one gets, the partner will report the opposite. For any single person, in either room, the chance of heads is exactly one half. Nothing about the individual odds differs. Yet the rooms behave completely differently as wholes. In the first room the number of heads wanders from toss to toss, sometimes 44, sometimes 57. In the second room it is exactly fifty, every time.

The individual chances, what statisticians call the **marginal** probabilities, are identical. What differs is how the events are related to one another: the **joint process**. Two systems can assign exactly the same probability to every individual event and still behave quite differently as wholes, because in one the events are tied together and in the other they are not. In short:

```text
the same individual odds
  are not the same joint process,
  which is not the same pattern of co-variation,
  which is not the same behaviour of the whole
```

## Coordination that matters

The book calls such relations among chance events **coordination**, and the word needs care in two directions.

First, coordination does not mean a controller standing over the events and arranging them. In the second room nobody directs the tosses. The coordination lies in the fact that each pair acts as one. Coordination is a relation among events, not an agent above them.

Second, and more important, coordination is not what makes an average stable. Ordinary aggregation already does that, as the tyre showed. Coordination matters for the other features of a whole, the ones that individual chances do not fix:

- how large the fluctuations are, and what shape they take;
- whether a quantity is held *exactly*, not merely on average;
- how the whole moves from moment to moment along its path;
- how it responds when disturbed;
- which future states remain open to it.

The two rooms of coin-tossers had the same average. They differed in everything else.

Exploratory work on chance events arranged in rings of locally coupled units, not yet a formal record, sharpens the point. Units with the same local chances could produce very different global behaviour depending on how their fluctuations were coupled. And it was not correlation as such that mattered. A relation between two events can be statistically strong and still irrelevant to the property in question. A modest dependence can be decisive if it is aligned with what matters. The questions that decide the outcome are these: which events are coupled, in which direction, under which conditions, what their coupling preserves, and what difference it makes downstream. Coordination that depends on the current state, tightening where a fluctuation would otherwise do harm, behaves differently from coordination applied uniformly.

The same work showed that stability comes in layers. A whole can be stable in its mean while its fluctuations are not, and some of its quantities may be exactly conserved while others still genuinely vary. A stable mean is not stable fluctuations, and stable fluctuations are not yet the full relational coherence of the whole.

None of this mathematics belongs to a relation-first ontology. Probability theory, statistical mechanics and network science describe dependence and coupling with great sophistication, and object-based models represent them routinely. What the relational proposal changes is the reading. On an object-first reading, the dependence structure is an extra fact about how already-complete things happen to be related. On the relational reading, it belongs to *what the physical whole is*: the coordination of its parts is part of their constitution, not a statistic laid over them. That is the book's ontological proposal about the mathematics, not a discovery within it. Nor does the book claim that local relations alone account for the regularity of created order as a whole. Its claim is that the regularity is real, and that local relation, coordination and constraint are part of how it holds.

## What mathematics is doing

The regularities of the physical world are described by mathematics, often with astonishing success. In 1960 the physicist Eugene Wigner wrote of "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences": structures worked out by mathematicians for their own sake turn out, again and again, to describe nature with precision nobody expected. What is the mathematics doing?

Consider the equations that describe moving fluids, such as air over a wing or water in a pipe. They are called the Navier–Stokes equations, after two nineteenth-century scientists, and they are used every day to design aircraft and forecast weather. They treat a fluid as a smooth, continuous substance whose velocity and pressure vary from point to point. Yet a fluid is made of molecules in constant chaotic motion. The equations work because, at the scale where engineers use them, the combined behaviour of the molecules is regular enough to be described *as if* the fluid were smooth. Physicists can derive the fluid equations, approximately, from the statistical behaviour of molecules, through averaging over many particles, conservation of mass, momentum and energy, and rapid local settling toward equilibrium. At very small scales or in very thin gases, the smooth description fails and the molecular picture must take over. The equations even have puzzles of their own: whether they always have smooth solutions is one of the great unsolved problems of mathematics.

This shows two things. First, deterministic mathematics at one scale does not imply that reality is deterministic at every scale. Second, a smooth field description need not be the ontology of what it describes. Water is not made of the continuous field the equations describe, and the equations are still true at their scale.

The Relational Substrate project takes from this a research question rather than a result. The project has not derived the Navier–Stokes equations, or any law of physics, from the relational substrate. The question it asks is this: *what kinds of relational constraint and coordination preserve the right invariants, correlations and continuity for stable effective laws to emerge at all?* That is a real question, because, as the coin rooms showed, not every arrangement of chance produces stable laws. It is also an open one.

## Hidden structure beneath a description

There is a further caution, connected to Chapter 6. A description can map many different underlying states onto one visible picture, and the hidden differences can still shape what happens next.

The project's simplest relational models give a striking, exact instance. Add a genuinely three-way relation among three points, a link joining all three at once that can exist independently of the ordinary two-way links, and ask what an observer who sees only the two-way links would find. Wherever the three-way relation could influence the two-way ones, the next visible change depended not only on how the visible links were now but on how they had just been. The visible process *appeared to have memory*, although nothing in the rules recorded the past. The memory was produced by hiding a present relation. A coarse description can turn hidden present structure into what looks like history.

So **mathematical equivalence under one description does not settle whether two things are the same.** A coarse description is often exactly right for its purpose. But whether what it leaves out is truly irrelevant depends on whether any reachable future process draws on it. That is the same principle Chapter 6 applied to identity, now applied to the mathematics of regularity.

The general lesson is the one Chapter 2 drew, now sharpened. **A mathematical structure that successfully describes behaviour is not, by that fact, the thing that reality fundamentally is.** Mathematics describes regularities: stable relations, invariants and the aggregate behaviour of coordinated processes. It describes them so well because they are genuinely there and the world is ordered and intelligible. That is a reason for great respect toward mathematics, not for suspicion. The respect is owed to what mathematics captures, without confusing the description with the described.

On the relational proposal, mathematics works because created order is structured by constraint, preserved by equivalence and invariance, and regular in aggregate through coordination. Those are precisely the things mathematics is best at describing. Scripture speaks of the heavens in exactly this register of ordered disclosure:

> `The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard.`
> *Psalm 19:1-3*

> `Knowest thou the ordinances of heaven? canst thou set the dominion thereof in the earth?`
> *Job 38:33*

> `For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead.`
> *Romans 1:20*

Creation is intelligible: it can be "understood by the things that are made". Its ordinances can be known, though not commanded. This is the ground of science, and it is why science can be trusted within its domain. Scripture does not tell us the Navier–Stokes equations. It tells us why a world in which such equations hold, and in which human minds can find them, is exactly what one should expect of a creation made through the Word.

## Measurement, model and interpretation

The layers of Chapter 2 apply here with full force. What physics measures about gravity, light and magnetism, and what its mathematics predicts, constrain every ontology, this one included. Electromagnetism, from Maxwell's equations to quantum electrodynamics, predicts with precision unmatched in any other field of knowledge. Where established measurement and prediction stand, they stand, and the relational reading must agree with them.

What a field is, whether spacetime is a thing or an ordering of relations, whether quantum chance is fundamental, and what a particle is between measurements are questions of interpretation, and they remain open. The equations do not dictate the answers. When an answer is presented as though they did, a metaphysical assumption has borrowed the authority of a measurement. The remedy is not to distrust measurement. It is to notice the assumption, and to test it for coherence like any other proposal.

## Gravity and time in the simplest models

Chapter 2 opened with a question the book has not yet answered: what *is* gravity? The project does not know, and has not derived gravity from the relational substrate. Its Gravity/Time programme asked something narrower: what can the simplest relational models show about attraction and about time? The answers are exact results on systems of at most seven points, and the book adopts them at that scope (Appendix E.8).

First, a rule that favours some pattern of links defines a *relational potential*: a count of that pattern, weighted by the rule. The system spends more time where the count is high. "Attraction" toward a structure is then participation in the favoured pattern, with no named centre and no force reaching across. **Status:** mechanism candidate, in models only.

Second, **gathering**, a lasting preference for relation to a reference structure, is not **succession** (Chapter 6). When the models grew from six points to seven, the directed handover kept its direction in every case, while the only gathering seen alongside it fell to the level of the comparison. Much apparent gathering is density alone, so a claim of gathering must survive adjustment for density. And a reference made of relations is not a neutral frame: its own links take part through the same rules.

Third, a negative result. The rules do *not* tie the rate of activity to the potential. The same preferred states admit very different rates near a structure and far from it, depending on how a rule's weight is split between forming and dissolving links. Measured clocks really do run slower deeper in a gravitational field. A relational reading of that fact would need a principle linking clock rates to relational structure, and these rules do not contain one. Every model claim about attraction or time must also name its clock, because counting steps and measuring continuous time can give opposite answers.

None of this is gravity, and none of it is a theory of time. It shows what the simplest relational rules can and cannot do.

## What would count against these proposals

A proposal that cannot be wrong in any identifiable way tells us little. So it is worth stating what would count against the specific claims of Chapters 3 to 7, and where they are currently hard to test.

- **Contradicting precise measurement.** Any specific Relational Substrate mechanism that contradicted a precise measurement would fail, however coherent it looked. This has already happened. A model the project built in 2026 predicted the direction of an effect in metal fatigue but its size many times too small, and the project recorded it as a failure of magnitude rather than adjusting the model to fit.
- **Intrinsic properties at the base.** The relation-first proposal (Chapter 3) would be seriously weakened if physics came to show that its most fundamental entities have fully intrinsic properties, fixed independently of every interaction. The trend of the last century runs the other way, but the question is open.
- **Entanglement and mediation.** The proposal that influence is always mediated (Chapters 3 and 7) faces real pressure from quantum entanglement. Measurements on two distant entangled particles show correlations that, by Bell's theorem, no theory satisfying certain locality assumptions can reproduce, and experiments have confirmed those correlations. They cannot be used to send a signal, which is why relativity is not violated, and several interpretations, including relational ones, give accounts of them. Bell's theorem rules out a specific class of local explanations, not every conceivable notion of mediation. But entanglement remains the hardest test case for an account of mediated relation, and it is not a solved problem for this one.
- **Gravity's clocks.** The project has no relational account of gravitational time dilation, and its simplest models show that one would need a principle they do not contain (above). That is an open gap, not yet a refutation, and it marks where a relational account of gravity is most exposed.
- **The stochastic hypothesis is currently hard to test.** The standard interpretations of quantum theory agree on every measurement made so far, so present evidence cannot decide between a fundamentally chancy world and a deterministic one at that level. (Theories that modify quantum dynamics with spontaneous collapse do make slightly different predictions, which experiments are now testing.) That protects the hypothesis from easy refutation, and it is also a reason to hold it no more firmly than a coherent hypothesis.
- **The models do not single out RS.** The project's models demonstrate possibilities. Ordinary computer science and probability account for every result they produced. The laboratory's most recent generation also recorded a negative result: a pattern of "dissolution leading to new organisation" that the project hoped to find in its simplest systems did not appear (Appendix B). Results of this kind narrow what the ideas can claim. They show that the ideas are coherent and workable, not that nature uses them.

These limits do not empty the proposals of value. They locate it. The value lies in coherence: in whether a relation-first account of physical order, with constrained chance, relational identity and consequential transmission, helps the whole of what we know hang together better than its alternatives. That is the test to which the next chapter turns.

## From the stone to everything else

The physical arc of the book is now complete. The stone is a stable, history-bearing configuration within a created order of relation, seen through a chain of preserved dependency, and regular enough to be written in exact mathematics because many constrained and coordinated chance events add up to a reliable whole.

The problem underneath this whole chapter, then, is not merely how randomness averages out. Mainstream science already understands that well. It is what relational organisation makes stable identity, stable consequence and stable effective law possible across scales, while preserving the distinctions that still matter. Averaging, coordination, effective equations and hidden structure are four different parts of that one question. The Relational Substrate project has not answered it. It has learned to ask it more precisely.

So far this is an account of stones and light. The book's claim, however, is that it matters for far more than stones and light, and that a relation-first physical world changes how the rest of reality, including you, can be understood. Chapter 9 makes that case.

# Chapter 9: Why Relation Matters Beyond Physics

On Monday you promise a friend that you will help her move house on Saturday. On Saturday morning you would rather stay in bed. You get up and go.

It is an ordinary scene, as ordinary as the stone on the path, and like the stone it rests on a great deal. For the promise to be what it seems, several things must be true. The person who gets up on Saturday must be the same person who spoke on Monday, though much in you has changed in between. Your words on Monday must have gone out from you and become something in her: an expectation, a plan, a reliance she has built on. What you do on Saturday must have consequences for her, and through her for others. And there must be something about the promise that *binds* you. It is not merely that you predicted you would go, but that you *owe* it. If you had stayed in bed, she would have been wronged, not merely surprised.

Identity through time, a distinction carried from one person to another and received as a change in her, consequence, obligation, belonging: these are among the most basic features of human life. This chapter asks how they sit with what the physical world fundamentally is. That is the question the earlier chapters have been preparing, and it is the hinge of the book.

## The tests the answer must pass

Chapter 2 distinguished local, regional and global coherence. It also argued that global coherence asks more than the absence of contradiction. The domains of an account must be genuinely connected. Ideas must cross from one domain to another without silently changing kind. Claims must keep their meaning through the layers. A claim taken round a cycle of domains must return the same. And the real distinctions must survive all of this.

The promise is a good place to apply those tests, because it crosses every boundary the book cares about. It involves a body that walks to a friend's house: physics and biology. It involves a person who remembers and intends: personal life. It involves an obligation: moral order. And for a Christian it involves faithfulness before God. A picture of the physical world must not only explain falling stones. It must allow the transitions from stone to body to person to promise to be intelligible, without contradiction and without reducing any of them to another.

## The serious alternatives

It would be easy to set up the question as a contest between the relational picture and a crude alternative: a world of hard, self-contained lumps, each with properties entirely its own, that only bump into one another. Some philosophers have held views close to that. A well-known modern version holds that the world is a vast mosaic of local, intrinsic facts, with everything else, including causes and laws, supervening on how those facts happen to be arranged. That view deserves a hearing. But it is not the strongest alternative, and an argument aimed only at it would prove little.

The serious alternatives already contain a great deal of relation.

- **Powers ontologies** hold that the basic properties of things are dispositions to act on, and be acted on by, other things. A power is already directed beyond its bearer.
- **Field ontologies,** which many physicists hold, treat fields spread through all of space as the basic physical reality, so that particles are disturbances in something continuous and everywhere interacting.
- **Hylomorphic accounts,** in the tradition of Aristotle and Aquinas, hold that things are constituted by form, and that form is intrinsically ordered toward activity and toward other things.
- **Process philosophies** take events and their connections to be more basic than enduring substances.
- **Structural realism** holds that what our best theories most securely describe is structure, the pattern of relations.

These are serious positions held by serious people. Several of them converge, in important ways, with the view this book defends. The question is therefore not whether the alternatives can make room for relation. Many of them can. It is *where* relation enters, and what that costs. Chapter 3 named one cost every ontology must meet: independently located things everywhere share one grammar of interaction. Where an alternative supplies that common interface through a field, a law, a form or a set of powers, the fair question is what work that common structure is doing, and whether it is constitutive relation under another name.

## Where relation enters

Here is the difference that matters. In the relational picture this book proposes, relation is *constitutive* at the base. What a physical thing is cannot be specified apart from how it stands to others. Chapter 6 even defined the identity of a thing by what could distinguish it in relation to possible future interactions. The contrast that matters is with ontologies in which the fundamental entities are individuated wholly or substantially independently of constitutive relation, so that what a thing is can be fully specified before its relations are. In such ontologies relation is something already-complete bearers *have*, *exercise* or *enter into*. It is not what they *are*. Not every substance ontology is like this. A sophisticated powers theorist may hold that a thing's essential powers are other-directed from the start, and a hylomorphist may hold that form and its ordered capacities are constitutive from the beginning. Those views already share part of what this book defends, and the argument below is not aimed at them.

That difference may look slight in physics, where both kinds of ontology describe the falling stone equally well. It becomes significant when the account must cross into human life, because human life is pervaded by relation that is not merely exercised but constitutive. You are, in part, *who you are* through your parents, your promises, your friendships, your history with others and your standing before God. A person is not a self-contained individual who then enters relationships, as a billiard ball enters collisions. Personal identity is relational in a much stronger sense.

So an ontology whose fundamental entities are individuated independently of constitutive relation must, at some point on the way from stones to persons, introduce constitutive relation as something new: a kind of reality that its base does not contain in any form. That is not impossible. But it is a discontinuity. It adds a bridge assumption that has to be explained, or accepted without explanation, somewhere between physics and personhood.

A relation-first ontology does not need that bridge. Its base already has the *form* of what appears, transformed and deepened, in human life:

- constitutive relation (Chapter 3);
- identity as relational equivalence carrying its own history (Chapter 6);
- distinctions preserved by dependency and received as consequence (Chapter 7);
- wholes stabilised by the coordination of their parts (Chapter 8).

When relation becomes personal and moral, it does not appear as a stranger from a different reality. It appears in a world whose grain it already follows.

## The argument, stated plainly

The central argument of the book can now be stated in steps:

1. Human life, as we live it and as Scripture describes it, is pervaded by constitutive relation, identity through change, consequence that passes from one person to another, and responsibility.
2. A physical ontology whose fundamental entities are individuated independently of constitutive relation must introduce constitutive relationality for the first time at some higher level, as a new kind of reality.
3. A physical ontology in which relation is constitutive at the base already contains the form of these features, so that living, personal and moral relation are continuous in form with the physical order they arise in, while remaining distinct in kind.
4. Other things being equal, an account with fewer unexplained discontinuities and fewer ad hoc bridge assumptions between its domains is more globally coherent, by the tests of connection and faithful translation.
5. Therefore, *if* a relation-first picture of physical order is consistent with everything we observe, it is to be preferred on grounds of global coherence.

The argument does not claim that other ontologies make promises impossible or responsibility unintelligible. That would be stronger than the book needs, and probably false. It claims something more modest and more defensible: relation-first ontology offers a more *economical continuity of form* across physical, living and personal reality, provided the boundaries between categories stay intact. The conditional in step 5 matters. That is why Chapters 3 to 8 took such care over what physics does and does not say.

If the argument is sound, it shows why the physics of this book is not an optional technical appendix to a theological book. *How* the material world is real bears on whether the rest of reality hangs together.

## What the relational picture does not provide

It is as important to say what the argument does not claim, because it is easily inflated.

The common relational grounding of material things is a kind of glue. It allows physical causation, biological organisation, embodied action, consequence in the environment, communication and social dependence to belong to one created order. But it is continuity across the boundaries, not reduction across them:

- Moral obligation is not a field, and a promise is not a force.
- Love is not covariance.
- Sin is not entropy.
- Hearing and receiving the word of God is not receptor chemistry.
- The soul is not a carrier.
- Resurrection is not the substrate repairing itself.
- Divine action is not a wider set of physical permissions.

The relational picture does not tell you that you ought to keep your promise. Chapter 12 will distinguish what is physically possible from what is morally right, and insist that the first never settles the second. What the relational picture does is remove the strangeness of a world in which promises could bind at all.

## The strongest objection: emergence

The most serious reply runs like this. Nobody claims that promises exist among particles. They *emerge*. Complex arrangements display properties their parts lack: wetness emerges from molecules that are not wet, and life from chemistry that is not alive. Why should constitutive relation not emerge in the same way, from a base without it?

The book accepts emergence fully. Chapter 10 will argue that living organisation is genuinely new and genuinely explanatory at its own level. The disagreement is narrower. In familiar cases, what emerges is a new and higher expression of capacities the base already has. Wetness expresses the attraction molecules already have for one another. Life expresses chemistry that already includes catalysis, templating and self-assembly. The question is not whether new properties appear. It is whether the base already contains the *relational resources* that make the emergent structures intelligible.

If the base contains entities individuated independently of their relations, whose relations are always something added or exercised, then constitutive relation emerges from a base where nothing is constituted by its relations. That can be asserted, but it is precisely the kind of discontinuity step 2 names. A powers ontology narrows the gap, because powers are already other-directed, and the book counts that as convergence. It does not count it as refutation. The relational picture's claim is a claim of *continuity of structural capacity*: what emerges in living, personal and moral life is new in kind, and it emerges from a base that already has its form. It is not a claim to derive moral truth from physics. That is a more coherent position, though not one that can be proved.

## A second case: the poisoned river

A factory upstream discharges waste into a river. Downstream, fish die, and the people of a village who drink the water fall ill. Each link is physical: chemicals are released, carried in the water, and received as changes in the bodies of fish and people, a chain of transmissions crossing from chemistry to biology to human life. But the case is also moral. Someone is responsible, and the villagers have been wronged.

Apply the round-trip test. Begin with the physical chain, translate it into the moral question of responsibility, then into the action required (stop the discharge, compensate the village), and back into the physical world (cleaner water). In a relational creation, every step of that cycle concerns the same world. Responsibility answers to *real* consequences flowing through a *real* order of relation, and the remedy flows back through the same order. The moral layer is distinct, since responsibility is not a chemical fact. But it is not a separate story attached from outside.

Scripture speaks of exactly this reach, in which human wrongdoing is received by the land itself:

> `Hear the word of the LORD, ye children of Israel: for the LORD hath a controversy with the inhabitants of the land, because there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land. By swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing, and committing adultery, they break out, and blood toucheth blood. Therefore shall the land mourn, and every one that dwelleth therein shall languish, with the beasts of the field, and with the fowls of heaven; yea, the fishes of the sea also shall be taken away.`
> *Hosea 4:1-3*

> `For the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation of the sons of God. For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope… For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now.`
> *Romans 8:19-20, 22*

Hosea's land mourns because of the people's lying and killing. Paul's whole creation groans. Scripture consistently presents human moral life and the created world as bound together, so that wrong done by persons is received in the land, and the redemption of persons concerns the whole creation. The book does not claim that a relational physics *explains* these texts. Their explanation is theological. The claim is that a relational account of the material world sits naturally with the world they describe, while an account of sealed and indifferent objects makes them sound like poetry about a reality that is not really like that.

## Why this is a Christian question

This argument is not a covert attempt to derive Christianity from physics. A reader who accepted every word of it would not thereby believe in God. But it bears on Christian faith in a particular way.

Scripture describes a world in which relation is fundamental at every level. God makes humanity in his image and declares that it is not good for the man to be alone (Genesis 2:18). He binds himself to his people by covenant. He calls them to love, to keep faith and to bear one another's burdens. In Christ he reconciles "all things unto himself… whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven" (Colossians 1:20). If the physical world were, at bottom, a world in which nothing is constituted by its relations, this scriptural world would rest on a creation of a different kind, and the transition would need explaining. If the physical world is relational from its foundation, created by the God in whom "all things consist", then the relational life Scripture describes is the fulfilment, at its own level, of the grain of the whole creation. That does not prove the gospel. It removes one reason for thinking the gospel must be about a world other than the one we physically inhabit.

## Coherence is not the same as truth

A book that argues for coherence must face its limits here.

A coherent account can be wrong. A detective story can be perfectly coherent and accuse the wrong suspect. More subtly, an argument can become *more* coherent as it proceeds while moving *further* from the truth, if every step builds on an early mistake nobody questions. The research behind this project met this directly, though in exploratory work rather than in formally recorded experiments. While developing an AI-assisted method of reasoning that runs several independent lines of thought on one problem, the author observed that when the lines were repeatedly merged into a shared summary and fed back, agreement rose steadily, and an error present in one early line could harden into a confident consensus. The project calls this a *false attractor*. The lesson, which this book adopts, is that coherence is not the same as fidelity to the truth. Agreement can be produced by a shared hidden premise as easily as by a shared grasp of reality.

That applies to this book. Its argument is coherent, but that does not make it true, and its own coherence could be a false attractor. The safeguards are those already described. The argument stays accountable to observation wherever observation can reach (Chapter 8 named where it cannot). It keeps its levels of claim distinct. It keeps alternatives in view (Appendix A). And it submits its conclusions to Scripture, which is not one more input to the coherence but the frame within which coherence is sought.

There is also a reason this matters for global coherence in particular. Global coherence is the most demanding test there is, because it must hold across every domain at once, and because errors hidden in one domain tend to surface as drift on the round trip through the others. A false attractor in one place is likely, sooner or later, to produce a contradiction or a silent change of meaning somewhere else. That is exactly why global coherence is a stronger test than local success, and why the book is willing to be judged by it.

## Relation across categories

![Figure 5. Relation across categories. Material, living, personal, moral and spiritual realities are distinct kinds, not layers built one out of another; relation runs through each in its own way; God is not a category within creation but its Creator, who upholds all things.](v2/figures/fig-categories.png)

The chapter can now complete what Chapter 1 began. Creation contains distinct categories of reality, and relation appears in each of them differently.

- In the **physical** world, relation is constraint, equivalence, coordination and the carriage of distinctions (Chapters 3 to 8).
- In the **living** world, it becomes metabolism, signalling, templating and repair (Chapter 10).
- In the **personal** world, it becomes address and response, knowledge and love, promise and trust (Chapter 11).
- In the **moral** world, it becomes obligation, rightness and wrong (Chapter 12).
- In the **spiritual** world, it becomes communion with God.
- God's relation to creation is that of Creator to creature. It belongs to no category within creation, and every created relation depends on it.

The same word, *relation*, is used across all these categories, and it is used truly. What runs through them is a continuity of form, not a single nature. The remaining chapters take the categories one at a time, beginning with life, and show how each stands within a relational creation without being swallowed by it.

# Chapter 10: Life

Turn the stone over. Underneath, in the damp, there is a woodlouse, a pale root, a thread of fungus, perhaps a small seed beginning to split. A moment ago the book was concerned with a lump of mineral. Now it faces things that feed, grow, respond, repair themselves and make more of their own kind.

Life is one of the clearest realities in the world, and one of the hardest for any account of reality to place well. Some pictures of the world are confident as long as they deal with inert matter and become hesitant once life appears in earnest. If the relational account is right, life should not need a separate explanatory afterthought. Nor should it be flattened into chemistry that merely happens to be complicated. This chapter asks what life is, within a relational creation, and where Scripture draws its boundaries.

## Life is given

Scripture places life near the centre of creation, and it speaks of it in a particular way. Life is commanded and ordered:

> `And God said, Let the earth bring forth the living creature after his kind, cattle, and creeping thing, and beast of the earth after his kind: and it was so.`
> *Genesis 1:24*

And life is given, held and sustained by God, not possessed by living things in their own right:

> `The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.`
> *Job 33:4*

> `Thou hidest thy face, they are troubled: thou takest away their breath, they die, and return to their dust. Thou sendest forth thy spirit, they are created: and thou renewest the face of the earth.`
> *Psalm 104:29-30*

> `Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things.`
> *Acts 17:25*

The pattern is consistent. Living things are real, active and their own: Genesis says the earth *brings forth* them, and they bring forth after their kind. Yet their life is never self-grounding. It is given, upheld and, in the end, withdrawn by God. The book holds both together. The account of living organisation that follows describes how life operates within created order. It does not describe where life ultimately comes from, which Scripture locates in the gift of God.

## Life as sustained organisation

What distinguishes the woodlouse from the stone? Both are configurations within created order, and both persist. The difference lies in *how* they persist.

The stone persists largely by being left alone: its crystal structure is stable, and the changes it ordinarily undergoes do not disturb it. A living thing persists in the opposite way. It is constantly taking in matter and energy, transforming them, and expelling what it no longer needs. A woodlouse is not a fixed collection of molecules. It is a pattern of organisation that keeps re-establishing itself through a continuous flow of material, like the flame of Chapter 6, but enormously more intricate and active. It maintains itself, repairs damage, responds to its surroundings, grows according to a characteristic pattern and reproduces.

So life is **sustained organisation**: persistence that is actively maintained, under many layers of constraint, by the continual work of the living thing itself. In the terms of Chapter 6, a living thing maintains its organised continuity by continually *re-actualising* itself within its relevant equivalence class, where the stone keeps its own largely by being undisturbed, and by actively correcting departures from it. Its continuing individuality also depends on the unbroken history of that living process. A living thing does not merely persist. It works to stay what it is.

## Signals within signals

Much of that activity is transmission. When a cell detects food, a hormone or a threat, a molecule at its surface changes shape. That change is released into a chain of further molecular changes, carried through the cell, and received at its destination as a determinate change: a gene switched on, a movement begun, a substance secreted. One cell signals to another by releasing molecules that are carried through the fluid between them and received at the other's surface. Nerves carry impulses, as Chapter 7 described. Hormones carry differences through the bloodstream from one organ to another.

A living body is, among other things, a vast composition of transmissions, nested within one another and running in parallel and in sequence. They are coordinated so that the whole responds as one. The grammar of Chapter 7, transmit, carry, receive (T-C-R), applies here with full force, and it applies more richly than it did to the stone and the light. In the living case, reception is not passive. A cell *regulates* what it will receive, adjusting its receptors to admit some signals and refuse others. The admissibility of reception, which in the eye was fixed by the structure of molecules, becomes in living things something the organism partly governs for itself.

## Templates and repair

Chapter 6 drew a firm lesson: preserving an equivalence class is not the same as repairing it. When information was destroyed, nothing in the system could bring it back. Recovery after damage requires that the information needed for recovery survive somewhere.

Life solves this problem, and the way it does so is one of its defining features. Living things carry **templates**: patterns from which their organisation can be re-established. The most famous is DNA. Every cell carries, in its DNA, information that takes part in shaping how proteins are built, when and where genes are active, and how the cell divides, repairs and specialises. When parts of a cell are damaged, that surviving information allows them to be rebuilt. When DNA itself is damaged, the cell often repairs it using the complementary strand, a second copy of the same information held in reverse. When an organism reproduces, the template is copied and passed on, so that a new organism of the same kind can grow.

DNA is not the only surviving information a cell can draw on. The cell's current state, the chemical marks that switch genes on and off (studied as epigenetics), the shape and arrangement of its parts, chemical gradients across a developing tissue, and its surroundings all carry constraints that guide repair and development. Living repair uses whatever relevant distinctions have survived, wherever they are held.

The first edition of this book described DNA as a *constraint map* rather than a program, and the description still serves. DNA does not generate life by issuing commands, the way software runs on a computer. It participates in a far larger web of constraints: the chemistry of the cell, its membranes, its surroundings and its history. Together these shape how living form is built, maintained and passed on. It is the surviving information that makes repair and reproduction possible, but only within the living whole that reads it. A strand of DNA in a test tube does nothing.

## Chance within life

Living things are full of chance at the molecular level. Molecules inside a cell jostle and collide erratically, genes switch on and off in bursts, and copying DNA occasionally introduces errors. Two genetically identical cells in identical surroundings can behave differently simply because of molecular chance.

Yet development is astonishingly reliable. A fertilised egg grows into an organism of its kind, with the right organs in the right places, again and again, despite all the noise. Biologists describe this robustness in terms of feedback, redundancy and buffering, ways in which developing tissues correct for fluctuations rather than amplifying them. In the language of this book, development is a striking case of **constrained chance producing reliable form**. Countless open molecular events, coordinated and constrained by the organisation of the developing body, add up to a characteristic outcome. The pattern of Chapters 5 and 8 appears here in its most impressive form.

Chance also plays a part in variation. The occasional errors in copying are one source of the differences between generations. Scripture's phrase "after his kind" speaks of patterned continuity in living things. Kinds reproduce kinds. It does not speak of rigid sameness. Living order includes both fidelity and variation: if inheritance were total rigidity, life would be lifeless repetition, and if variation were sheer randomness, nothing would be inherited at all. The first edition called this **bounded generativity**, and the phrase is worth keeping. Variation belongs *inside* ordered continuity, within the admissible range that the created order allows. How wide that range is, and how far living kinds have changed over history, are questions on which Christians who share this book's commitments disagree, and the book does not settle them. It claims only that variation is bounded, ordered and real, and that life's capacity to vary is itself part of what God has made.

## Wholes that are more than their parts

A tissue is more than a crowd of cells, and an organism is more than a crowd of tissues. That does not mean some extra substance is inserted at each level. It means the organisation itself makes a real difference to what happens, and explains things at its own level that a list of parts cannot. What makes the difference? Chapter 8 showed that relations between events can stabilise a whole in ways that independent events cannot, and that what matters is coordination aligned with consequence, not correlation for its own sake. That is the key here as well. A collection becomes a genuine **higher-order whole** when its parts are coupled so that they no longer behave independently. Then a disturbance to one part is redistributed through the whole, and the whole has properties, such as a temperature it regulates or a shape it restores, that no part has by itself.

The living body is full of such coupling. The heartbeat couples with breathing, both couple with the rhythms of the nervous system, those couple with sleep and with hormonal cycles, and each rhythm both responds to the others and steadies them. Lose one major coupling, through illness or injury, and the effects spread far beyond the place of damage. Feedback keeps body temperature, blood sugar and oxygen within narrow ranges: when a quantity drifts, processes activate to bring it back. Other kinds of feedback push the body through thresholds that gradual change could not cross, as in blood clotting or birth.

The result is the organism, a pattern that is actively sustained. It is less a thing that happens to have a pattern than a pattern that keeps itself in being. It is a genuine unity, real in its own right, and its organisation does explanatory work that a list of its parts cannot do.

## Direction and purpose

Living things are directed. A seed grows toward the plant it will become. An embryo develops through stages in an ordered sequence, toward a characteristic adult form. A wound heals toward the restoration of what was there. It is very difficult to describe living things without saying what their parts and processes are *for*. The eye is for seeing. The heart is for pumping blood.

The book takes this directedness to be real. It is not imposed on the living thing from outside at every moment, and it is not an illusion. It is part of what the organisation of a living thing *is*: an internal orientation toward characteristic form. Chapter 4 placed purpose upstream of the substrate. Divine purpose shapes what is possible, and the substrate itself intends nothing. Living directedness fits that account. The organism is not a little mind planning its own development. Its directedness is built into the form it has been given, which unfolds, through constrained chance and coordinated relation, toward the end that form is shaped for.

> `For thou hast possessed my reins: thou hast covered me in my mother's womb. I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvellous are thy works; and that my soul knoweth right well.`
> *Psalm 139:13-14*

The psalmist praises God for his formation in the womb. The language is of God's work, yet it is the ordinary process of development the psalmist describes. God's directing of a living thing's formation does not compete with the formation; it works through it.

## What life is not

Two boundaries close the chapter.

Life is not a special substance or force added to matter, a "vital spark" outside the order of created physical reality. On the relational account, living things are built from the same created order as stones, and their life is the most demanding coordination of that order's possibilities. There is no hidden life-fluid.

Nor is life *nothing but* chemistry. Organisation is real and explanatory, and the living whole is a genuine unity, not a heap. More importantly, Scripture insists that life is given and sustained by God. The account of living organisation describes how life operates. It does not make life self-explaining. The breath that God sends forth and takes away (Psalm 104:29-30) is not one more chemical in the cell.

This leaves living things where Genesis places them: real, active, fruitful, ordered after their kinds, and dependent on their Creator for every moment of their life. And it leaves the book at the threshold of the next category, the one Genesis marks with a third act of creation. Among the living things God made is one formed from the dust of the ground, into whose nostrils he breathed the breath of life. The next chapter turns to humanity.

# Chapter 11: Dust, Breath, and Person

Go back to the moment of seeing the stone. Light from its surface carried its distinctions to your eye, where they were received, and a cascade of signals passed along your nerves. Chapter 7 followed that chain as far as physical description can follow it. But the chain did not end in a nerve. It ended in *you*: in someone who saw a stone, recognised it, found it beautiful or dull, and decided to pick it up. Every link before that point is a transmission between configurations. The last one is different. The one who sees is not one more configuration in the chain. The one who sees is a person.

This chapter is about that person. It is the centre of the book's account of creation, as it is the climax of the creation account in Genesis. It is also the place where the distinction between categories, introduced in Chapter 1 and defended through every chapter since, matters most.

## Formed from the dust

Scripture describes the making of humanity in two complementary ways. Genesis 1 announces it as a new act of creation, the third use of *bara*:

> `And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.`
> *Genesis 1:26-27*

Genesis 2 shows the inner structure of that act:

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

The sequence is exact. First a body is *formed*, and it is formed from *dust*, the same material as the rest of the earth. Then *breath* is imparted, life given directly by God. Then the result: a *living soul*, a living person.

The word "dust" is not decoration. Humanity is not made from some special heavenly substance set apart from the rest of creation. It is formed from the same created order as the soil, the stone and the woodlouse. That is why the curse after the Fall can say "dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" (Genesis 3:19), and why the psalmist can say that God "knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14). In the terms of this book, the human body belongs fully to the relational physical order of Chapters 3 to 8 and to the living order of Chapter 10. It is a configuration in the substrate, persisting by constrained chance and coordinated relation, alive by the sustained organisation that every living thing shares.

But dust alone is not a person. The breath comes from God, and the living soul is the outcome of formed dust and imparted breath together. What distinguishes humanity from the rest of creation lies in what God has made it to be, and not in its material: an image-bearing, conscious, responsible person called into relation with him.

## Embodiment is good

The biblical account resists a temptation that has run through much religious and philosophical history: to treat the body as a container, or a prison, for the real self. Genesis does not present dust and breath as two opposed principles temporarily yoked together. It presents them as jointly making one living creature. The body is part of what a human person is, not an encumbrance to be escaped.

This is why Christian hope is the resurrection of the body (Chapter 14), and not the soul's escape from it. It is also why bodily life carries real moral weight. Limitation, vulnerability, time, place, dependence on others, the capacity to give oneself and the capacity to be wounded all belong to embodied existence. They are the conditions under which creaturely persons can love, keep faith and sacrifice. A human person is a person *by being embodied*.

## Personhood is deeper than embodiment

Yet Scripture also requires a careful distinction here, one that reasoning from ordinary experience alone would not discover. Every person we meet is embodied, and so it is natural to assume that being a person simply *is* being a certain kind of body. Christian faith confesses something that rules this out.

> `God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.`
> *John 4:24*

The Father is a person. The Son is a person. The Holy Spirit is a person. The Father and the Spirit have no bodies. The Son took a human body in the Incarnation, but he was a person before he did so, eternally. If personhood were constituted by embodiment, the doctrine of the Trinity would be incoherent. Since that doctrine is foundational, personhood must be the deeper category.

So the book distinguishes. A **person** is a centre of awareness, relation, agency, address and responsibility: one who can know and be known, speak and be spoken to, act and answer for the act. **Embodiment** is the mode in which creaturely persons exist and act within created order. For human persons, embodiment is not optional. It is the way we are persons, and Genesis 2:7 governs everything said about us. But personhood as such is not a property of bodies. Human persons are persons in a creaturely, embodied mode. God is personal without being embodied. In the Incarnation the eternal Son took the creaturely, embodied mode to himself, and it is now his forever.

This distinction is a clear example of Scripture shaping ontology. Philosophy working only from human cases would naturally define the person by the body. Scripture forces a more careful account, and the more careful account is also more coherent: it explains why persons cannot simply be reduced to configurations of matter, while honouring the body as the God-given mode of human personhood.

## The inner person

Scripture uses a consistent vocabulary for the inner life of a human person, the soul and the spirit, which is neither identical with the body nor detachable from it in this life:

> `For what man knoweth the things of a man, save the spirit of man which is in him?`
> *1 Corinthians 2:11*

> `The spirit of man is the candle of the LORD, searching all the inward parts of the belly.`
> *Proverbs 20:27*

> `But there is a spirit in man: and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth them understanding.`
> *Job 32:8*

> `Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.`
> *Ecclesiastes 12:7*

> `And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.`
> *Matthew 10:28*

> `And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.`
> *1 Thessalonians 5:23*

> `For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.`
> *2 Corinthians 4:16*

These texts do not give a technical theory of the soul, and Christians have long debated how best to describe the relation of soul and spirit to each other and to the body. The book does not settle those debates. But the texts establish several things together. The inner person is real. It knows itself as nothing else knows it. It is given by God and returns to God. It cannot be killed by those who can kill the body. And it can be renewed even while the outward person wastes away. The human person is not flat. It has depth, outward and inward, and the inward is not reducible to the outward.

Here the book must apply its own guard with full force. The soul is **not** a configuration of the relational substrate. The spirit is **not** a signal carried by a constrained correlation. Nothing in the physical account of Chapters 3 to 8 describes, explains or contains the soul. The physical order is the order in which embodied persons live and act, and the soul is not part of its furniture. When the book says that relation runs through every category of creation, it means that persons are relational beings in a creation that is relational from its foundation. It does not mean that persons are made of the same relations as stones.

## Consciousness

What, then, of consciousness, the felt awareness of seeing the stone, of its greyness, of its cold weight in the hand? Here the book must be more modest still.

Consciousness clearly participates in physical order. It depends on the body, on the brain, on the chain of transmissions that brings the world to us. Damage the brain, and consciousness is changed or lost. Alter its chemistry, and experience alters. A Christian account has every reason to take this seriously. We are dust, and our awareness is embodied.

But how physical processes relate to felt awareness, why there is anything it is like to see grey at all, is one of the hardest open questions in philosophy. Philosophers call it the hard problem of consciousness, and no account of physics, mainstream or relational, has solved it. **The Relational Substrate does not explain consciousness.** It claims something far smaller: a relational physical world is a more fitting setting for conscious persons than a world of sealed objects, because it is already a world in which differences become consequences for something else. But the step from consequence to awareness, from a changed molecule in the retina to the experience of seeing, is not taken by the substrate. The book does not pretend otherwise.

The book's position here is scriptural. The one who finally receives the seeing of the stone is a person, and persons are not one more stage in a physical chain. Physical transmission brings the world to the person. The person's knowing of it belongs to another category.

## Image, calling and relation

What makes humanity distinctive? Genesis 1:26-27 says that humanity is made in the image of God. The phrase has been interpreted in many ways through the history of the church: as reason, as moral capacity, as dominion over creation, as relationship with God, as the human calling to represent God in his world. The text itself joins image to dominion. Humanity is made in God's image *and* given a charge to rule and care for the earth.

The book's reading is broad and, it hopes, non-sectarian. The image of God is not a material feature of the human body. It is a status and a calling: humanity is made to know God and be known by him, to stand before him and answer to him, and to represent his care within creation. That is a relational reality from first to last. Animals are not called to account in Scripture; humans are. Animals are not addressed by God as "thou" and called to answer; humans are. The image of God is the deepest form of creaturely relation, the relation of a person to the God who made persons.

> `But now thus saith the LORD that created thee, O Jacob, and he that formed thee, O Israel, Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine.`
> *Isaiah 43:1*

This verse speaks to Israel, but its pattern reveals something about personal identity in general. What makes a person *this* person, and not another, is not finally a configuration of dust, however stable. It is known, called and named by God.

## The identity of a person

Chapter 6 proposed that physical things remain the same kind of thing by remaining within their relevant equivalence class through change, and the same individual through a continuing, history-bearing lineage. Human persons persist through bodily change in a way that resembles this. Much of the material of your body is replaced over the years, while you remain you. Your character, memories and commitments carry your history in your present, much as Chapter 6 suggested configuration can carry memory.

The resemblance is real and illuminating. It shows why personal identity does not depend on keeping the same atoms, and why the replacement of material is no threat to who you are. But it is a structural analogy, not the definition of a person. A person's identity is not merely an equivalence class of their bodily configuration, because a person is not merely a configuration. Personal identity is grounded more deeply: in the continuity of the inner person Scripture describes, and ultimately in God's knowing and calling. This is why, as Chapter 14 will argue, death does not end a person, and why resurrection is not the making of a copy but the raising of the same person. Continuity of equivalence helps us see why the replacement of bodily material need not threaten personal identity. It cannot carry the whole weight of what a person is.

## Persons in a relational creation

The chapter can end where Chapter 9 left the argument. Human beings live lives saturated with relation: they are born into families, formed by others, bound by promises, called to love, held responsible, and addressed by God. On an object-first picture of the physical world, that relational life would sit uneasily on a foundation of sealed things. On the relational picture, the human person, formed from dust that is itself relational at its base, is at home in creation. The person is not reducible to the dust. The dust is simply the kind of order in which persons can live.

That is the place of humanity in this ontology: continuous in form with the whole creation, distinct in kind from everything else in it, and uniquely related to its Creator. It also brings the book to the question every serious account of persons must eventually face. If persons are responsible, what are they responsible *for*? What is it for something to be allowed, or right, or wrong? That is the subject of the next chapter.

# Chapter 12: What Is Allowed

A surgeon draws a scalpel across a patient's skin. An assailant draws a knife across a victim's skin. Physically, the two acts may be almost identical: the same sharp edge, the same depth, the same parting of tissue. One is an act of healing and the other a crime. Nothing in the physics of the cut tells you which is which.

The previous chapters described a world in which some things are possible and others are not, and in which the possible is shaped before anything happens. Persons now enter that world as agents. They act, choose and answer for what they do. This raises a question that a careless use of the word "admissible" could easily confuse: when we say that something is allowed, what kind of allowing do we mean?

## Three kinds of "allowed"

Chapter 4 introduced **admissibility** as whether a transition is physically possible at all, and promised that the word would carry only that sense until this chapter. Here the promise is kept by making a distinction. There are three quite different questions that the word "allowed" can ask:

- **Is it physically possible?** Can this happen at all, within created order? This is *physical admissibility*, the sense used since Chapter 4. If the answer is no, the act is impossible for anyone.
- **Is it within this agent's power?** Can this particular agent bring it about? This is *operational admissibility*. If the answer is no, the act is possible in principle but inaccessible to this agent.
- **Is it right?** Should this act be done? This is *moral admissibility*. If the answer is no, the act may be both possible and within the agent's power, and still wrong.

The three questions are independent, and the most serious confusions about human action come from running them together. Lying is physically possible: speech can carry false statements as easily as true ones. It is within ordinary human power: anyone who can speak can lie. And it is wrong. Sustaining creation in being is physically admissible in the sense that it occurs, but it is within no creature's power; it belongs to God alone. Creating something beautiful is possible, within power and good. The scalpel and the knife are equally possible and equally within the power of their wielders; what divides them is the moral question.

The first edition of this book named three characteristic mistakes that follow from collapsing these layers, and they are worth repeating because they are so common.

- **The naturalist mistake** collapses the moral into the physical: if it is possible, and nature produces it, it cannot really be wrong.
- **The pietist mistake** collapses the physical into the moral: if it is wrong, God will not let it happen.
- **The technological mistake** collapses the physical into the operational: if we can now do it, it must be in the nature of things that we should.

Each loses a real distinction, and each has done real harm.

## Why the physical cannot settle the moral

This distinction is the book's firmest safeguard against misreading Chapter 9. That chapter argued that a relational physical world removes the gulf between the material creation and a human life in which consequence, relation and responsibility are fundamental. It is now possible to say exactly what that argument does and does not imply.

It does imply that moral acts are real events in the world. When you lie, a falsehood is sent out, carried and received. It becomes a change in someone who now believes something untrue and acts on it. When you keep a promise, a commitment carried across days is honoured in a way that has real consequences for another. The relational physics of Chapters 3 to 8 describes the *kind of world* in which such acts can be real and consequential. Moral life is not a ghostly overlay on a world of sealed objects that it cannot touch.

It does not imply that physical admissibility, or any physical fact, settles moral admissibility. The physics of a lie and the physics of a true statement are the same physics. A relational account of the world can tell you that your words will be received and will have consequences. It cannot tell you that you ought to tell the truth. The moral question belongs to a different category, the category of persons answerable to one another and to God, and it is answered by a different authority.

> `He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?`
> *Micah 6:8*

> `For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another.`
> *Romans 2:14-15*

What is good has been *shown*. It is revealed by God and written on the heart, and it is not deduced from the behaviour of matter. Moral admissibility is a real layer of created order, with real content, and its source is the will and character of God, not the physics of the substrate.

## Alignment

If the moral layer is real, what does it mean for a person or an act to be in or out of order with it? The book uses the word **alignment**: fitting the order a thing or person is made for.

For things, alignment is simple to picture. A bone set straight heals aligned. A string tuned to its pitch is aligned with the instrument's design. For persons, alignment is a moral and relational reality, not a measurement: it is living in right relation to God, to others and to the creation one is set within. Justice, mercy, faithfulness, truthfulness, love: these are what alignment looks like in persons.

The word needs one guard. The first edition of this book proposed that beauty, order, truth and goodness are "four perspectives on the same structural property". That went too far. It risked turning moral and aesthetic realities into a feature of physical configuration, which is exactly the collapse this chapter has argued against. It also contradicted the technical volume, which says plainly that alignment as a structural notion is not a synonym for goodness. This edition withdraws it. Goodness, truth and beauty are related, and in God they are one, but in creation they are not reducible to a single physical measure. When the book speaks of a person's alignment, it is speaking of a moral and relational condition, described in the language of Scripture, not of a quantity the substrate possesses.

## Agency and responsibility

Moral admissibility only makes sense where there is genuine personal agency: persons who act, respond and are answerable for what they do. Without that, love would be mechanism, obedience would be automation, and moral praise and blame would be category mistakes. Christian traditions differ on what such agency requires. Some hold that responsibility requires that a person could genuinely have done otherwise. Others hold that real responsibility is compatible with God's sovereign ordering of all things, so long as persons act willingly from their own desires and reasons. This book does not decide between them.

Scripture presents human beings as genuinely able to choose, and as held responsible for their choices:

> `I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live.`
> *Deuteronomy 30:19*

How human freedom relates to God's sovereignty is among the oldest questions in Christian theology, and different traditions answer it differently. This book does not resolve it. It does observe one thing within its own subject, and it is a limited observation. A world of constrained possibility, in which actualisation is not fixed in advance at every level (Chapter 5), does not settle the question of human freedom in either direction. Constrained chance is not freedom. A coin toss is not a choice, and the book makes no claim that human agency is a matter of molecular chance. Physics cannot prove free will, and the relational ontology does not try to. Agency belongs to persons, in their own category.

## Boundaries that free

It is common today to think of every moral boundary as a restriction on freedom, and of freedom as the absence of constraint. Chapter 4 argued that in the physical world, constraint is generative: a river needs its banks. Scripture says the same of the moral world, and says it more emphatically:

> `The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes.`
> *Psalm 19:7-8*

> `And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts.`
> *Psalm 119:45*

> `For the commandment is a lamp; and the law is light; and reproofs of instruction are the way of life.`
> *Proverbs 6:23*

Law, testimony, statute and commandment are each a constraint, and each is described as giving life, wisdom, joy and light. Liberty is found *in* seeking God's precepts, not in escaping them. This is not a paradox once the structure is seen. A boundary rightly placed does not shrink the space of good action. It is what gives the space its shape, so that a person can grow within it into what they are made to be.

Here a structural analogy is again at work, and again it must be held as analogy. The constraint that shapes a crystal and the commandment that shapes a life share a form: a boundary that makes possible rather than merely forbidding. They do not share a nature. The commandment is spoken by a Person to persons and is received in conscience, trust and obedience. The crystal's constraint is given in the order of matter and received by nothing. The likeness is real, and it is one of the ways creation bears witness to its Maker. But a commandment is not a law of physics, and a law of physics is not a commandment.

## The question that follows

Persons, then, live in a world where much is possible, a good deal is within their power, and not all of it is right. They can align themselves with the order they are made for, or they can turn against it. Scripture says plainly that they have turned against it, and that the whole creation bears the consequences. The next chapter asks what that turning, corruption, is, and how a relational account of the world can help describe it without either exaggerating or excusing it.

# Chapter 13: Corruption

Any account of reality that speaks only of order soon becomes unbelievable. The stone on the path is beautiful in the morning light, but the path also has litter on it, the river it follows may be poisoned, and the person who sees the stone may be grieving, or bitter, or lying to someone they love. The world is good, and it is also deeply wrong. This chapter asks what that wrongness is, within the account of creation this book has given.

## Real, but not ultimate

Scripture does not soften the reality of corruption:

> `Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned.`
> *Romans 5:12*

> `For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope… For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now.`
> *Romans 8:20, 22*

These texts rule out two opposite mistakes. One treats disorder as ultimate, so that goodness is a temporary illusion in a world that is finally chaos or cruelty. The other treats disorder as superficial, a minor malfunction that a serious account of reality can largely ignore. Scripture allows neither. Creation is good, real and meaningful. Corruption is also real, grievous and historically consequential.

The way these fit together has been understood in Christian thought since Augustine. Corruption is not a rival substance standing beside the good, and it is not a second creator. It is **parasitic**: a distortion of what is good, which has no being of its own and exists only by deforming something that God made. Evil cannot create; it can only spoil. That is why the book speaks of corruption as *distortion*, *misalignment* and *rupture*, words that presuppose an order being distorted, misaligned or ruptured.

## Corruption as persisting misalignment

Chapter 12 described alignment as fitting the order one is made for. Misalignment, a departure from that order, is not by itself the whole of corruption. Every growing creature departs from its mature form, and every learner makes mistakes. A wrong act is wrong in itself, whether or not it is ever repeated: a single lie, swiftly repented of, was still a lie, and a single act of cruelty is still cruel. Its wrongness lies in what it is and whom it wrongs, judged by the moral order of Chapter 12, not in how long it lasts. What persistence explains is something else: how wrongdoing **entrenches**. Misalignment that is not restored persists and spreads, hardening into habit, narrowing what a person can see and choose, and reaching into others. Corruption, in the full sense this chapter describes, includes both the wrong and its entrenchment.

Scripture describes sin in exactly these terms: not as a series of isolated lapses but as a trajectory that hardens, enslaves and narrows:

> `But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin.`
> *Hebrews 3:13*

> `Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin.`
> *John 8:34*

> `But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed. Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and when sin is finished, it bringeth forth death.`
> *James 1:14-15*

Lust conceives and brings forth sin. Sin, finished, brings forth death. Sin hardens and enslaves. Misalignment that is not restored does not stay where it is. It propagates, entrenches and steadily narrows the very capacity that might have turned it back.

Here a structural analogy from Chapter 6 becomes useful, held carefully as analogy. Chapter 6 proposed, as a hypothesis, that the past can live on in present configuration, changing what is likely or possible next. The bent paper clip carries its bending in its crystal structure. Something with the same form is visible in human character: a habit of deceit makes the next deceit easier and honesty harder, and a grievance nursed reshapes what a person is able to see. Scripture's "hardened" is a strikingly physical word for this. The book does not claim that sin *is* a configuration of the substrate. Sin belongs to persons, in their relation to God, and is not a physical state. But the form of corruption, a persisting past that narrows the future, is the same form that runs through history-bearing physical order. In that sense corruption works with the grain of a relational world, against the purpose for which that world was made.

## Corruption in transmission

The grammar of Chapter 7 gives a second lens, again used only where it clarifies.

Much human wrongdoing is wrongdoing in what is *transmitted*. A lie sends out a false distinction: a claim that the world is otherwise than it is, offered to be received and acted on. Slander sends out a false distinction about a person. A broken promise breaks a carriage that was relied on: a commitment that should have persisted from the day it was made to the day it was due, and did not. Gossip distorts in carriage, so that what arrives is no longer what was released. Scripture's long catalogue of sins of the tongue reflects how much of human corruption runs through what people send to one another:

> `Death and life are in the power of the tongue: and they that love it shall eat the fruit thereof.`
> *Proverbs 18:21*

Corruption can also be a failure of *reception*. Chapter 7 distinguished arrival from reception: a letter can arrive unopened. Scripture's prophets lament a people to whom God's word arrived again and again without being received:

> `And now, because ye have done all these works, saith the LORD, and I spake unto you, rising up early and speaking, but ye heard not; and I called you, but ye answered not.`
> *Jeremiah 7:13*

To hear and not heed, to be warned and not change, to be loved and not respond: these are failures of reception, in which a difference that should have become a consequence in someone was refused. The image will matter in the next chapter, because restoration includes the restoring of reception: ears opened, hearts softened, a word that comes "not … unto you in word only, but also in power" (1 Thessalonians 1:5).

These lenses must not become a theory of everything. Not all corruption is usefully described as transmission, and a vocabulary that tried to describe every sin in the same technical phrase would trivialise sin. They are offered because they sometimes help us see a pattern Scripture already describes, not because they explain it.

## Five patterns of corruption

The first edition described five recurring patterns by which misalignment persists without restoration. They remain useful, and they appear at every scale of creaturely life.

**Degradation** is the slow thinning of what should hold: a body wasting from hunger, a friendship starved of attention, a building whose upkeep has been deferred for years, a character that has stopped practising the disciplines that formed it. What is needed is sustained input that is not arriving.

**Fragmentation** is the breaking of a whole into parts no longer in real relation: a family whose members have stopped speaking, a community split into factions that no longer listen across their divides, a body whose immune system attacks its own tissues. The coupling that made a whole has failed.

**Inversion** is the reversal of right order, where what should serve comes to rule: a government that treats its people as instruments of its own power, a school run for its administrators rather than its pupils, a love that has become one person's use of another.

**Counterfeit** keeps the form of a good while betraying its substance: virtue performed for an audience, worship attended without worship, a leader who uses the language of service while gathering power. The appearance continues as though the substance were still there, and the deception compounds the loss.

**Parasitism** sustains itself by feeding on a good it cannot generate: a predatory loan that consumes the savings of those it claims to serve, a manipulative relationship that draws on another's trust without return, a system that consumes attention and gives nothing back. It persists only by weakening the host it depends on.

The five are distinguishable but not separate. A false institution may be counterfeit and inverted at once. A wounded body may suffer degradation and fragmentation together. What unites them is the logic of parasitic corruption. None is creative. All depend on an order still functioning around them. Each persists by distorting what was made for something better.

## Suffering and sin

Corruption must be spoken of directly, without the anaesthetic of abstraction. People suffer pain, loss, exhaustion, grief, fear, injustice and decay. Scripture gives voice to all of it:

> `My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?`
> *Psalm 22:1*

> `Man that is born of a woman is of few days, and full of trouble.`
> *Job 14:1*

> `Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.`
> *Ecclesiastes 1:2*

These texts do not deny that creation is good. They speak from within a good creation under corruption.

And human beings are not only victims of corruption. They are agents of it:

> `All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.`
> *Isaiah 53:6*

> `For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God.`
> *Romans 3:23*

Any account of corruption that described it only as impersonal process, as decay or entropy or breakdown, would miss what Scripture insists on. Corruption is also personal, moral and culpable. The relational account helps here only in a limited way. It shows how personal wrongdoing can have consequences that reach far beyond the person: into others, into communities and into the land, as Hosea saw (Chapter 9). It cannot make sin less personal than it is, and it does not try to.

## Limitation is not corruption

One distinction prevents a great deal of confusion. Not every limit is corruption. Creaturehood itself includes finitude. Human beings are not God. They need sleep, they know in part, they occupy one place at a time, they depend on others. None of this is a defect. It is the shape of being a creature, and Genesis calls it very good.

If every limit were treated as evil, creaturehood itself would become a fault, and the only salvation would be to stop being a creature. Scripture's hope is quite different: creatures restored and glorified as creatures. Conversely, if corruption were collapsed into limitation, the grievous wrongness of death, cruelty and injustice would be softened into mere finitude. The book holds the two apart. Limitation is good creaturehood. Corruption is the distortion of good creaturehood.

## Darkness, again

Chapter 1 insisted that the darkness of Genesis 1:2 is not evil. On the reading given there, it is a real, created condition of the world before light, not yet made manifest, and not malign. That distinction must hold even now. Later Scripture uses darkness for ignorance, concealment, judgment and sin, and rightly. But moral darkness is not a substance created alongside light. It is something more like *hiddenness turned to disorder*: the created capacity for concealment, which in God's purpose can be a place of rest, mystery and reverence, seized for evasion, deceit and revolt. "Men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19). The darkness they love is not the darkness of Genesis 1. It is that darkness misused.

## Remembrance

If corruption is misalignment that persists and hardens, what holds a people aligned over time? Scripture is strikingly insistent on one answer: remembrance.

> `Remember the days of old, consider the years of many generations: ask thy father, and he will shew thee; thy elders, and they will tell thee.`
> *Deuteronomy 32:7*

> `And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me.`
> *Luke 22:19*

The Passover, the memorial stones, the appointed feasts, the written law, the retelling of the exodus and the Lord's Supper are not incidental rituals. They are the means by which a people's alignment is renewed across generations. Each is a deliberate, repeated return to the order the people belong to. In the terms of this book, remembrance is how a people keeps carrying what was given to it, so that what God did long ago is received again, in each generation, as a present reality. Forgetting is one of the ordinary roads into the slow drift that becomes corruption.

## Corruption is not the last word

Corruption is real, personal, parasitic and spreading. It is not ultimate. It has no being of its own, and it cannot create. Everything it touches remains God's creation, wounded but not abolished. That is why the question of the next chapter is not idle: if creation is wounded but not abolished, what would its healing be, and on what ground could anyone hope for it?

# Chapter 14: Restoration

Human beings are capable of optimism, distraction, denial and private consolation. None of these is hope. Hope, in the strong sense, requires that reality itself contain more promise than corruption can destroy. If disorder is ultimate, hope is theatre. If creation is God's, and God remains its Lord, hope may be the most realistic stance there is.

This chapter asks what restoration means within the account the book has given, and on what ground it can be hoped for. Its first claim is a boundary, and everything else depends on it.

## Restoration is God's act

Chapter 6 drew a lesson: preserving an equivalence class is not the same as repairing it. When information was destroyed, nothing in the system brought it back. Conservation kept what remained. It did not restore what was lost. Chapter 10 showed how living things partly overcome this, by carrying templates from which damage can be repaired. But that capacity is itself limited, and it fails in the end: every living thing dies.

So the healing of creation cannot be expected from creation's own resources. If restoration came only from the persistence of the world's invariants, there would be no restoration of what corruption has destroyed: no raising of the dead, no righting of wrongs, no new creation. Scripture places restoration elsewhere, in the action of God:

> `For it pleased the Father that in him should all fulness dwell; And, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself; by him, I say, whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven.`
> *Colossians 1:19-20*

The One through whom all things were made (Colossians 1:16) and in whom all things consist (1:17) is the One through whom all things are reconciled. Redemption is not alien to creation. It is the Creator's answer to the wounding of his own work. But it is *his* answer. It is not a process inside the substrate, a self-correction of physical order, or the outworking of some law of nature.

This is why Chapter 4 insisted that God is not one more cause among the causes of the world, and that the book offers no account of how God acts in miracles, providence or redemption. The relational ontology describes created runtime order: how things ordinarily persist, transmit and change. God's redeeming work is not ordinary runtime process. The resurrection of Christ was not an unusually improbable configuration of the substrate. It was an act of the Creator. When Scripture records miracles, the book receives them as Scripture records them. It does not explain them as hidden physics, and it does not explain them away.

## Hope must be real

Scripture's hope is not that souls might escape a ruined world. It is that God will renew the world:

> `Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God.`
> *Romans 8:21*

> `And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new.`
> *Revelation 21:3-5*

> `Nevertheless we, according to his promise, look for new heavens and a new earth, wherein dwelleth righteousness.`
> *2 Peter 3:13*

> `The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid; and the calf and the young lion and the fatling together; and a little child shall lead them… They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain: for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.`
> *Isaiah 11:6, 9*

The scope is not modest, and it is not only human. *The creature itself* is delivered. *All things* are reconciled. *New heavens and a new earth* are promised. What corruption has fragmented, the wolf and the lamb, is brought together. What destroys is removed. The earth is filled with the knowledge of God.

## Why a relational creation matters for hope

Chapter 9 argued that a relational physical world removes the gulf between the material creation and the relational life Scripture describes. That argument has its fullest consequence here.

If the physical world were a collection of sealed objects with no intrinsic bearing on persons, then the redemption of persons and the renewal of creation would be two unrelated projects. One would be spiritual and the other cosmic, and it would be hard to see why the second should matter to the first. Some forms of Christianity have drifted toward exactly that, imagining salvation as the rescue of souls from a material world destined for the scrap heap. Scripture does not allow it. The whole creation groans *with* humanity and waits *for* the revealing of the children of God (Romans 8:19-22). Its deliverance and theirs belong together.

On the relational account, that belonging is not a puzzle. Persons are formed from the dust of a creation that is relational from its foundation, so their wounding and their healing are bound up with the creation they belong to. The restoration of persons and the renewal of creation are one work of one God in one world. Again, the relational ontology does not *explain* redemption. It shows why the redemption Scripture describes is redemption of *this* world, and not of some other.

## Restoring reception

Chapter 13 described corruption as, among other things, a failure of reception: a word that arrives and is not heard. Scripture describes restoration as, among other things, the restoring of reception:

> `A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh.`
> *Ezekiel 36:26*

> `For our gospel came not unto you in word only, but also in power, and in the Holy Ghost, and in much assurance.`
> *1 Thessalonians 1:5*

A heart of stone does not receive; a heart of flesh does. The gospel that comes "in word only" has arrived. The gospel that comes "in power, and in the Holy Ghost" has been received. This is a structural analogy, and the book holds it as one. The Spirit's work in giving a new heart is not a physical process, and faith is not a change of state in a receptor. Nor is hearing the gospel a transaction that must be recorded once, like a payment; Scripture speaks of a word received, rehearsed, remembered and received again. What the analogy shares is only the outline: something sent, carried and heard, and finally received as a change in the one who hears. That outline runs through the whole of creation. It is fitting that the God whose word "shall not return unto me void" (Isaiah 55:11) should, in redemption, make his creatures able to receive it.

## The same person, raised

The central hope of Christian faith is resurrection, and it is a hope about *identity*.

> `Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have.`
> *Luke 24:39*

> `So also is the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:42-44*

> `Marvel not at this: for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, And shall come forth; they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation.`
> *John 5:28-29*

The risen Christ is recognisably himself: *it is I myself*, with the wounds of the crucifixion. The resurrection of the dead is the raising of the same persons, transformed, not the manufacture of replacements. And Scripture is plain that it concerns everyone, the righteous and the unrighteous alike, to life or to judgment.

How can the same person be raised, when the body has returned to dust? Chapter 6 offered an account of how physical things remain the same kind of thing, by remaining within their relevant equivalence class through admissible change. It also drew a sharper line. Something reconstructed to be indistinguishable from what was lost may belong to the same equivalence class without being the same individual, and when what carries identity is destroyed, conservation cannot bring it back. Chapter 11 added that a person's identity is not merely an invariant of bodily configuration, but is grounded in the continuity of the inner person and in God's knowing and calling.

Put together, these give an honest answer. Resurrection is not the substrate preserving a person's invariants through death, and it is not the reconstruction of an indistinguishable replacement. Death destroys the configuration, and the dust returns to the earth (Ecclesiastes 12:7). The identity of the person raised is held by God. The spirit "shall return unto God who gave it". God, who calls his people by name, does not lose track of whom he has called. The book makes no claim about the mechanism of resurrection, and Scripture gives none: "it doth not yet appear what we shall be" (1 John 3:2). What the relational account contributes is a negative clarity. It shows exactly why resurrection cannot be a natural process of created order, and therefore why it must be, as Scripture says it is, the act of God.

## Human death and the Cross

Scripture presents human death as bound up with sin (Romans 5:12) and as an enemy to be destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26). Human death is not humanity's intended created end. Christians who share this book's commitments read the wider history of the natural world, including animal mortality, in different ways, and the book does not settle that question. It holds the narrower scriptural claim: the death of persons is not what God made them for.

The death of Christ is presented in a striking way within that account. In the ordinary case, death is corruption prevailing over a creature that cannot resist it. The Gospels present Christ's death as yielded:

> `Therefore doth my Father love me, because I lay down my life, that I might take it again. No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again.`
> *John 10:17-18*

> `When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.`
> *John 19:30*

The suffering was real and terrible, and the death was real. But it was not corruption overtaking him. It was a life laid down when the work was finished. The psalm the apostles applied to the resurrection makes the point:

> `For thou wilt not leave my soul in hell; neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption.`
> *Psalm 16:10*

The ordinary sequence, death and then the decay that death lets loose, was interrupted in him. Corruption did not win even at the Cross.

## Restored alignment can be higher than before

One of the first edition's most valuable reflections concerned what restoration produces, and it deserves to be kept in condensed form. It is offered as theological and pastoral reflection, drawn from Scripture and from Christian experience, not as a doctrine binding on every reader and not as a claim that evil was necessary. A restored life is not merely returned to where it was before the damage. It can become something richer.

Innocence that has never been tested is real goodness, but it is unowned. Its loyalties are untried and its weaknesses hidden, because nothing has exposed them. When disorder comes, it exposes what was hidden. If it is then truthfully faced, repented of and repaired, the result can be an alignment that is conscious, tested, freely chosen and humbler than before. A marriage repaired after a serious rupture, a faith that has come through honest doubt, and a community rebuilt after a wound can each hold a kind of coherence that could not exist without the wound and the repair together. Scripture describes this pattern repeatedly:

> `Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby.`
> *Hebrews 12:11*

> `And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope.`
> *Romans 5:3-4*

> `But he knoweth the way that I take: when he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold.`
> *Job 23:10*

Four clarifications keep this from being misheard.

First, disorder is not good in itself, suffering is not valuable in itself, and evil is not God's means of producing good. The higher alignment comes only when disorder is truthfully confronted and repaired, "unto them which are exercised thereby". Otherwise corruption simply deepens. God redeems evil that has occurred. That is a different and greater claim than that evil was needed.

Second, restoration is costly. At the scale of a single life it costs repentance and painful exposure. At the scale of creation, it cost the Cross:

> `For when we were yet without strength, in due time Christ died for the ungodly.`
> *Romans 5:6*

> `Forasmuch as ye know that ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold… But with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot.`
> *1 Peter 1:18-19*

Third, the freedom that makes owned alignment possible is a freedom to love and trust God, not a licence to corrupt. Faith must be a genuine, willing response of the person, however the traditions describe the freedom that involves, and the possibility of turning away is not thereby a good one. Faith is the creature's willing trust in a God it does not yet see in full, extended across the very gap that testing opens:

> `But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him.`
> *Hebrews 11:6*

Fourth, Genesis itself shows that freedom never required evil to be actualised. Before the Fall the command was real, and so was the possibility of disobeying it:

> `And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat: But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.`
> *Genesis 2:16-17*

Yet the creation in which that command stood had already been pronounced good without reserve:

> `And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good.`
> *Genesis 1:31*

A genuine moral choice was present in a creation that contained no actualised evil. So moral agency does not need evil as one of its ingredients, and the good creation was complete without it. What restoration achieves after the Fall is God's answer to evil that did occur, not the fulfilment of a plan that needed it. Whether humanity, had it obeyed, would have come to know good and evil in some other and rightful way is an interesting theological possibility. The text does not say so, and this book offers it only as speculation.

## Consummation, not return

Scripture's hope is therefore not simply the restoration of Eden. It is consummation: a creation in which corruption has been judged, paid for and removed, and in which creatures whose alignment with God has been tested and freely chosen are confirmed in it. Death is not merely postponed; it is swallowed up:

> `So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:54*

Judgment belongs to this hope, not against it. If corruption is real, hope cannot consist merely in soothing those who suffer while leaving evil uninterpreted. It includes the exposure of lies, the defeat of wickedness and the vindication of truth. Scripture is sober about the outcome for those who refuse: "these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal" (Matthew 25:46). Without judgment, redemption would be sentimental. With it, redemption is morally serious.

## A foretaste in the gathered people of God

Between the resurrection of Christ and the renewal of all things, Scripture places the gathered people of God. In concrete local assemblies, believers pray, worship, teach, correct, suffer and serve together. These assemblies do not bring in the new creation; that is God's work. But they can be real, if imperfect, places where restored relation becomes visible: where truth is spoken and received, wrongs are confessed and forgiven, and remembrance (Chapter 13) is kept. The book does not attempt a doctrine of the church, and readers from different traditions will fill this out differently. It notes only that the restoration Scripture promises has a present, embodied, communal foretaste, not only a future one.

## The ground of hope

The final answer to corruption is not intellectual elegance, and it is not a better ontology. It is the Lord who creates, judges, redeems and renews.

> `For all the promises of God in him are yea, and in him Amen, unto the glory of God by us.`
> *2 Corinthians 1:20*

> `He which testifieth these things saith, Surely I come quickly. Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus.`
> *Revelation 22:20*

What the ontology can do, and has tried to do, is show that this hope is about the same world we inhabit: the world of stones and light, of living things and persons, of promises and wounds. A relational creation, wounded but not abolished, is the kind of world in which the redemption Scripture describes can be redemption of everything that is real. The last chapter gathers the whole picture together.

# Chapter 15: One World, Many Kinds

The stone is still on the path. Nothing about it has changed since the first page. What has changed, the book hopes, is how much can be seen in it.

It is a creature, made by God and held by him, not divine and not self-explaining. On the proposal this book has developed, it is a stable form within a created order of relation: real and distinct, but constituted within relation rather than sealed off from it. Its possible forms were bounded and weighted before any became actual, and those bounds were shaped by purpose, though the stone itself intends nothing. Its actual history unfolded by constrained chance. It persists because the changes it undergoes preserve what it is. It comes to you because a difference at its surface constrains the light, is preserved by a chain of dependency, and becomes a consequence in you. Its behaviour, and the light's, is regular enough to be written in exact mathematics, because countless open events, constrained and coordinated, add up to a reliable whole.

And you, who see it, are formed from the same dust, alive by the same gift that gives life to the woodlouse beneath it, and yet a person: known and called by God, answerable for what you do, capable of keeping or breaking a promise, wounded by corruption and offered restoration. You belong to the same world as the stone, and you are not the same kind of thing.

## The shape of the whole

Gathered together, the book's account runs like this.

God creates reality. Created physical order is real, and it is not self-grounding: it holds together because it is held. The deepest physical ontology the Relational Substrate proposes is relational rather than object-first. Actualisation is conditioned before anything happens, both by what is forbidden and by what is favoured. The book calls those conditions T0 and reads them as constrained possibility, while leaving open what kind of thing they finally are. What has already happened shapes what is currently reachable. Actualisation is, on the book's working hypothesis, fundamentally stochastic: consistent with quantum theory, though not established by it. Persistence, identity and regularity arise through structured relational continuity. A thing remains the same *kind* of thing by preserving its relevant equivalence class, judged by what reachable futures could tell apart, with invariants as its certificates and provenance as part of it where it still matters. Whether it is the same *individual* is a further question, of continuity and lineage, which remains open. Regularity is the stable behaviour of many chance events, constrained and coordinated in the right way. Transmit, carry and receive name the grammar by which any distinction becomes consequential to another state: a distinction capable of constraining beyond itself, a dependency that preserves it, and a consequence that depends on it. The book proposes that this is the form of consequential relation itself, and that cause, in a chancy world, is a distinction modifying what another state can become. Stable global behaviour does not require every underlying transition to be determined in advance. Mathematics describes that stable behaviour, often with astonishing precision, without thereby becoming the reality it describes.

Because material reality is relational from its foundation, the physical world does not begin as a scatter of metaphysically isolated objects that must somehow acquire relation later. That matters for global coherence. Life, personhood, morality, consequence, corruption and restoration need not be bolted onto a physical world of a different kind. Yet material, biological, personal and spiritual categories remain genuinely distinct. Relation runs through all of them, and relation across categories does not erase category. The substrate is not the soul. Transmission is not revelation. A law of physics is not a commandment. Creation is not God.

Observation, measurement and successful mathematics strongly constrain what can responsibly be claimed about the physical world. Empirical description alone does not exhaust ontology, and the metaphysical pictures that travel with it are proposals to be tested like any other. Scripture provides the governing frame within which this book seeks coherence. The whole project is not an empirical proof of ultimate ontology. It is an attempt to construct the most globally coherent account available, while remaining honest about observation, mathematics, experiment, metaphysics, uncertainty and revelation.

## What kind of confidence this deserves

The account contains claims of very different strength, and the reader is owed a final sorting of them.

Some of it the book asserts because Scripture asserts it: creation, the Creator–creature distinction, the reality of persons, corruption and restoration, the resurrection. These are held as commitments of faith, received rather than derived, and the book has tried not to make Scripture say more than it says.

Some of it is observation, measurement and well-tested mathematics. Physics describes the world with extraordinary precision. Quantum predictions are probabilistic. Deterministic large-scale laws can be the stable behaviour of countless chancy small-scale events. None of this is the book's own, and none of it is in dispute here. What the book does dispute is that any one interpretation of these findings, including the familiar picture of independent objects in a container of space, inherits their authority.

Some of it is philosophical argument. Description does not by itself settle ontology. Every account starts from assumptions. Structural analogy is not identity. And, most importantly, global coherence is a stronger test of a worldview than local success. These the book has argued for, and they stand or fall with the arguments.

And some of it is the Relational Substrate proposal itself: that relation is foundational, that actualisation is constrained chance, that relational equivalence can preserve sameness of kind through change (individual continuity being a further question of lineage), that T-C-R names something basic about how created order is connected. These are hypotheses. The project's models have shown them to be coherent and workable, and they do not show that nature works this way. The models have also marked limits: the simplest relational rules give no account of gravity's effect on clocks (Chapter 8). The book's case for them is the case of Chapter 9: that together they make the whole of what we know hang together better than the alternative, with no gulf between the physical world and the human one.

That combination calls for what the first edition called *disciplined confidence*: confidence where the grounds are strong, openness where they are not, and honesty about which is which. Appendix E records the current status of every major claim in one place, together with the questions that remain open and the readings the book rejects.

## The limits of creaturely knowing

There is a deeper reason for that discipline, and Scripture gives it.

> `Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.`
> *Job 38:4*

> `For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known.`
> *1 Corinthians 13:12*

Human beings know from inside creation. They cannot step outside it and see its foundations from the vantage point of its Maker. Whatever the deepest order of the world is, we meet it through its effects, through instruments and inference, through a glass. That is not a defect to be overcome by enough research. It is part of being a creature. It is also why this book has insisted that its ontology is a coherence argument, not a proof, and why Scripture, which comes to us from the One who laid the foundations, is the frame within which the argument is made.

But Job's question is not a rebuke to inquiry. The same book that asks "where wast thou?" catalogues the wonders of creation in loving detail, and the God who asks it invites Job to look. "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter" (Proverbs 25:2). Partial knowledge is still knowledge. To search out the order of creation, while knowing that we see in part, is one of the honours God gives to creatures made in his image.

## Living in a relational world

If the world is as described here, some practical consequences follow. They are not deductions from physics, which would contradict Chapter 12, but ways of living that fit the grain of the world.

Science is to be honoured. It studies a real, ordered, intelligible creation, and its successes are possible precisely because creation is not chaos. It is also to be held in its place: it describes how the created order behaves, and the questions of what that order is, what it is for and how we should live in it are not answered by description alone.

Consequence is to be taken seriously. In a relational world, what we say and do goes out from us, is carried, and is received in others and in the creation around us. Words, promises, neglect and care all travel. Responsibility is not an arbitrary overlay. It answers to real effects flowing through a real order.

Wisdom is more than technique. A civilisation can learn to calculate brilliantly while forgetting what its calculations are about, and to build powerfully while forgetting what its buildings are for.

> `Through wisdom is an house builded; and by understanding it is established: And by knowledge shall the chambers be filled with all precious and pleasant riches.`
> *Proverbs 24:3-4*

Cleverness can build a tower against the heavens. Wisdom asks first what is being built and why.

And creation is to be cared for, because it is not a stage set for human drama but a creation that groans with us and waits with us for its deliverance. The land mourns when its people lie and kill (Hosea 4:3). It will rejoice when its Lord comes (Psalm 96:12-13).

## The end of the matter

A book about ontology can end in abstraction, and this one should not. The whole account, with all its distinctions and cautions, points beyond itself: to the One who made the stone and the light and the eye and the person who sees, who holds all things together, who has not abandoned what he made, and who has promised to make all things new.

> `Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil.`
> *Ecclesiastes 12:13-14*

The framework ends there, not because it is unimportant, but because that is where everything it describes finally rests.

# Appendix A: Points of Divergence

The body of this book builds a positive account and spends little time on alternatives. This appendix gathers the main points at which the account diverges from other serious views of reality. Each view is stated as fairly as space allows, together with where and why this book parts company with it. Several of these views have been held by thinkers of great ability, and the book does not regard their holders as foolish. The divergences fall into four groups: views of the world's origin and ground, views of what matter is, views of persons, and views of knowledge.

## Origin and ground

**Eternal or self-explaining cosmos.** Many ancient philosophies, and some modern cosmologies, treat the physical cosmos as having always existed, or as needing no explanation beyond itself. Some modern proposals treat a vast or infinite ensemble of universes, a proposal about many physical cosmoi, as the final explanation of why ours is as it is. *Divergence:* Scripture teaches that the world had a beginning in God's creative act and depends on him continually (Genesis 1:1; Colossians 1:17). The book does not reject cosmological research, whose results it accepts as description. It rejects the metaphysical step by which a physical ensemble is treated as self-explaining.

**Pantheism and strong panentheism.** Pantheism identifies God with the world. Strong forms of panentheism hold that the world is part of God's own being, or that God develops through it, as in some process theologies. *Divergence:* the Creator–creature distinction (Psalm 100:3; Romans 1:25). This matters especially for a relational ontology. A deepest created order is easily mistaken for something divine, and the book insists that the substrate is a creature.

**Dualisms of light and darkness, or spirit and matter.** Manichaean dualism treats darkness or evil as a co-eternal principle opposing the good. Gnostic systems treat matter as the defective work of a lesser power and salvation as escape from it. *Divergence:* darkness in Genesis 1 is a created condition, not a rival principle; evil is parasitic, not original (Chapter 13); matter is good, and redemption is resurrection, not escape (Chapter 14).

**Deism and occasionalism.** Deism holds that God made the world and withdrew. Occasionalism holds that created things have no real operations, so that God alone acts in every event. *Divergence:* Chapter 4's "non-agentic runtime under continuous upholding" sits between them. Created order really operates, and it is never self-sustaining.

## Matter and the physical world

**Materialism and reductionism.** Materialism holds that matter, or whatever physics ultimately describes, is all there is. Reductionism, in its strong form, holds that every higher-level reality is nothing but its lower-level parts. *Divergence:* the book accepts that persons are made of dust and that living things are built from chemistry, and it insists on the explanatory reality of organisation (Chapter 10). It rejects the claim that persons, consciousness, obligation and meaning are nothing but physical configuration (Chapters 11 and 12). It also argues, in Chapter 9, that object-based materialism leaves an unexplained gulf between the physical base and human life.

**Object-first ontology.** Distinct from materialism, this is the widespread assumption that reality's basic furniture is self-contained objects with intrinsic properties, and that relations are secondary. It is compatible with theism, and many Christians hold it. *Divergence:* this is the main philosophical contrast of the book (Chapters 3 and 9). It is argued on grounds of global coherence, chiefly that self-contained objects must be given a common interface from elsewhere to explain why everything interacts by one grammar, and it is not presented as a matter of faith.

**Mathematical universe views.** Some thinkers hold that reality simply *is* a mathematical structure, so that the success of mathematics in physics is explained by identity. *Divergence:* Chapter 8 treats mathematics as describing real regularities with great success, without being identical to what it describes.

**Idealism.** Idealism holds that reality is fundamentally mental, or constituted by being perceived or interpreted. *Divergence:* Scripture presents a world that is really there before any creature perceives it (Genesis 1), and the relational ontology is not a mental one: relation, in this book's sense, is a real standing-between, not an act of thought.

**Panpsychism.** Some recent philosophers propose that consciousness, or something like it, is a fundamental feature of all matter. *Divergence:* the book does not attribute awareness or intention to the substrate (Chapter 4). It treats consciousness as belonging to persons and creatures, and as unexplained by the ontology (Chapter 11).

## Persons

**Cartesian substance dualism.** This view treats mind and body as two separate substances that somehow interact. *Divergence:* Genesis 2:7 presents one living creature formed from dust and breath, not two things yoked together. The book also holds that the inner person is real and not reducible to the body (Chapter 11). It does not offer a theory of how soul and body relate, and Christians hold several.

**Embodiment-constituted personhood.** This view holds that to be a person is simply to be a certain kind of body. *Divergence:* the Trinity (Chapter 11).

**Annihilationism and universalism without judgment.** The first holds that death ends the person. The second holds that all will be restored without moral reckoning. *Divergence:* Scripture teaches universal resurrection, to life or to judgment (John 5:28-29; Matthew 25:46).

## Knowledge

**Scientism.** Scientism is the view that science is the only route to knowledge, or that whatever science cannot measure does not exist. It differs from science itself. *Divergence:* Chapter 2 distinguishes methodological naturalism, a sound rule of scientific practice, from metaphysical naturalism, which does not follow from it. The inability of a method to detect something does not show that the thing is not there.

The first edition of this book devoted a chapter to Paul's warning against "oppositions of science falsely so called" (1 Timothy 6:20). The point of that chapter can be stated briefly. Paul's warning is not a rejection of inquiry. It names a recurring structural fault, in which genuine but limited knowledge gains authority, then claims more than it has established, smuggles in metaphysical premises, and sets itself against truth. The fault can occur in any discipline, including theology. A related fault is the demand that any new ontology must first reproduce the entire formal apparatus of an incumbent theory before it may even be considered, even when the question raised is precisely what that apparatus describes. The appropriate response is not suspicion of science. It is attention to the difference between what has been measured and what has been inferred from the measurements.

**Relativism.** Relativism holds that there is no truth about reality, only perspectives. *Divergence:* coherence, in this book, is a means of seeking truth, and Chapters 2 and 9 insist that coherence is not the same as truth. The book holds that there is a truth to be sought, and that God knows it fully.

## Exploratory research not yet published as formal records

Several lines of the project's research were carried out in conversational research sessions whose records are not in the repository. Their conclusions inform Chapters 6 to 9. They are described in the book as exploratory, and they should not be cited as completed experiments until formal records exist. The repository file `books/v2/analysis/conversational-research-import-v2.md` lists them with their status.

- **Contextual identity and provenance** (Chapter 6). States can be equivalent under one description and distinguishable by a later interaction. A distinction may be ignored only where no reachable future can draw on it. Past paths can survive in present structure. *Adjacent recorded evidence:* Generation 1b's hidden variety; the calculator's equivalence classes. *Limits from the same exploratory line:* rebuilt organisation or function was not always the same identity restored; some exploratory runs drifted toward one dominant regime; identity-specific recovery sometimes needed contextual help; no universal mechanism of identity was found.
- **Coordination in rings of chancy units** (Chapter 8). Equal individual chances do not fix whole-system behaviour. What stabilises is coordination aligned with consequence, and stability of the mean is not stability of fluctuations. *Adjacent recorded evidence:* the calculator's covariance and ring-locality results.
- **Effective laws** (Chapter 8). Deterministic continuum equations such as Navier–Stokes as effective descriptions; the open question of which relational conditions make stable effective laws possible. No derivation is claimed.
- **Projection and hidden structure** (Chapters 6 and 8). The lesson was drawn while studying the geometry used in some modern calculations of particle interactions (the "positive Grassmannian" and "amplituhedron"). Distinct underlying configurations can project to one mathematical object and still differ in what they do. The book makes no claim about that physics itself.

## The calculator as an illustration

Chapter 5 uses the stochastic calculator once, as an illustration of how chance and exactness relate. It is not evidence about nature. The details are recorded here:

- 40,000 additions and subtractions were all correct.
- From one fixed starting arrangement, 4,000 runs followed 4,000 different paths, and all were correct.
- With an occasional fault (the outright deletion of a relation), accuracy fell smoothly as enforcement weakened, as an ordinary survival formula had predicted before the decisive runs. A rule enforced 99.9 per cent of the time held the right answer in about half of runs after 2,000 steps.
- After destruction, the original number was never recovered (1,800 trials).
- When a carried ten could be received twice, accuracy fell to about 83 per cent at a low replay rate and 48 per cent at a high one. With protection against duplicate receipt, it was correct every time. This illustrates a transactional receiver (Chapter 7).
- Paired insertion and removal of neutral relations gave each count a variance of about 77 and a covariance of exactly −77, so the total did not vary (Chapter 8).
- Restricting cancellation to neighbouring regions on a ring kept every answer exact but raised the mean step count from about 30 to about 356 at 32 regions. Multiplication and division needed whole-system bookkeeping.

## Equivalence is not the same individual

The exploratory research distinguished four kinds of recovery: of function, of organisation, of the relevant equivalence class, and of the same individual or lineage. The first three were sometimes achieved without the fourth, and recovery of the same lineage sometimes depended on contextual help. Chapter 6 therefore separates sameness of kind or state (equivalence) from individual continuity (lineage). The project has not established what carries individual continuity in physical systems.

## An open problem: where carriers come from

The project has built models in which distinctions, provenance and equivalence are carried through explicitly designed relational protocols, and it has shown that carriers can be substituted while what matters is preserved. It has **not** shown that a physically adequate architecture of carriers and lineages emerges by itself from minimal relational rules, or that nature uses the project's carrier grammar. That is one of the most important open questions in the programme.

The first edition rested on specific proposed mechanisms (knotted closures in a continuous medium, a closed list of five kinds of change, particular accounts of light and magnetism) that later work did not support; the section below records where each went. The second edition (October 2026) rebuilt the book on more foundational ideas. Revisions 2.1 to 2.3 corrected it against further research and review. ## First-edition material now held here

**The ontological stack.** The first edition divided actual order into levels T1A to T1D2 (primitive geometry, micro-organisation, objecthood, kinds, persistence of kinds, created and creature-made orders), with T2 for lived manifestation. This edition uses T0 (whatever conditions actualisation, read as constrained possibility) and T1 (actuality, understood as carrying its own history). The finer levels remain useful for technical classification. The T2 label is retired. "Manifestation" is described in ordinary words and kept distinct from conscious awareness, so that it cannot be mistaken for an explanation of consciousness.

**The five operations.** The first edition proposed that every change in the substrate is one of five operations (propagation, reconfiguration, alignment adjustment, closure formation, dissolution), and associated light, electricity and magnetism with the first three. It is retained in the technical volume as a classificatory proposal. It has not been tested, and this edition does not rely on it.

**Closure knots.** The first edition proposed that matter's stable identities are knot-like closures in the substrate, with a specific mechanism of persistence. This edition keeps the general idea that identity can be carried by invariant organisation, which the July 2026 computations support in principle. The specific mechanism is the technical volume's candidate, not declared ontology.

**Light, electricity and magnetism.** The first edition described light as a coherent transient torsional disturbance, magnetism as held twist and electricity as ordered reconfiguration. The July 2026 work derived the *structure* of the homogeneous Maxwell equations from an assumed substrate geometry, a notable internal result. No quantitative account of light or electromagnetism has been produced from the substrate, so these mappings do not appear in the body of this book.

**Time and relativity.** The first edition explained the slowing of clocks by the substrate's resistance to reconfiguration. That explanation was never made quantitative and added no prediction to general relativity, which describes these effects precisely. This edition retains only the reading of time as the ordering of actualisation (Chapter 6).

**Agent "dialects" and miracles.** The first edition proposed that miracles are operations performed under wider permissions within the same created grammar. This edition withdraws that proposal, because it risked placing God's action inside the possibilities of created order. Chapter 14 states the present position: Scripture's accounts of miracles are received as given, and the ontology offers no mechanism for them.

**Earlier T-C-R wording.** The first draft of this edition defined transmission as release "at a cost to the source" and reception as occurring "once". Both came from the calculator's arithmetic protocol, and both became subcases in v2.1. Revision 2.4 went further. It retired the description of transmission as a distinction becoming "available beyond its locality", because that presupposed a locality and something travelling from it. T-C-R is now stated as the grammar of consequential manifestation: distinction, preserved dependency, consequence (Chapter 7).

## A note on the label T0

"T0" has been used in three ways in the project's work. In the books it has meant constrained possibility, the shape of what created order can bear. In the project's AMS research notes it names the continuous substrate domain, an underlying order inferred rather than observed. And the July 2026 research notes use "T0" to "T7" as rungs of a ladder of thought experiments, in which "T0" means the substrate at rest. From revision 2.4 this book holds only the minimal commitment fixed: T0 names whatever conditions actualisation. "Constrained possibility" is the book's working reading. What kind of thing T0 finally is remains open (Chapter 4 and Appendix E).

# Appendix C: Glossary

Each term is used in this book in the single sense given here. The chapter in brackets is where the term is first explained. For the status of each claim, and the structure of the ontology as a whole, see Appendix E.

**Actuality, actualisation (T1).** Actualisation is the coming-to-be of one admissible configuration or transition among those that are possible. Actuality is what has come to be, and it is not a memoryless snapshot: it carries the relational traces of its own past. (Ch 4, Ch 6)

**Admissibility.** Whether a transition is possible at all within created physical order: a hard boundary of the possible. Not to be confused with moral permission. Chapter 12 distinguishes physical, operational and moral admissibility; unqualified, the word means physical. (Ch 4)

**Alignment.** Fitting the order a thing or person is made for. In persons, a moral and relational condition, not a physical measurement. (Ch 12)

**Attractor.** In the mathematics of changing systems, a state or set of states toward which a system tends from a wide range of starting points and to which it returns when disturbed. A reliable outcome is not by itself evidence of one. (Ch 8)

**Carrier.** Whatever bears the dependency that preserves a distinction at a given stage: in manifest cases a wave, a current, a fire, a record, a chain of local interactions. Carriers may be substituted without loss, provided the relevant distinction is preserved. At the most primitive level the relation itself may be the carrier. (Ch 7)

**Cause (working account).** A distinction in one relational condition modifying the admissible actualisation, or consequential state, of another through a distinction-preserving dependency: by excluding, admitting or weighting states, constraining correlations, preserving an invariant, or changing later accessibility. A working hypothesis. (Ch 7)

**Category.** A genuinely distinct kind of reality: material, living, personal, moral, spiritual. God is not a category within creation but its Creator. (Ch 1)

**Chaotic.** Deterministic but so sensitive to its starting point that prediction becomes impractical. Distinct from stochastic. (Ch 5)

**Coherence.** Non-contradiction is necessary but not sufficient. Coherence also requires real connection between the parts of an account, valid interfaces where domains meet, meaning kept through explanatory layers and round trips, stability when one domain is repaired, accountable distinctions between kinds of knowing, and preservation of real distinctions. *Local*: a model explains a bounded phenomenon. *Regional*: neighbouring domains integrate. *Global*: the whole account of reality passes these tests together. Global coherence runs both ways: it tests the metaphysics that commonly travels with physics as well as the book's own proposal. Coherence is not the same as truth. (Ch 2, Ch 9)

**Constrained stochastic actualisation.** The Relational Substrate hypothesis that what becomes actual is stochastic in the ontic sense: genuinely open among admissible options until it occurs, weighted by tendency and shaped by constraint. (Ch 5)

**Currently reachable.** What can actually happen next, given the present state and the history it carries. Distinct from total admissibility, everything created order could ever bear. (Ch 4)

**Coordination.** Relations among chance events that make them vary together. What stabilises a whole is coordination aligned with consequence and responsive to the current state, not correlation as such. It is not a controller. (Ch 8)

**Corruption.** Distortion of good order: wrongdoing, which is wrong in itself, together with its entrenchment as misalignment persists and spreads without being restored. Parasitic, with no being of its own. (Ch 13)

**Created runtime order.** Creation as operating, the domain of ordinary physical process, distinguished from creation's origin in God's act. The term is borrowed from computing, but creation is not a computer. (Ch 3)

**Distinction (source distinction).** A distinguishable state or relation that could make a difference to something other than itself. (Ch 7)

**Effective determinism.** Regular, reliable, predictable behaviour at the scale observed, whatever the underlying process. A fact about regularity, not a metaphysical claim. (Ch 8)

**Equivalence class.** All the states that count as "the same" for a given purpose: those that no relevant interaction could tell apart. (Ch 6)

**Gathering.** In the project's models, a time-weighted preference for relation to a reference structure. Distinct from *succession*; neither is gravity. A claim of gathering must name its clock and survive adjustment for density. (Ch 8)

**Identity (of physical things).** Sameness of kind or state: continuity of a thing's relevant equivalence class through admissible change, judged by what reachable future interactions could discriminate. Invariants certify it, and provenance is part of it where it remains consequential. Distinct from *individual continuity*. Personal identity resembles both but is not defined by them. (Ch 6)

**Individual continuity.** Being the same continuing individual, and not merely an equivalent replacement. It requires continuity of lineage and history in addition to equivalence. What carries it in physical things is an open question. (Ch 6)

**Invariant.** A quantity or feature left unchanged by a set of transformations. One carrier or certificate of identity, not necessarily all of it. (Ch 6)

**Joint process.** How chance events are related to one another, as distinct from the chance of each event alone (its *marginal* probability). Equal marginals do not imply the same joint process or the same behaviour of the whole. (Ch 8)

**Manifestation.** Created order presenting itself to other things: detectable and encounterable. A physical matter, distinct from anyone's awareness of it. (Ch 6)

**Mathematical determinism.** A property of equations: the state at one moment fixes the state at every later moment. Compatible with a non-deterministic world underneath. (Ch 8)

**Matter.** Stable relational form, not primitive substance. Real, measurable and consequential. (Ch 6)

**Memory (physical).** Past distinctions embodied in present relations so that they alter what is currently reachable, how likely it is, or how a thing responds. No separate memory substance is required. A working hypothesis. (Ch 6)

**Ontology.** An account of what must be there for described behaviour to be possible. Distinct from description. (Ch 2)

**Persistence.** Continued re-actualisation within the same relevant equivalence class, against some kind of disturbance, over some length of time; for a continuing individual, along an unbroken history. (Ch 6)

**Person.** A centre of awareness, relation, agency, address and responsibility. Personhood is deeper than embodiment; human persons are persons in an embodied, creaturely mode. (Ch 11)

**Possibility, constrained.** The book's working reading of T0: what created order is capable of, meaning the configurations and transitions that can occur at all, together with how they are weighted. A way of speaking, not a realm. (Ch 4)

**Projection (coarse-graining).** A description that maps several distinct underlying states onto one visible state. It can hide distinctions that later matter. (Ch 6, Ch 8)

**Provenance.** Past distinctions that survive in present relational structure. Part of a thing's identity where some reachable future could draw on them; safely ignored where none could. (Ch 6)

**Reception.** Another state changed, or constrained in what it can become, because of the source distinction. Reception is consequence, not mere arrival. How a receiver treats repeated or continuing arrivals (once, continuously, cumulatively, or harmlessly repeated) is a property of the receiver. (Ch 7)

**Receiver compatibility.** What a receiver can register, and how it responds, as set by its own admissibility. (Ch 7)

**Relation.** A real standing-between in which each relatum is conditioned by the other. *Interactional* relation holds between things already fully what they are. *Constitutive* relation partly makes things what they are. The Relational Substrate proposal concerns the second. (Ch 3)

**Relational substrate.** The proposed created relational order within which physical things are constituted and act. It is not matter, not ether, not a field under another name, not a mind, not a soul and not God. Whether it is itself spatial is open. (Ch 3)

**Stochastic.** Described by probabilities over defined possibilities. The word itself does not say whether the probabilities are *epistemic* (ignorance of hidden details) or *ontic* (genuine openness). Not lawless, not arbitrary, not chaotic. (Ch 5)

**Succession.** A settled, directed order of change. In the project's models it needs a non-gradient tendency or a one-way rule. Distinct from *gathering*. The book's reading of time as the ordering of actualisation concerns order; succession concerns direction. (Ch 6)

**T0.** The book's label for whatever conditions actualisation. That much is a firm commitment. What kind of thing T0 is remains open; "constrained possibility" is the working reading (Appendix E). (Ch 4)

**Structural analogy.** A likeness of form between realities of different kinds. Distinct from *ontological identity*, sameness of kind. (Ch 1)

**Tendency.** The relative likelihood of an admissible transition compared with the others. A weighting, not a permission, and not a probability that anyone assigns. (Ch 4)

**Transmission.** A distinction in one relational condition being capable of constraining something other than itself. The source may or may not be changed in the process. (Ch 7)

**Transmit–Carry–Receive (T-C-R).** The grammar of consequential manifestation: distinction → preserved dependency → consequence. It presupposes no travel, metric space, intermediate object or exactly-once reception. The book's working hypothesis is that it is the form of consequential relation itself, and recursive. (Ch 7)

**Universal interoperability.** The observed fact that independently located physical things share one grammar of interaction: they can affect one another, have compatible receptivity and commensurable quantities, and compose into stable structures. Every ontology must account for it. (Ch 3)

## A note on method

This framework was developed with substantial help from AI systems, including ChatGPT and Codex (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic) and Grok (xAI). They were used for surveying literature across physics, philosophy, biology and theology, for building and running the computational experiments, for testing the framework's internal coherence, and in drafting. The second edition was restructured with the help of an explicit concept-dependency analysis and a readability review, and revision 2.4 followed a full human reading of the finished book. The records are kept with the manuscript in the project repository. There is one human author. The AI systems were tools of analysis and drafting, not members of a research team.

What was not delegated is judgement. The positions retained in the book were evaluated, challenged and selected by the author. Where AI assistance produced a claim, it was checked against sources and against the research record, not accepted on trust. Several first-edition claims were withdrawn in this edition precisely because that checking found them unsupported. The honest description of the process is a collaboration between human reasoning and machine-assisted research and synthesis, with responsibility for coherence, theological containment and final judgement kept in human hands.

# Appendix E: Current Ontological Reference

This appendix answers one question: *what is the ontology of this book, right now?* It can be read on its own, without the research history. It records the structure of the proposal, the status of each major claim, the questions that remain open, and the readings the book rejects. It is also a governance record. A later revision that changes any entry here should change it here first and say why.

## E.1 The map

The proposal has the shape below. The entries are not all the same kind of thing, and the map is not a stack of layers. Each entry is marked with what it is: a **reality** (something held to exist), a **category** (a kind of reality), a **process**, a **formal handle** (a label for a distinction, used in reasoning), or a **theological reality** (known by revelation).

## E.3 T0

**Minimal commitment.** T0 names whatever is logically prior to, and conditions, actualisation: the constraints, potential, admissibility, organisation or relational conditions according to which an actual state can come to be.

**Working reading.** *Constrained possibility*: the shape of what created order can bear, meaning which configurations and transitions can occur at all (admissibility) and how they are weighted (tendency).

**Live alternatives.** T0 might be better understood as:

- a created potential order;
- a generative architecture of constraint;
- the relational state of the substrate itself, considered before a particular actualisation;
- a formulation not yet found, perhaps one suggested by further work on stochastic processes.

**Not claimed.** T0 is not a realm, a store, a place or a second world. "Possibility space" is a way of speaking. T0 is created, and it is not a divine attribute.

**T0 and T1.** The relation is not a one-way pipeline. *Total admissibility* is everything the created order could ever bear. What is *currently reachable* depends on present state and provenance. Actual history changes what is currently reachable without changing whatever T0 ultimately is.

## E.4 T-C-R

**Formulation.** T-C-R is the grammar of consequential manifestation: the logical structure by which one distinction becomes consequential to another state.

```text
distinction → preserved dependency → consequence
```

- **Transmit.** A distinction in one relational condition is capable of constraining something other than itself.
- **Carry.** The dependency between the distinction and its consequence preserves enough of the distinction for the consequence to depend on it. The carrier need not be a separate object; at the most primitive level the relation itself may be the carrier. Test: if two source states can never make any difference to any consequence, the distinction between them has not been carried.
- **Receive.** Another state becomes actual, or its admissible actualisation is constrained, because of the source distinction. Reception is consequence, not arrival.

**Not presupposed.** Travel; a metric space; a sequence of locations; an intermediate object; encoding and decoding; a stack of hidden and visible layers; exactly-once reception; consumption of the source. Each occurs in particular cases. None defines the grammar.

**Hypotheses.** T-C-R is the operational form of consequential relation itself, which explains why it recurs from substrate to speech. It is recursive: every receiver can transmit, so consequence forms a network in which present actuality is received history and a source of what comes next.

**Causation.** The working account is that a causal relation exists where a distinction in one relational condition modifies the admissible actualisation, or consequential state, of another through a distinction-preserving dependency. It can do so by excluding, admitting or weighting states, constraining correlations, preserving an invariant, or changing later accessibility.

**Manifestation.** A consequence-preserving relationship holds between distinctions in the underlying order and the manifest world. Its architecture is not known. The substrate and the manifest world might be two descriptions of one fact, different resolutions of one order, related by an unknown mapping, or related in a way not yet conceived. The book does not say that the invisible is "transformed" into the visible.

## E.5 Spatiality

Whether the substrate is itself spatial is **open**. Three broad families remain live:

- **intrinsic spatiality**: substrate structure corresponds significantly to experienced physical space;
- **emergent spatiality**: physical extension arises from a relational organisation that is not itself spatial;
- **proto-spatial structure**: the substrate has adjacency, ordering, topology or connectivity from which metric space emerges.

There may be others. Scripture's language of heavens stretched out provokes questions about container-space. It supplies no substrate geometry. Words like "transformation", "projection", "mapping" and "passes through" are not used in this book to choose among these families.

## E.6 Identity and provenance

- **Sameness of kind or state**: continuity of the relevant equivalence class through admissible change, judged by what reachable futures could discriminate.
- **Invariants** certify equivalence in some systems. They are not the whole of identity.
- **Provenance**, past distinctions surviving in present structure, belongs to identity where some reachable future could draw on it.
- **Individual continuity** (lineage) is distinct from equivalence. An equivalent replacement is not the same individual.
- **Four recoveries** are kept apart: of function; of organisation (structural or recursive); of the equivalence class; of the same individual. The first three can occur without the fourth.
- **Personal identity** resembles physical identity and is not defined by it.
- **History as present state.** In the project's models, the same visible configuration can face different futures because of history carried by the process. Admitting that history into a richer present state restores the dependence on the present alone. Such history can stay predictive after exact detail is forgotten, and a coarse enough description can hide it completely. This supports the reading of memory as past distinction embodied in present relation. It establishes no physical law of memory and no irreducible time.

## E.7 What the ontology does not currently claim

- Spacetime is not established as a substance, and space is not assumed to be a container.
- Time is not a flowing thing; it is read as the ordering of actualisation.
- Mathematical description is not ontology by default.
- A successful physical model does not uniquely fix metaphysics.
- Fields, particles, energy and information are descriptions or candidate structures, not assumed furniture.
- Laws of nature are not agents.
- T0 is not established as a literal realm.
- T-C-R does not imply packet transport or physical travel.
- Stochasticity is not an agent, and it is not a proof of free will.
- The substrate is not a mind, not a soul, not God, and not a spiritual ontology.
- Information transfer is not consciousness, and the ontology does not explain consciousness.
- Structural analogy is not ontological identity.
- Equivalence is not individual continuity.
- Physical reconstruction is not personal continuity, and resurrection is not natural substrate reconstruction.
- Moral evil is not merely physical instability, and redemption is not substrate self-repair.
- Evil was not necessary.
- No temporal claim drawn from a model is clock-free. A model's count of steps is not a universal clock.
- Gathering in a model is not gravity, and a directed order of change in a model is not the arrow of time.
- A model reference is not a centre of nature, and a held structure in a model is a boundary condition, not something the system made.
- Coherence does not prove the Relational Substrate.

## E.8 Gravity, time and persistence in the simplest models (adopted in revision 2.5)

Revision 2.5 adopts the ontology proposals of the project's Gravity, Temporality and Emergent Spatiality programme (`gravity-time/docs/ontology-proposals.md`, OP-1 to OP-20). Each was written for later review, and each is adopted at the status it proposed for itself. Where a later proposal amends an earlier one, the amended form is adopted. **All are model-scoped**: they describe exact results in finite relational models, at most seven points, whose rules and clocks the experimenter chose. None is a claim about gravity or time in nature.

**Forbidding and favouring (OP-10, OP-14, OP-15; Chapter 4).** In aggregate statistics such as how often, how directed, and how long absent, a hard ban behaves like the extreme of ever-stronger disfavouring. What is new with a ban is the *structure of possible histories*: one-way changes, recovery only by first undoing a supporting relation, and no direct way back. An absence that persists is therefore of two kinds. It is *rate-limited* when return is possible but slow, and *gate-limited* when return is impossible until something else changes. Under hard formation bans nothing is permanently lost. Hard admissibility can also drive a system into a state in which nothing at all is admissible, a total arrest; whether anything in created order corresponds to that is open (OP-4).

**Direction of change (OP-5, OP-7, OP-10; Chapter 6).** Favouring rules come in two kinds. When the rule is the slope of some quantity, a *gradient* tendency, it produces a basin of preferred states with no settled direction of change. A *non-gradient* tendency produces a stationary, directed order of change, which the book calls **succession**. In these models a hard one-way rule can also produce direction, with no favouring at all. The characteristic directed pattern is a *handover*: a relation forms, then the relation that supported it dissolves. The book's reading of time as the ordering of actualisation is untouched. These results concern *direction*, not ordering. **Status:** mechanism candidate, model-scoped.

**Succession is not gathering (OP-8, OP-17; Chapter 8).** **Gathering** is a time-weighted preference for relation to a reference structure. The two separate cleanly. The handover kept its direction in every case tested when the models grew from six to seven points, while the only gathering effect seen alongside it fell to the level of the null comparison. Evidence for one is not evidence for the other. An apparent gathering counts only if it survives adjustment for density; much of it is otherwise density alone.

**Attraction, clocks and potential (OP-1, OP-2, OP-3, OP-6, OP-9; Chapter 8).** In these models a constraint rule defines a *relational potential*: a count of a relational pattern, weighted by the rule's strength. Preferred states follow that potential, and "attraction" toward a structure is participation in the counted pattern, with no named centre and no direction. **Status:** mechanism candidate, model-scoped. But the rules do *not* tie the rate of activity to the potential. The same basin admits very different near and far rates, depending on how a rule's weight is split between forming and dissolving. Under irreversible rules the split changes the preferred states themselves. A relational reading of gravitational clock-rate differences would therefore need a principle these rules do not supply. **Status:** open question, with a negative model result. Every statement that something "attracts" must say which clock is meant, whether it is adjusted for density, and in what density regime. Different clocks can give opposite signs.

**A reference takes part (OP-12, OP-13, OP-16 as qualified; Chapter 8).** A reference made of relations acts on other relations through the same rules, not as a neutral frame. Its internal support is one lever on where new relations form, alongside other structural channels. It is not a general law that support makes a site less available. **Status:** methodological principle; the lever is a mechanism candidate.

**Persistence before reference (OP-18, OP-19, OP-20; Chapter 6).** A structure counts as a reference only once it is shown to outlast its own constituent relations, compared with a matched null, in each clock. *What is likely* and *what lasts* are separate questions. Two systems with the same stationary picture, the same density and the same average activity can differ in lifetime by more than twenty times. A persistence claim must therefore name its comparison and what that comparison matches. It must separate *reaching* a renewed state from *surviving* in it, and it must preserve directed flows rather than averaging them away. **Status:** methodological principles.

**Not adopted.** A size-normalised rule family (not needed; GT, "Not proposed"). Any inference from these models to gravity, physical time, memory, purpose or theology. Any common operator shared with the constraint laboratory, which has not been tested.
