# Before You Begin

## What the title means

**Ontology** is the study of what things really are: not only how they look or behave, but what kind of reality they have. **Biblical** means that the book starts from the Bible and lets it set the frame. **Coherent** means that the picture must hang together across everything we know, from physics to people to the Bible's story.

So the book asks one big question: *what kind of world did God make, so that stones, light, living things, persons, right and wrong, and the Bible's account of creation and redemption all belong to it?*

## How to read the claims

The book makes several kinds of claim, and it labels them. Watch for these:

| Kind of claim | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Scripture teaches it** (or it is inferred from Scripture, and marked as an inference) | God created the heaven and the earth. |
| **Observed or measured** | A dropped stone falls. |
| **Mathematical or physical result** | Distance fallen grows with the square of the time. |
| **Argued** (an inference from the above) | Describing behaviour does not by itself tell you what a thing is. |
| **The book's hypothesis** | The physical world is relational at its foundation. |
| **Mechanism candidate** | A particular proposed structure for matter, named but not relied on. |
| **Open question** | How consciousness relates to the physical world. |

Two more labels say where a result came from. A **model result** happened in one of the project's computer models. A model can show that an idea is possible and workable. It cannot show that nature works that way.

The most important label is the fifth. The book's central idea, the **Relational Substrate**, is a hypothesis. Scripture never mentions it, and the book never presents it as biblical teaching.

## One scene

The book keeps returning to one ordinary scene: a clear morning, and a stone on the path in front of you. You see its grey surface and a pale vein in it. You could pick it up and find it in your pocket tomorrow. What is the stone? How does it reach your eye? Why is it the same stone tomorrow? And who is the one who sees it?

## About this edition

Earlier editions described specific mechanisms, such as stable "knots" as the basis of matter and a structure called the vorton, as though they were settled. This edition does not rely on them. They remain mechanism candidates, worth testing and nothing more.

A free electronic edition of this book is available, offered to encourage reading, sharing and honest discussion. Updated versions and related materials are at <https://relational-substrate.blogspot.com/> and <https://github.com/gellsmore-svg/Relational-Substrate>.

# Chapter 1: Scripture and the World That Is There

Every account of reality starts somewhere. This book starts with Scripture, and says so openly.

> `All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness.`
> *2 Timothy 3:16*

## Scripture speaks first

Scripture is authoritative in the form it actually takes. Genesis is narrative and worship. It is not a laboratory report, and it is not a hidden code for physics.

So "Scripture first" means this:

- Scripture sets the **boundaries** within which an account of reality may be built.
- Inside those boundaries there is room for **patient inquiry** into how the world works.
- A proposal that **contradicts** Scripture is ruled out, however elegant.
- A proposal Scripture **neither teaches nor forbids** may be explored, but never presented as if the Bible taught it.

When the Bible and science seem to collide, the fault usually lies in a human extension: a text pressed beyond what it says, or a conclusion drawn beyond what was measured.

The Relational Substrate is a modern proposal, so the book keeps four relations carefully apart:

1. **Scripture teaches** something (the world is created, held, good and distinct from God).
2. The proposal **is consistent with** what Scripture teaches.
3. The proposal offers **a possible mechanism** for something Scripture says, without Scripture teaching the mechanism.
4. Scripture **provokes a question** the proposal explores.

Only the first carries Scripture's authority. The others never borrow it.

## A world that is really there

> `In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.`
> *Genesis 1:1-3*

Notice the order. The world is created in verse 1. In verse 2 the earth, the deep and the waters are already there, unformed. Only then is light spoken. So Genesis 1:1 is a real beginning, and what follows is forming and filling *within* a real world.

That shapes how we read the darkness of verse 2. It is not nothing, because the earth and waters are there with it. It is not called evil. The book infers, and labels it as an inference, that this darkness is a real condition of a real world not yet made manifest.

If darkness were nothing, then whatever cannot be seen would seem less real than what can. Genesis forbids that. **Visibility is not the measure of reality.**

> `Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear.`
> *Hebrews 11:3*

## Creator and creature

The most important boundary in the book is this: God made the world, and the world is not God.

> `For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible... and he is before all things, and by him all things consist.`
> *Colossians 1:16-17*

The boundary cuts both ways:

- **Created things are not divine.** Nothing in creation, however vast or orderly, is God or part of God.
- **Created things are not self-explaining.** Nothing holds itself together by its own power. "By him all things consist."

This matters because the book will propose a deepest created order of the physical world. Such an order could easily be mistaken for something more: a cosmic mind, a world-soul or a quiet substitute for God. Scripture closes that door in advance. The substrate, if it exists, is a **creature**. It is not divine, not intelligent and not the source of its own order.

## Kinds of reality

Genesis describes distinct kinds of reality. The Hebrew verb *bara*, "create", used only with God as subject, marks three thresholds: the beginning of everything (Genesis 1:1), animal life (1:21) and humanity (1:27). Genesis 2 shows the inner structure of the last:

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

The book calls these kinds of reality **categories**: the material, the living, the personal and the spiritual. God is not a category within creation. He is its Creator.

## The same word is not the same thing

In Genesis 1:3 God creates light. Christ says, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). John writes, "God is light" (1 John 1:5). Nobody should conclude that God is made of sunlight. Yet the word is not used by accident. Light discloses and makes things visible, and Christ does something like that with the truth about God.

The two share a *form* of relation. They do not share a *nature*. The book names this distinction:

- A **structural analogy** is a likeness of form between different kinds of thing.
- **Ontological identity** would mean they are the same kind of thing.

The book uses four ways of linking categories, and says which it means each time:

| Relation | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Structural analogy** | Created light and Christ's making-manifest share a form. |
| **Representation** | Creation was designed to show something of God: "the invisible things of him... are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made" (Romans 1:20). |
| **Causal connection** | Within one person, a lie spoken with the tongue is also a wrong. The two are connected, not the same. |
| **Ontological identity** | Rejected. Different categories are never the same kind of thing. |

## What Scripture leaves open

Scripture rules out a good deal: a self-made or eternal world, and matter as evil or illusory. But it leaves much open, such as how electricity works or what the deepest structure of matter is, and it invites the search:

> `It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter.`
> *Proverbs 25:2*

## Chapter recap

- Scripture sets the boundaries; the book's proposal never borrows Scripture's authority.
- The world is real from the start; God upholds it, and it is not God. The substrate, if it exists, is a creature.
- Creation contains distinct categories, and structural analogy is not identity.

# Chapter 2: How We Come to Know What Is There

Drop the stone and it falls. With Newton's law you can calculate the Moon's orbit, and with Einstein's general relativity the corrections that keep satellite navigation accurate to metres. Physics knows how gravity *behaves* with extraordinary precision.

Now ask: what *is* gravity? A force reaching across space? A curving of spacetime? Physicists who accept every measurement still disagree. The disagreement is not about whether the stone falls. It is about what kind of reality the equations describe.

That gap, between describing behaviour and knowing what something is, is the key to the whole book.

## Six layers of knowing

"Knowing" something about the stone can mean several different things:

1. We have **observed** it falling.
2. We have **measured** it: its mass, the time of its fall.
3. We have a **mathematical relation** that predicts the measurements.
4. We have a **physical model** that organises the behaviour (a pull between masses, or a path through curved spacetime).
5. We hold an **interpretation**: what must really be there for the behaviour to happen.
6. We live within a **worldview** in which that interpretation feels natural.

The earlier layers do not guarantee the later. Einstein replaced Newton's picture of what gravity *is* while keeping most of his predictions. And one set of quantum equations is read in sharply different ways, some saying the world is chancy at bottom and some that it is fully fixed.

**A successful description can be joined to more than one account of what is there.**

## What the outside of a thing can tell you

Programmers sometimes work with a *private function*: code they can run but not read. By testing it they learn what it does, which is real knowledge. But quite different code could give the same outputs, so testing alone cannot tell them how it is written.

We meet the world like that. Knowledge of **behaviour** is genuine knowledge. It is not, by itself, knowledge of **what a thing is**.

Questions can stand in different positions:

- some we can **explain mechanically**, step by step;
- some we **understand** without a full mechanism, as we understand why a promise binds;
- some lie **beyond present means** but may become accessible, as atoms did;
- some may **never be fully comprehended** by a creature, though partial understanding is still real.

Two errors are to be avoided: declaring something permanently hidden without reason, and assuming that everything will one day be observed as one more object.

## Every account starts somewhere

Every picture of reality includes assumptions beyond measurement. Looking only for natural causes is a sensible working rule in science; it cannot show that nothing else exists, any more than a metal detector can show that there is no wood.

Explanations also go wrong in a characteristic way. Imagine someone who blames every odd event in a house on an invisible resident, giving it whatever new power each event needs. Nothing could count against it, so it explains nothing. The book calls this **metaphysical elasticity**, or informally the *Harry Potter problem*. It happens when a word like "matter" is stretched to cover whatever turns up. It could happen in this book too, if the substrate gained a new power for every new problem. So the book fixes its commitments in advance and says what would count against them.

## Local, regional and global coherence

How do we judge between pictures of reality if no measurement can display the answer? By **coherence**: how well an account holds together. A doctor forms the diagnosis that best fits all the symptoms together, and revises it if a new result does not fit. Asking for the most coherent account of reality is the same activity at the largest scale.

![Figure 1. Three scopes of coherence, nested: local coherence (a model explains one bounded phenomenon), within regional coherence (neighbouring domains such as physics, chemistry and biology integrate), within global coherence (the whole account of reality, from physics to Scripture, remains mutually compatible).](v2/figures/fig-coherence.png)

- **Local coherence**: a model explains one thing well, such as falling stones.
- **Regional coherence**: neighbouring fields fit together, as chemistry fits atomic physics.
- **Global coherence**: the whole account fits together, physics, life, persons, morality, history and Scripture.

Global coherence asks more than freedom from contradiction:

- the domains must be **really connected**, not just placed side by side;
- where one kind of reality meets another, the **meeting must be explained**;
- a word must **keep its meaning** as it crosses from physics to persons;
- a claim taken **round a cycle** of domains should come back unchanged.

And real distinctions must survive: Creator and creature, analogy and identity, a person and a pattern. Connection is not reduction.

The book's central thesis about method is this: **success in one area is not enough if the same account produces contradiction elsewhere.** A picture that explains falling stones but makes promises or love unintelligible has a problem its local success cannot cure.

## Coherence runs both ways

The book must not contradict what has been observed and measured. Any proposal that does is finished.

But the picture of reality that usually travels with physics is also a proposal: space as an empty container, the world as independent objects. These belong to layers 4 to 6, not to layers 1 to 3. They gain no evidence from being familiar or widely taught. So the usual picture is held to the same test as this book's.

## Comparing without numbers

Can we judge one account more plausible without calculating a probability? Yes. Pass your hand along an electric fence once a centimetre away and once a metre away: contact is more likely on the first pass, and you need no model of your breakfast to know it.

So three judgments are different:

1. **Possible**: no contradiction.
2. **More or less plausible**: compared with alternatives.
3. **Probable to a number**: calculated under a model.

The third is often unavailable. The second can still be justified.

## Coherence is not truth

A detailed, consistent story about a flat Earth is still false. Coherence is not truth. Observation rules out accounts that contradict it and tests specific proposals, but it rarely picks out a single winner. So the warrant this book offers is a **global coherence argument**: what it proposes, why, what supports it, what would count against it, and what remains unknown.

The right posture is **disciplined confidence**. Confidence must not outrun its grounds. And justified understanding should not be discounted just because it is incomplete.

## Chapter recap

- Describing behaviour does not by itself settle what a thing is, and no explanation may stretch to fit whatever turns up.
- Global coherence tests the whole account, the usual picture of physics included.
- Plausibility can be compared without numbers; coherence is a guide, not a guarantee of truth.

# Chapter 3: Relation Before Things

List what you know about the stone. It is grey, hard, heavy, cold this morning, and it has a shape.

Now look at each item. *Grey* is how it answers daylight; in the dark it shows no colour. *Hard* is how it resists being scratched. *Heavy* is the pull between it and the Earth; on the Moon it would weigh a sixth as much. *Cold* is the direction heat flows between it and your hand. Even its *shape* is where it stops letting other things into its space.

Every property is a way the stone stands in relation to something else. Strip away the relations and it is hard to say what is left.

## Two pictures of the world

- **The object picture.** Reality is a collection of separate, self-contained objects in empty space. Relations, such as collisions or attraction, are real but secondary: added to objects that would be the same without them. Think of snooker balls.
- **The relational picture.** Things are real and distinct, but they are constituted *within* relations. What a thing is cannot be separated from how it stands to everything else.

The object picture is useful for building bridges, but it assumes, without saying so, that relation is an afterthought.

## Why the object picture strains

1. **Physics finds no truly isolated thing.** An electron's charge is not a label it carries but its capacity to interact in a particular way.
2. **The deepest theories describe relations naturally.** General relativity describes gravity as geometry relating events. In quantum *entanglement*, the joint state of two systems cannot be broken into separate states of each. The same mathematics fits relation-first readings.
3. **Coherence.** If the basic furniture is self-contained objects, then causation, communication, belonging and responsibility must all be added to a world that contains none of them. That becomes a serious problem with life and persons (Chapter 10).

## What "material" means

The word *material* needs a fixed meaning. The book's **working definition**: something is **material** when it has a lasting, individual identity in space and can, at least in principle, be picked up or moved while staying the same identifiable thing. The stone is the model case. A grain of sand under tweezers, or a single atom moved by a microscope tip, also counts.

This is narrower than "physical". A sound crossing a room is physical and real, but nobody can pick up a sound and carry it somewhere else (Chapter 6). The definition is not to be widened whenever a case is awkward.

## One grammar everywhere

Take a granite stone from Hampshire and another from Western Australia. Nobody arranged for them to fit together. Yet they press, scratch, warm and cool each other by the same rules. Light from either reaches the same eye. A meteorite joins the same system of interaction at once, and so does a newly made alloy.

The book calls this **universal interoperability**: independently located things everywhere share one grammar of interaction. It is an observation, and any account of reality has to explain it. It goes deep. The electric charges of the proton and electron are equal in size to at least one part in a thousand billion billion, which is why ordinary matter is neutral. Physicists do not treat that as luck.

If every kind of thing were complete in itself, nothing would guarantee that it fits every other kind. Something common must supply the fit, and every account, this one included, must say what that is and what it costs.

## Three propositions

Three positions can be set side by side:

- **A. Matter is fundamental.** Material things are the basic furniture of creation, with their capacities built in.
- **B. A non-material foundation, with matter emerging.** A deeper created relational order, not itself material, constitutes and sustains material things. Matter is fully real, but its materiality arises rather than standing on its own.
- **C. Two fundamental orders.** Material things and a separate relational order both exist fundamentally; the relational order supplies how matter interacts.

All three are compatible with creation, and design alone does not choose between them.

The author goes further. *That* matter comes from a foundation that is not itself material is held as a **theological premise**, read with Hebrews 11:3. On that premise, A and C are excluded. The book still compares the three, for two reasons: the comparison reaches the same conclusion by a second, independent route, and it shows what B still owes.

- **A** must give every kind of thing capacities that match every other kind's exactly, and pressed to explain the matching it either makes relation essential, adds a governing order (C under another name) or leaves it unexplained.
- **C** must still explain why independent material things fit its relational order so exactly.
- **B** explains interoperability simply: things fit because they are constituted within one order. But B has a real **debt**: *how* stable material things arise within a non-material order has not been shown.

## The claim

The book's central thesis: **what is most fundamental about created physical reality is relation.** An atom, a stone or a star is a configuration *within* relation, never an item that first exists alone and then relates. This is an **RS working hypothesis**, not an observation and not a theorem. It is supported by the strains in the object picture, by the fact that physical description does not settle what is there (Chapter 2), and by interoperability.

Three clarifications:

1. **Things are still real and distinct.** A knot in a rope is real; you can count it and untie it. Yet it is not an object added to the rope but a shape of the rope itself. (The picture has a limit: rope fibres exist before the knot, and the proposal has nothing like them underneath.)
2. **Relation is not a thought**, and it is **stronger than "everything interacts"**. Snooker balls interact and stay the same; the proposal is that physical things are *constituted* within real relation.

## The relational substrate

The **relational substrate** is the created relational order within which physical things are constituted and through which they act. *Substrate* means "what lies beneath". It is:

- **not matter**, since matter is a stable form within it (Chapter 6);
- **not a field or an ether** under another name;
- **possibly not spatial**: whether space arises from it is open;
- **not a mind**, since it thinks and intends nothing;
- **not God and not a soul.**

One consequence follows. If relation is foundational, nothing acts on anything across a gap of non-relation. Influence is always **mediated**.

## What Scripture requires

> `Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power...`
> *Hebrews 1:3*

Scripture does not mention a relational substrate. It rules out any deep order that is self-sufficient or divine. If there is a substrate, it is a creature, and its coherence is received from Christ, not generated by itself. A world in which nothing stands on its own fits a world in which everything depends on its Creator. That is consistency, not something Scripture teaches.

## Chapter recap

- The stone's properties are relations, and universal interoperability needs explaining.
- That matter has a non-material foundation is the author's premise; how it arises (B) is a hypothesis with an unpaid debt.
- The central thesis is that relation is foundational; the substrate is a creature, not God, not a mind, not a soul.

# Chapter 4: What Can Be and What Comes to Be

Water, cooled enough, becomes ice. It never becomes glass or iron. Snowflakes differ endlessly, yet every one is six-sided. Something leaves room for huge variety and still draws firm limits.

The previous chapter proposed that things are configurations within a created order of relation. This chapter asks what bounds the configurations that order can bear, and how a particular one becomes actual.

## Possibility is not a blank cheque

What *could* happen is wider than what *does*. A coin could land heads or tails; it lands tails. But the possible is itself structured. A coin cannot land on a colour. An acorn cannot grow into a sunflower. Physics describes these bounds in many ways: conservation laws, symmetries, the fixed energy levels allowed in an atom. The bounds are part of what the world is.

The book is confident of a minimal claim: **something is prior to any particular happening and bounds it.** What kind of thing that is remains an **open question**. The book's working reading is **constrained possibility**: the shape of what created order can bear. "Possibility space" is a useful way of speaking, not a separate realm or store.

## Forbidding and favouring

A rule can shape what happens in two quite different ways:

- It can **forbid**, making something impossible: a locked door.
- It can **favour**, making something more or less likely while leaving every option open: a well-worn path.

The project's **constraint laboratory** tested every simple rule of both kinds, exactly, in tiny worlds of a few points where links form and dissolve.

The result was clean. **Only forbidding rules changed which patterns could be reached at all.** Favouring rules shifted how much time the system spent in each pattern, sometimes a lot, but never closed anything off.

These are **model results**, not discoveries about nature. They give two terms the book uses throughout:

- **Admissibility**: whether a transition is possible at all.
- **Tendency**: how likely an admissible transition is compared with the others.

Admissibility decides the shape of the possible. Tendency weights it. Tendency is not a probability anyone assigns; it names how created order is disposed among its options. The book proposes that created order has both.

One caution. In this book *admissible* means **physically possible**. It never means "morally allowed". Chapter 13 keeps those apart.

## Constraint makes things possible

We often think of limits as negative. But a river is a river because of its banks. Remove them and the water becomes a marsh, not a freer river. The rules that bound which crystals water can form are the reason ice exists at all.

Scripture describes creation in just these terms:

> `Thou hast set a bound that they may not pass over; that they turn not again to cover the earth.`
> *Psalm 104:9*

Light from darkness, sea from land, kind from kind: each division is a constraint, and each makes room for a richer world.

## From the possible to the actual

A space of possibilities does not contain a single actual stone. Something must happen. The book calls this **actualisation**: one of the possible becoming actual.

What is actual is not a bare snapshot. It carries its history, and that history changes what can happen next. So the book distinguishes:

- **Total possibility**: everything created order could ever bear.
- **What is currently reachable**: what can actually happen next, given the present state and its history.

A stone that has cracked now has a fracture line that was not there before. That does not decide whether it will break, or exactly how, but it changes how later forces act on it. A seed that has germinated can no longer stay dormant. Actual history changes what is reachable. This is an **RS working hypothesis**, and model systems show the pattern.

Note the crack carefully. It may have formed by chance. Once there, it *necessarily* changes the conditions of later fracture. **A thing that came about by chance can carry constraints that are necessary.**

One more distinction. An **opportunity** is an occasion at which some possible thing is actually available. An opportunity can pass with nothing happening: the seed that could have germinated this spring, and did not.

![Figure 2. Constrained possibility and actualisation. Circles are possible configurations and lines are admissible transitions; dashed, crossed lines are forbidden; shaded lines are favoured; the gold line is one actual history, reached by constrained chance from among the admissible routes.](v2/figures/fig-possibility.png)

## A game with no players

Chess helps. Its rules define the legal moves: that is admissibility. Some legal moves are played often and some rarely: something like tendency. And there is the game actually played tonight: actualisation.

But the analogy fails at one crucial point. In chess, **players** choose the moves. In created physical order there are no players. Nothing inside it chooses which transition happens, and nothing inside it wants anything. Press the analogy to supply a player and you have turned the substrate into a mind, the very error Scripture forbids.

So how does one transition, rather than another, become actual? Chapter 5 answers: constrained chance.

## Purpose shapes what is possible

If nothing inside creation chooses, is the world purposeless? Scripture says no:

> `While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.`
> *Genesis 8:22*

The question is *where* the purpose sits. The book's answer, a theological inference:

1. **Purpose shapes what is possible.** God's intention is expressed in what created order can and cannot bear.
2. **Possibility shapes what can become actual.**
3. **Actualisation proceeds without the substrate intending anything.**

This protects two truths. The world is not an accident, since its very possibilities were given with intent. And physical order is real and lawful in its own right, neither a puppet nor a mind in disguise. The book calls this **non-agentic runtime**: the ordinary running of creation, purposed without being purposeful in itself.

It avoids two older mistakes: that created things have no real operations of their own, and that God made the world and withdrew. And one caution: to say the substrate intends nothing is not to say God is confined to its possibilities. The primitive order is fixed for every creature that works within it. It is not fixed for its Creator. The book describes the ordinary working of what God made, not the manner of his action in miracles or redemption.

## Chapter recap

- Forbidding (admissibility) and favouring (tendency) differ, and only forbidding changes what is reachable.
- What has happened changes what can happen next; chance events can carry necessary constraints.
- Nothing inside creation chooses; purpose belongs to the Creator, who is not confined by it.

# Chapter 5: Constrained Chance

Inside a lump of old campfire charcoal is an atom of carbon-14. When will it decay? On the standard understanding of quantum physics, nobody can say, and not because we lack information. (Some interpretations dispute this.) It might decay next second or in fifty thousand years. Yet in a large sample, half the atoms will have decayed in about 5,730 years, precisely enough to date the campfire.

Open in the single case, exact in the many. This chapter is about chance: what it is, what it is not, and what the book proposes about it.

## Three meanings of "random"

1. **Lawless**: anything at all could happen, for no reason.
2. **Unpredictable to us**: there was a cause, but we could not foresee it.
3. **Probabilistic**: outcomes are open, but governed by definite odds over definite possibilities.

A fair die is the third kind. Any face can come up, but never a seven, and over thousands of throws each face appears very nearly one time in six.

The book uses the word **stochastic** for the third meaning: governed by probabilities over defined possibilities. Stochastic does not mean lawless. Nor does it mean *chaotic*. A chaotic system, like the weather, follows fixed rules, but tiny differences grow into large ones, so it is hard to predict even though nothing in it is chancy.

Calling a process stochastic says how it is *described*. It does not say *why*. The odds might reflect our ignorance of hidden details (**epistemic** probability), or real openness in the world (**ontic** openness). The same description fits both.

## Chance in physics

Quantum theory gives its predictions as probabilities, and these are confirmed with great precision. Whether nature is *fundamentally* chancy is contested: some interpretations read the probabilities as real openness, others as ignorance of a fully fixed reality, and all agree about every measurement made so far. **The probabilities are established. Whether nature is fundamentally indeterministic is not.**

## The proposal: constrained chance

Chapter 4 left a question: if nothing inside created order chooses, what makes one possible transition actual rather than another? The book proposes that **actualisation is fundamentally stochastic**, in the ontic sense. Which reachable transition occurs is *not uniquely determined* by what came before; tendency weights the outcomes without selecting one in advance.

The word **constrained** carries most of the weight:

- **Unconstrained** chance, where anything at all is equally open, would give a world without stable form.
- **Constrained** chance works inside the shape of the possible. Admissibility rules out whole regions; tendency weights what remains; and what has already happened changes what is likely next.

So the book rejects **true randomness**: happening governed by no limits or pattern at all. That would be incompatible with a coherent created order.

One confusion runs the other way. If the constraints are necessary, is the outcome determined after all? No. The rules of chess are never broken, and they never say which legal move will be made. **Determinism** would require something stronger: that the present state, under its governing relations, allows exactly one next state. The proposal is that created order is **necessary in its constraints and open in its outcomes.**

**Status:** RS working hypothesis. It fits the probabilistic character of quantum theory without following from it. A demonstration that the probabilities are only ignorance of a deterministic process beneath would count against it.

## Exact answers from chance

One of the project's computer models makes the point vivid. To add 17 and 28, it builds two small populations of positive and negative relations, mixes them, and lets them change at random under a few local rules, where opposite pairs can cancel. Thousands of runs followed thousands of different paths, and every one arrived at 45.

**An exact outcome can coexist with many chance-driven paths.** The exactness came from rules built in to conserve the total, not from chance. It is an illustration, not evidence about nature.

When a forbidden change was let through, accuracy fell: stability is always **of something, against some disturbance, over some time.**

## Chance and providence

Does chance take the world out of God's hands? Scripture is calm:

> `The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.`
> *Proverbs 16:33*

Casting lots was the ancient world's classic chance event. Scripture does not deny that the lot falls by chance. It says the whole outcome belongs to the Lord.

So the book draws a theological inference: chance is a **created way in which events unfold**, within possibilities God has shaped. It is not a rival power. And the openness is openness *with respect to what came before in creation*. It is **not a claim that God lacks knowledge** of the outcome. How God's complete knowledge and action relate to that openness is not something the book claims to explain.

Four ideas are often run together:

| Idea | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Mathematical randomness | A description uses probabilities. |
| Ontic stochasticity | What happens is genuinely open. |
| Purposelessness | Nothing is for anything. |
| Atheism | There is no God. |

None follows from the one before. Proverbs accepts the first, perhaps the second, and denies the third and fourth in one breath.

## Chapter recap

- "Stochastic" means governed by probabilities, not lawless; whether nature is chancy at bottom is contested.
- The book proposes constrained chance: necessary constraints, open outcomes, no true randomness.
- The openness concerns created determination, not God's knowledge.

# Chapter 6: Identity Through Change

You cannot step into the same river twice, said Heraclitus. The water has moved on. Yet everyone calls it the same river. A candle flame stays steady while its burning gas is replaced every moment. Much of your body's material is replaced over the years, yet you are the same person.

What makes a thing the same thing through change? Not that its stuff stays the same, because often it does not.

## What every allowed change preserves

In the calculator of Chapter 5, the relations changed constantly, but the total never moved. It lived in what every allowed change preserved.

A quantity that a set of changes leaves unchanged is an **invariant**. Physics is built on them: energy, momentum and electric charge are conserved in every process. A knot in a loop of string is a visual example: twist and squash the loop without cutting it and the knot looks different but cannot be undone. Identity can be carried by organisation rather than by stuff.

That is sound, but not the whole story.

## The same in what respect?

Two coins in your hand look identical. For shopping they are the same. Then a bank tests one and finds it is a counterfeit. The difference was there all along, but nothing you did could find it. Two keys cut by eye look alike, but only one turns the lock.

**Whether two things are "the same" depends on what could tell them apart.** A description summarises, and a summary can hide differences. Whether the hidden differences matter depends on whether anything that can actually happen will draw on them. The principle:

> **A difference can safely be ignored only where no reachable future consequence can draw on it.**

So a thing stays the same *kind* of thing when its changes keep it within its **equivalence class**: the set of states that nothing able to happen to it could tell apart. This is not "in the eye of the beholder". The counterfeit was different whether or not it was ever tested.

## Same kind, or same individual?

There is a second question, and it must not be folded into the first: **is it the same individual?**

Two coins struck from the same die can pass every test and still be two coins. A replacement for a lost coin is not the lost coin found. So keep four achievements apart:

- restoring **function** (it does the job again);
- restoring **organisation** (it has the same pattern again);
- restoring the **equivalence class** (nothing could tell it from the original);
- restoring the **same individual** (the same lineage carried on).

The first three can each happen without the fourth. What carries individual continuity in physical things is an **open question**. It will matter greatly for persons and resurrection.

## History in the present

Sometimes two present states differ only in how they came to be. A bent and straightened paper clip looks new but is weaker, because its bending remains in its metal. A healed bone carries the remodelling of its break.

The book calls this **provenance**: past differences that survive in present structure. There is no separate record attached to a thing. **The changed relations are the record.** And consequence can persist even where its history can no longer be traced.

So the actual world is not a series of memoryless snapshots. A present state carries its past, and what happens next depends on it.

From this the book draws a hypothesis about **memory**: at the physical level, memory is past differences embodied in present relations, so that they change what can happen next, as iron stays magnetised after the magnet is removed. **Status:** RS working hypothesis.

## Persistence, and its limits

A thing **persists** by continually re-establishing itself within its equivalence class, as the river does within its banks. The stone lasts centuries against rain and a moment against a hammer: stability is always of something, against something, for some time.

And **preserving is not repairing**. When one model relation was destroyed rather than rearranged, the system never recovered the original total. If anything is to be recovered after damage, the information needed must survive somewhere: in a template, a copy, a wider order or an agent able to restore it. Living things do this (Chapter 11). Restoration in the theological sense is not the world's invariants reasserting themselves (Chapter 15).

## Handling and disturbing

You can **handle** the stone: carry it, and it arrives as the same stone. Or you can **disturb** something without moving any separate thing. Clap your hands and a sound crosses the room, yet no air molecule makes the journey; what crosses is an organised pattern of pressure.

The sound is real but not one more material object. The book calls it **material organisation**: a real pattern carried by material things. Keep four things apart: the **carrier** (the air), the **activity** (the travelling pressure), the **identity** of the pattern (this note) and the **mathematical description** (a wave equation).

Some patterns test the line: a solitary wave can travel along a canal for over a mile while the water stays behind, behaving very like a thing.

## Matter as stable form

So what is the stone? The book's proposal: **matter is stable form within the relational substrate.** Atoms are very stable relational forms, not tiny self-existing pellets. A stone is a vast, coupled configuration that has stayed stable long enough to be picked up. This does not make matter less real. (Physics already tells us that most of the mass of ordinary matter comes from the energy binding quarks together, not from the quarks' own masses.)

Two kinds of emergence must be kept apart:

- **Emergence within matter**: new patterns arise from organised matter. This is observed everywhere: crystals, flames, waves, cells.
- **The emergence of matter**: material things themselves arise within a created order that is not material. That this is so is the author's premise; *how* is the book's hypothesis.

The first supplies analogies and questions for the second. It does not demonstrate it. So the book's central question is: **how does a configuration of something not itself material acquire lasting, handleable individuality?** It has not been shown.

## Time

If change is actualisation, then **time is the ordering of actualisation**: the before-and-after of change itself, not an empty container ticking on its own. It must fit what is measured, including clocks that run at different rates at different heights.

The order has a **direction** from consequence: the bell sounds because it was struck, not the reverse. The book calls this **succession** and holds it as a theological and philosophical premise. To **measure** time is to compare one recurring process with another, such as a pendulum; clocks count succession, they do not create it.

## Two kinds of sameness

> `They shall perish, but thou shalt endure: yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment... But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end.`
> *Psalm 102:26-27*

Created things persist through change and can wear out like a garment. God's sameness is of a different order: he is not a configuration and has no limits imposed on him. And whether a *person's* identity is simply a pattern of bodily configuration is a different question. The book's answer, in Chapters 12 and 15, is no.

## Chapter recap

- Same kind means nothing reachable could tell the states apart; same individual is a further, open question.
- Things carry their history, and preserving is not repairing.
- On the book's hypothesis matter is stable form; how matter emerges is unshown. Time is the ordering of change, directed by consequence.

# Chapter 7: Transmit, Carry, Receive

Sunlight falls on the stone. Its grey surface returns some light and absorbs the rest; its pale vein returns a little more. Some of that light reaches your eye. Molecules change, then cells, then nerves, and you see a grey stone with a pale vein.

The stone did not move. Nothing flew from it into your head. Yet a difference in the stone became a difference in you. The book names what is required for that **Transmit, Carry, Receive**, or **T-C-R**, and proposes that it is the grammar by which any difference in created order becomes consequential to anything else.

## The grammar

```text
distinction → preserved dependency → consequence
```

Each part is a role, not a component:

- **Transmit.** A difference in one condition is able to affect something other than itself.
- **Carry.** The dependency between that difference and its effect preserves enough of the difference for the effect to depend on it.
- **Receive.** Another state is changed, or its options are constrained, *because of* the source difference.

There is a simple test. If two different source states could never make any difference to anything downstream, the difference between them has not been carried, however much else has happened.

The grammar assumes very little. Nothing has to travel, and the source need not be used up: telling someone your name does not make you forget it.

## Carried without travelling

Drop a pebble in a still pond and a ring of waves reaches the bank. No water travelled from the centre; each parcel only bobbed a little. What crossed was a *pattern*.

Ancient peoples sent news by signal fires. A fire on one hill was seen, and a second lit on the next, and so on for a hundred miles. No fire travelled, yet the news arrived, because each fire was lit *in dependence on* the one before.

A telephone call turns your voice into electrical current, radio waves, pulses of light in glass, current again, and sound at your friend's ear. The vehicle changes several times. Your friend still hears what you said.

What must persist is **the relevant difference, preserved by a chain of dependency.** Whatever bears it at each stage is the **carrier**, and carriers can be swapped. At the deepest level the book proposes something simpler still: where two conditions are directly related, the relation itself is the carrier.

A carrier need not preserve everything. A phone line drops the fine texture of a voice but keeps the words, because nothing your friend does depends on that texture. It cannot drop the difference between "yes" and "no". This is Chapter 6's equivalence class at work.

## Reception is consequence

Light from the stone also passes through the window of a passing car. Was the window changed in a way that depended on the stone's pattern? No. It would have been the same if the stone had been another colour. The light *arrived* there, but the difference was not *received*.

A letter left unopened on the mat has arrived. A letter read and acted on has been received. **Reception is not arrival. It is consequence.**

What a receiver can receive depends on the receiver. This is **receiver compatibility**:

- Your eye registers visible light, not the radio waves passing through you.
- Receivers respond differently to repeats. A thermostat responds continuously; a light switch already on is unchanged by being switched on again; a bank ledger must record each payment exactly once.
- An FM radio must tune to the right band *and* have the right circuit to recover how the wave varies.

So **consequential reception depends on compatibility between the way a difference is carried and the way the receiver is organised.**

![Figure 3. Transmit, Carry, Receive, as it appears in manifest cases. A distinction at the source becomes capable of constraining something beyond itself; a chain of dependency preserves the relevant distinction across substitutable carriers; a compatible receiver is changed in a way that depends on it. The dependency persists while the vehicles change. At substrate depth the carrier may simply be the relation.](v2/figures/fig-tcr.png)

## Chains within chains

T-C-R **nests** (the nerve impulse is its own chain) and **composes** (light from every point of the stone forms one image). And it is **recursive**: the molecule that receives light then transmits to the cell, which transmits to the nerve. Every present state is the reception of earlier differences and a source of later ones. That is how the actual world carries its history forward.

## Why the grammar recurs

The same three-part form appears in light and sound, in DNA copying, in nerves, in speech, in law and in liturgy. Is that coincidence? The book proposes a deeper reason. If A is genuinely consequential for B, then something about A must be able to make a difference to B; that difference must be preserved; and B must be different because of it. That already *is* T-C-R. So T-C-R is not a mechanism added to a relational world. It is the form of consequential relation itself.

**Status:** RS working hypothesis, one of the book's central ones. It would be weakened by a clear case of real consequence that cannot be described as a preserved difference. And its limit matters: light, a gene and a sentence share a form of consequence. They do not thereby become the same kind of thing.

## Cause without fixed results

In a world of constrained chance a cause need not *produce* a fixed effect. It can rule out some next states, make others possible, or change how likely they are. The book's working formulation: **a cause is a difference in one condition that changes what another can become, through a dependency that preserves it.**

## Manifestation

For a hidden relational order to show up as stones and light at all, differences in it must make a difference to the world we meet. The book claims only that. It does not assume that something invisible rises through layers into view; how the two are linked is open.

## T-C-R and God's word

> `So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it.`
> *Isaiah 55:11*

> `So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God.`
> *Romans 10:17*

The shape is unmistakable: something sent, carried and received. In a creation made by the Word, that likeness is not surprising. But it is a **structural analogy**. God's word is not a physical signal, and faith is not the absorption of light. If anything, the illumination runs the other way: a world made by a God who speaks is a world in which it fits that even light and sound are consequential like this.

## Chapter recap

- T-C-R: a difference able to affect something else, a dependency that preserves it, and a change that depends on it.
- Nothing needs to travel; reception is consequence and depends on compatibility.
- God's word shares the shape as a structural analogy, not as the same kind of thing.

# Chapter 8: Organised Identity

A cellist begins the Prelude of Bach's first Cello Suite: G, D, B, A, B, D, B, D. Now hear it on a guitar, on a marimba, through a phone's tiny speaker, or from a file of numbers on a disc. The wood, strings, air and electronics are all different. In the file there is no sound at all until something plays it. Yet anyone who knows the piece recognises it at once.

Something has persisted that is not a piece of material. This chapter asks what such **organised identities** are and how they survive changes of carrier.

## One phrase, many carriers

Each instrument colours a note with its own overtones, its *timbre*. So what survives?

A melody is recognised even when it starts on a different note. What is preserved is not any frequency but the **relations between pitches**, the intervals. An octave is a ratio of two to one, a perfect fifth very nearly three to two. Rhythm survives as relations among durations, which is why a phrase played a little faster is still the same phrase.

So the phrase's identity lives largely in **relations**, not in any carrier. That much is observed, not hypothesised: **the continuity of an organised identity need not depend on one particular material carrier.**

## How much can change

A phone speaker barely reproduces the cello's lowest C, about 65 vibrations a second. Yet a listener hears that note at the right pitch. The ear and brain rebuild the missing fundamental from the pattern of overtones the speaker does produce.

There are limits. Change the intervals and it becomes a different melody. Scramble the rhythm and the phrase is gone. Identity under variation is Chapter 6's equivalence class seen from the listener's side: the range of variations a compatible receiver still treats as the same.

## Does the receiver help make the identity?

When the ear rebuilds a note the speaker never played, is it just recognising something already complete, or taking part in making the identity it hears?

The sounds and their relations are really there whether anyone listens or not. But the pitch heard is what a compatible receiver makes of them. A rainbow is a clearer case: the sunlight and raindrops are there regardless, but two people a few metres apart see bows formed by different drops. And tuning forks near a struck chord each respond to the part that matches their own frequency, with no consciousness involved.

The book's proposal, which the author regards as a credible candidate: **a receiver's compatible organisation may be part of what makes a particular identity possible as an identity that makes a difference.** This is *not* the claim that things exist only when perceived. The cello vibrates in an empty hall. **Status:** RS working hypothesis, scope open.

## Identity without its first carrier

The dodo died out more than three hundred years ago. We still know a good deal about it from sailors' accounts, paintings, bones, a head kept in Oxford and DNA. In 2018 scans revealed lead shot in the Oxford head: the bird had been shot. That difference was carried for centuries before a compatible receiver could read it.

**Preserving information about something is different from that thing continuing to exist.** And what is preserved must be sorted: the dodo as a *species*, the Oxford bird as one *individual*, and any *reconstruction*, which at most would fall within the same kind. A reconstruction would not be any dodo that lived. The same line will matter for persons: a person is not the information that survives about them.

## History as received difference

History works this way: a past event leaves differences in documents, objects and testimony, and a historian judges how much each carrier could have kept. Charles Darwin was a great practitioner of such reasoning, and knew how incomplete his carriers were; he called the rock record "a history of the world imperfectly kept". Readers differ on the scope of his theory, and the book does not decide that. Its point is about method.

## From difference to higher-order identity

Follow the music upwards. Air pressure varies, so a note can be told apart from silence. Notes stand in relations. Relations are organised into chords and lines. The organisation acquires an identity: this phrase, this movement.

The book adopts this as a working formulation of how new wholes arise:

```text
distinction → relationship → organisation → higher-order identity
```

Not every noticeable pattern is a new whole. A **higher-order identity** must pass three tests:

1. It **outlasts its own parts**, judged against a fair comparison.
2. It is **consequential as a whole**: something responds to it as a unit.
3. It can be **realised in more than one way**, as the phrase can on cello, guitar or disc.

**Status:** RS working hypothesis. It is not a ladder of separate substances.

A whole can also act back on its parts. A musical key is set up by the notes of a piece, and once established it shapes how every later note is heard. Physics has clear examples of the same loop.

## One pier at a time

None of this derives gravity or quantum mechanics from the simplest relations. In the author's image, we need not bridge the Atlantic; we can study one pier and see what it supports. The project's models are such piers, and negative results count too.

One candidate for how material individuality might arise comes from the physics of **oscillation**: interacting vibrations can lock into step, and certain shaken materials form stable, particle-like peaks. Perhaps identity could reside in relations among vibrations, as a melody's resides in its intervals. **Status:** mechanism candidate, favoured by the author, not assumed.

Music shows that new wholes emerge *within* matter. It does not show how matter itself emerges. Nor is a soul a melody played on a body: persons are a category of their own (Chapter 12).

## Chapter recap

- A melody's identity lives in relations and survives changes of carrier; a receiver may help make it manifest.
- Information about something is not the thing, and a reconstruction is not the individual.
- New wholes arise within matter; that does not show how matter emerges.

# Chapter 9: Regularity, Coordination, and Mathematics

A bicycle tyre's pressure is the combined effect of countless air molecules striking the inside wall, each moving erratically. Yet the pressure is steady and obeys a simple law: halve the volume at the same temperature and the pressure doubles.

If the book is right that constrained chance runs deep in created order, how can the world be so reliably regular? And what is the mathematics that describes it?

## Four different problems

1. Events described by probabilities can **combine into stable totals**, without settling whether the underlying events are chancy.
2. How events are **related to one another** can change the behaviour of the whole, even when each event's odds stay the same.
3. A simple law can describe the whole at one scale **without describing what happens beneath it**.
4. A description can **leave out differences** that do not matter for its question but matter for others.

## Regularity from many events

Flip a coin ten times and you might get seven heads. Flip it ten million times and the proportion will almost certainly be within a tenth of a per cent of a half. This is the **law of large numbers**, and it belongs to mainstream science.

Physics uses such statistical regularities in many places: the gas laws, the jittering of small particles in water (traced by Einstein in 1905 to the irregular impacts of molecules), and radioactive half-lives. These establish **reliable behaviour in aggregate**. They do not, by themselves, establish that the world is chancy at the smallest scale. Classical physics can produce the same regularities from fixed underlying motion.

Physicists call such large-scale laws **effective**: precise at their own scale, silent about what happens underneath. The book calls behaviour that is regular and predictable to the precision we can observe **effective determinism**. It is a fact about regularity, not a claim that nothing could have happened otherwise.

## Same odds, different behaviour

Imagine two rooms, each with a hundred people tossing fair coins at once, again and again. In the first room everyone tosses independently. In the second, people work in pairs, and each pair agrees that one will report the opposite of the other.

For every single person, in either room, the chance of heads is exactly one half. Yet the rooms behave completely differently. In the first, the number of heads wanders: 44, then 57. In the second, it is exactly fifty, every time.

```text
the same individual odds
  are not the same joint process,
  which is not the same behaviour of the whole
```

The book calls such relations among events **coordination**. Two cautions:

- Coordination is **not a controller**. Nobody directs the tosses; each pair simply acts as one.
- Coordination is **not what steadies an average**; aggregation does that. Coordination shapes the rest: fluctuations, exactness, and response to disturbance.

None of this mathematics belongs to the book's view; probability theory describes it already. What the relational proposal changes is the *reading*: on this view, the coordination of a whole's parts belongs to what the whole is, rather than being a statistic laid over separate things.

## What mathematics is doing

In 1960 the physicist Eugene Wigner wrote of "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics". How can equations describe nature so well?

Take the Navier–Stokes equations, used to design aircraft and forecast weather. They treat a fluid as smooth, though it is made of molecules in constant motion. They work because, at the scale engineers use, the molecules' combined behaviour is regular enough to be described *as if* the fluid were smooth.

Two lessons follow:

- A **fixed, deterministic law at one scale** does not show that reality is fixed at every scale.
- A **smooth mathematical description** need not be what the described thing is. Water is not the continuous field in the equations, and the equations are still true at their scale.

## Hidden structure

The project's simplest models give an exact instance of the fourth problem: hide a genuine three-way relation, watch only the two-way links, and they seem to "remember" their past, although nothing in the rules records history.

So **mathematical sameness under one description does not settle whether two things are the same**, and **a mathematical structure that describes behaviour is not, by that fact, what reality is.** Mathematics works so well because the regularities, invariants and coordinated behaviour it describes are really there. That earns it deep respect, without confusing the description with the described.

> `The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork.`
> *Psalm 19:1*

Scripture does not give us the equations. It tells us why a world in which such equations hold, and minds can find them, is what one should expect of a creation made through the Word.

## Measurement still rules

What physics measures and predicts constrains every account, this one included. What a field *is*, or whether quantum chance is fundamental, are questions of interpretation, still open.

The project's Gravity/Time models, on systems of at most seven points, found that a rule favouring some pattern of links produces something like "attraction" with no force reaching across. But the same rules do **not** tie clock rates to that pattern, so they cannot explain why clocks run slower deeper in a gravitational field. None of this is a theory of gravity or time.

## What would count against the proposals

A proposal that cannot be wrong tells us little. These would count against the book's claims, or press them hardest:

- **Contradicting measurement.** Any specific mechanism that contradicted precise measurement would fail. One already has: a 2026 model predicted the direction of an effect in metal fatigue but its size many times too small, and the failure was recorded.
- **Intrinsic properties at the base.** If physics showed its most basic entities have properties fixed independently of all interaction, the relational thesis would be seriously weakened.
- **Entanglement.** Distant entangled particles show correlations that no theory of a certain local kind can reproduce. Several interpretations explain them, but this is the hardest test for an account of mediated relation, and it is not solved here.
- **Gravity's clocks.** The project has no relational account of why clocks slow in gravity.
- **Chance is hard to test.** Present evidence cannot decide between a chancy world and a fixed one at the quantum level, so the hypothesis should be held no more firmly than a coherent hypothesis.
- **Matter's emergence.** If investigation kept failing to produce lasting, individual organisation from simpler relations, the mechanism of Proposition B would be weakened. At present the debt is unpaid, not defaulted.

## Chapter recap

- Statistical regularity does not by itself prove the world is chancy at bottom.
- The same odds can give different wholes (coordination), and mathematics describes reality without being it.
- The book names what would count against it and where it is most exposed.

# Chapter 10: Why Relation Matters Beyond Physics

On Monday you promise a friend you will help her move house on Saturday. On Saturday you would rather stay in bed. You get up and go.

For that promise to be what it seems, several things must be true. The person who gets up on Saturday is the same person who spoke on Monday. Your words went out and became something in her: a plan she relies on. Your action has consequences for her and others. And the promise *binds*: if you stayed in bed she would be wronged, not just surprised.

Identity through time, a difference carried from one person to another, consequence and obligation are basic to human life. This chapter asks how they sit with what the physical world is. It is the hinge of the book.

## Where relation enters

The serious alternatives to this book already contain a lot of relation: things as *powers* directed at other things, *fields* spread through space, or *form* ordered towards activity in the tradition of Aristotle and Aquinas. Several come close to this book's view.

So the real question is not whether an account has room for relation, but **where relation enters**. In this book's picture, relation is **constitutive** at the base: what a physical thing is cannot be specified apart from how it stands to others. The contrast is with accounts in which basic things are fully what they are *before* any relation, so that relation is something they *have* or *do*, not what they *are*.

That difference looks small in physics. It matters when the account reaches people, who are, in part, *who they are* through parents, promises, friendships, history and standing before God.

## The argument

1. Human life, as we live it and as Scripture describes it, is full of constitutive relation, identity through change, consequence passing between persons, and responsibility.
2. A physical account whose basic things are complete before any relation must introduce constitutive relation for the first time at some higher level, as a new kind of reality.
3. An account in which relation is constitutive at the base already contains the *form* of these features, so living, personal and moral relation are continuous in form with the physical world, while remaining different in kind.
4. Other things being equal, an account with fewer unexplained gaps between its domains is more globally coherent.
5. Therefore, *if* a relation-first picture fits everything we observe, it is to be preferred on grounds of global coherence.

The argument does not claim other views make promises impossible. It claims something more modest: relation-first offers a more economical **continuity of form** from stones to persons, provided the categories stay distinct. Step 5 is conditional, which is why Chapters 3 to 9 took such care over the physics.

## Continuity, not reduction

The shared relational grounding lets physical cause, living organisation, action, communication and social life belong to one created order. But it is continuity across boundaries, not collapse:

- A promise is not a force.
- Love is not a statistic.
- Sin is not decay.
- The soul is not a carrier.
- Resurrection is not the substrate repairing itself.

The relational picture does not tell you that you *ought* to keep your promise. It removes the strangeness of a world in which promises could bind at all.

## The strongest objection: emergence

"Promises don't exist among particles; they *emerge*, as wetness emerges from molecules that are not wet. Why can't constitutive relation emerge from a base without it?"

The book accepts emergence fully. But in familiar cases what emerges expresses capacities the base already has, as wetness expresses the attraction molecules have for one another. If the base contains only things complete before relation, constitutive relation must appear from nowhere: the gap step 2 names. The book's claim is not to derive morality from physics. It is that what emerges in personal and moral life arises from a base that already has its form.

## The poisoned river

A factory discharges waste into a river. Fish die, and villagers who drink the water fall ill. Each link is physical: chemicals released, carried and received as changes in bodies. But the case is also moral: someone is responsible, and the villagers have been wronged.

Take it round a cycle, from the physical chain to responsibility to remedy and back to cleaner water, and every step concerns the same world.

Scripture binds human wrongdoing and the land together:

> `By swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing, and committing adultery, they break out, and blood toucheth blood. Therefore shall the land mourn...`
> *Hosea 4:2-3*

> `For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now.`
> *Romans 8:22*

The book does not say relational physics *explains* these texts; their explanation is theological. It says a relational world sits naturally with the world they describe. The author goes further, holding that creation is designed as the theatre of moral consequence and the language in which acts take effect. **Status:** a theological premise about creation's purpose, not derived from physics.

## Coherence, error and convergence

An argument can become *more* coherent while moving *further* from the truth, if every step builds on an early mistake. While developing an AI-assisted method that runs several lines of reasoning on one problem, the author saw this happen: when the lines were repeatedly merged and fed back, agreement rose, and an error in one early line hardened into confident consensus. The project calls this a **false attractor**.

The remedy is to ask where agreement came from. When **genuinely independent** lines reach results that fit together, as sailors' accounts, bones and a genome do for the dodo, their agreement carries weight. Many voices repeating one source are one source.

And error has an anatomy. In "2 + 2 = 5" almost everything is sound; only the asserted result is wrong. When a claim fails, find the relationship that failed and keep what remains sound. (This is about mistakes, not moral evil.)

## Relation across categories

![Figure 4. Relation across categories. Material, living, personal, moral and spiritual realities are distinct kinds, not layers built one out of another; relation runs through each in its own way; God is not a category within creation but its Creator, who upholds all things.](v2/figures/fig-categories.png)

- In the **physical** world, relation is constraint, sameness and carried difference.
- In the **living** world, it becomes metabolism, signalling and repair.
- In the **personal** world, it becomes knowledge, love, promise and trust.
- In the **moral** world, it becomes obligation, right and wrong.
- In the **spiritual** world, it becomes communion with God.
- God's relation to creation is that of Creator to creature, belonging to no category within it.

## Chapter recap

- Relation-first makes relation constitutive at the base, leaving fewer unexplained gaps between stones and persons, if the physics holds.
- This is continuity of form, never reduction.
- Coherence can mislead; independent convergence is the safeguard.

# Chapter 11: Life

Turn the stone over. Underneath, in the damp, are a woodlouse, a pale root, a thread of fungus and a seed beginning to split. Now the book faces things that feed, grow, respond, repair themselves and make more of their own kind.

## Life is given

> `And God said, Let the earth bring forth the living creature after his kind... and it was so.`
> *Genesis 1:24*

> `Thou hidest thy face, they are troubled: thou takest away their breath, they die, and return to their dust. Thou sendest forth thy spirit, they are created.`
> *Psalm 104:29-30*

Living things are real, active and their own: the earth *brings forth* them. Yet their life is never self-grounding. It is given, upheld and finally withdrawn by God. The account that follows describes how life *operates* within created order. It does not describe where life ultimately comes from.

## Sustained organisation

The stone persists mostly by being left alone. A living thing persists the opposite way: constantly taking in matter and energy, transforming them and expelling what it does not need. A woodlouse is not a fixed heap of molecules. It is a pattern that keeps re-establishing itself, like a flame but far more intricate. It maintains itself, repairs damage, responds, grows and reproduces.

So life is **sustained organisation**: persistence actively maintained by the living thing itself. In Chapter 6's terms, it keeps re-actualising itself within its equivalence class, and actively corrects departures from it. A living thing does not merely persist. It works to stay what it is.

## Signals and templates

Much of that work is T-C-R. A cell detects food or a hormone when a molecule at its surface changes shape; that change is passed along a chain and received as a switched-on gene or a movement begun. Hormones carry differences between organs; nerves carry impulses. In living things reception is not passive: a cell *regulates* which signals it will receive.

Chapter 6 warned that preserving is not repairing. Life meets that problem with **templates**: patterns from which its organisation can be rebuilt. The best known is DNA. It takes part in shaping how proteins are built, when genes are active and how cells divide and repair. When DNA itself is damaged, the cell often repairs it from the complementary strand.

DNA is better described as a **constraint map** than a program. It does not command the cell like software. It takes part in a much larger web of constraints: the cell's chemistry, membranes, surroundings and history. A strand of DNA in a test tube does nothing.

## Chance within life

Living things are full of molecular chance: molecules collide erratically, genes switch on in bursts, copying makes occasional errors. Yet development is astonishingly reliable. A fertilised egg grows into an organism of its kind, again and again. Feedback, redundancy and buffering correct for fluctuations. Development is a striking case of **constrained chance producing reliable form**.

Chance also plays a part in variation between generations. Genesis's "after his kind" speaks of patterned continuity, not rigid sameness. Living order includes both fidelity and variation: all rigidity would be lifeless repetition, and all randomness would mean nothing was inherited. The first edition called this **bounded generativity**. How wide the bounds are, and how far kinds have changed over history, are questions on which Christians who share this book's commitments disagree, and the book does not settle them.

## Wholes and their parts

A tissue is more than a crowd of cells. No extra substance is added at each level. Rather, when parts are **coupled** so that they no longer behave independently, a disturbance to one spreads through the whole, and the whole has properties no part has, like a temperature it regulates or a shape it restores (Chapter 9).

The body is full of such coupling: heartbeat with breathing, both with the nervous system, and feedback holding temperature and blood sugar in narrow ranges. The organism is less a thing that has a pattern than a pattern that keeps itself in being.

## Direction and purpose

Living things are directed: a seed grows towards the plant it will be, a wound heals, and the eye is *for* seeing.

The book takes this directedness to be real, and built into the form a living thing has been given. It fits Chapter 4: purpose belongs to the Creator and is expressed in what is possible, while the organism is not a little mind planning its own growth.

> `I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvellous are thy works.`
> *Psalm 139:14*

God's forming of a living thing does not compete with its development. It works through it.

## What life is not

- Life is **not a special substance or force** added to matter, a "vital spark". Living things are built from the same created order as stones.
- Life is **not "nothing but chemistry"**. Organisation is real and explanatory, and the living whole is a unity, not a heap. And Scripture insists life is given and sustained by God.

That leaves living things where Genesis puts them: real, fruitful, ordered after their kinds and dependent on their Creator. And it brings the book to the threshold Genesis marks with a third act of creation: humanity.

## Chapter recap

- Life is God's gift; the book describes how it operates, not where it comes from.
- Life is sustained organisation, and constrained chance yields reliable form within bounded variation.
- Life is neither a vital spark nor "nothing but chemistry".

# Chapter 12: Dust, Breath, and Person

Go back to seeing the stone. Light carried its differences to your eye, and signals passed along your nerves. Chapter 7 followed that chain as far as physical description can. But the chain did not end in a nerve. It ended in *you*: someone who saw a stone, found it beautiful or dull, and decided to pick it up. The one who sees is not one more link in the chain. The one who sees is a person.

## Formed from the dust

> `So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.`
> *Genesis 1:27*

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

The sequence is exact. A body is *formed* from *dust*, the same material as the rest of the earth. *Breath* is given directly by God. The result is a *living soul*, a living person.

So your body belongs fully to the physical order of Chapters 3 to 9 and the living order of Chapter 11. But dust alone is not a person. What sets humanity apart is not its material but what God has made it to be.

## Embodiment is good

Genesis does not treat body and breath as enemies yoked together. The body is part of what a human person is, not a prison, which is why Christian hope is the resurrection of the body (Chapter 15).

It follows that a moral act is not a spiritual event with a bodily sign attached. When someone lies, the words, the body that speaks them and their effects on others belong to the act. Its moral reality, a wrong before God and against a neighbour, is not the same thing as those events, and not separate from them either. Within one person **the two are causally connected, not identical** (Chapter 1).

## Personhood is deeper than embodiment

Every person we meet has a body, so it is natural to assume that being a person *is* being a certain kind of body. Christian faith rules this out.

> `God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.`
> *John 4:24*

The Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are persons. The Father and the Spirit have no bodies. The Son was a person eternally before he took a human body. If personhood were made of embodiment, the doctrine of the Trinity would be incoherent. So personhood is the deeper category.

- A **person** is a centre of awareness, relation, agency and responsibility: one who can know and be known, speak and be spoken to, act and answer for the act.
- **Embodiment** is the way creaturely persons exist and act within creation. For humans it is not optional. But personhood as such is not a property of bodies.

## The inner person

> `And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul.`
> *Matthew 10:28*

> `For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.`
> *2 Corinthians 4:16*

These texts give no technical theory of the soul, and Christians debate the details. But together they show that the inner person is real, not killed by those who kill the body, and renewed while the outward person wastes.

Here the book applies its own guard at full strength. **The soul is not a configuration of the relational substrate.** Nothing in the physical account describes, explains or contains it. Persons are relational beings in a relational creation, but they are not made of the same relations as stones.

## Consciousness

Consciousness clearly depends on the body. Damage the brain and awareness changes or is lost. Yet why there is anything it is like to see grey at all is a question no physics has answered; philosophers call it the *hard problem*.

**The Relational Substrate does not explain consciousness.** It claims something much smaller: a world in which differences already become consequences for other things is a more fitting setting for conscious persons than a world of sealed objects. The step from a changed molecule in the eye to the experience of seeing is not taken by the substrate.

## Knowing and choosing

Knowing and choosing are real events in persons: you recognise the stone, and you pick it up, and it is somewhere it would not otherwise be. But created order itself is not a knower. It is not conscious, and it did not become conscious by growing complex.

## Image and calling

Genesis joins the image of God to a charge: humanity is made in God's image *and* given care of the earth. The book reads the image broadly. It is not a material feature of the body. It is a status and a calling: to know God and be known by him, to answer to him and to represent his care in creation. Animals are not called to account; humans are.

> `Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine.`
> *Isaiah 43:1*

What makes a person *this* person is not finally a configuration of dust, however stable. It is being known, called and named by God.

## The identity of a person

Chapter 6 showed how things stay the same kind through change. Persons resemble that: your material is replaced while you remain you, and your character and memories carry your history. The resemblance is real and helps explain why replacing atoms does not threaten who you are. But it is a structural analogy, not a definition. Personal identity is grounded more deeply, in the continuity of the inner person and in God's knowing and calling. That is why death does not end a person, and why resurrection is the raising of the same person, not a copy (Chapter 15).

## Chapter recap

- Humans are dust and breath; an act and its moral reality are causally connected, not identical.
- Personhood is deeper than embodiment, and the soul is not a configuration of the substrate.
- The book does not explain consciousness; personal identity rests on God's knowing and calling.

# Chapter 13: What Is Allowed

A surgeon draws a scalpel across a patient's skin. An attacker draws a knife across a victim's skin. Physically the two acts may be almost identical. One is healing and the other a crime. Nothing in the physics of the cut tells you which.

Persons now enter the world of constrained possibility as agents who act and answer for what they do. So when we say something is "allowed", what kind of allowing do we mean?

## Three kinds of "allowed"

| Question | Name | If the answer is no |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can it happen at all, within created order? | **Physical admissibility** | Impossible for anyone. |
| Is it within this agent's power? | **Operational admissibility** | Possible, but not for this agent. |
| Is it right? | **Moral admissibility** | Possible and within power, and still wrong. |

The three are independent. Lying is physically possible, within ordinary human power, and wrong. Sustaining creation in being happens, but it is within no creature's power; it belongs to God alone. Creating something beautiful is possible, within power and good.

> `And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat.`
> *Genesis 3:6*

The fruit was within reach and within their power. What was forbidden was the means: wisdom seized by taking what God had withheld. The act was allowed in the first two senses and not the third.

Running the layers together produces three common mistakes: *if nature produces it, it cannot be wrong*; *if it is wrong, God will not let it happen*; and *if we can now do it, we should*.

## Why the physical cannot settle the moral

Chapter 10 argued that a relational world removes the gulf between the physical creation and a life of consequence and responsibility. This chapter says exactly what that does and does not imply.

It **does** imply that moral acts are real events. When you lie, a falsehood is sent, carried and received, and becomes a change in someone who now believes something untrue.

It **does not** imply that any physical fact settles what is right. The physics of a lie and of a true statement is the same physics. A relational account can tell you your words will have consequences. It cannot tell you that you ought to tell the truth.

> `He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?`
> *Micah 6:8*

What is good has been *shown*: revealed by God and written on the heart (Romans 2:14-15). It is not deduced from the behaviour of matter. Moral admissibility is a real layer of created order, and its source is the will and character of God.

## Alignment

The book uses **alignment** for fitting the order a thing or person is made for. A bone set straight heals aligned. For persons, alignment is a moral and relational reality, not a measurement: living in right relation to God, to others and to creation. Justice, mercy, faithfulness, truthfulness and love are what alignment looks like in persons.

One guard. The first edition said that beauty, order, truth and goodness are "four perspectives on the same structural property". That went too far, because it risked turning moral realities into physical ones. This edition withdraws it. Goodness, truth and beauty are related, and in God they are one, but in creation they are not one physical measure.

## Agency and responsibility

Moral admissibility only makes sense where persons genuinely act and answer for their actions. Christian traditions differ on what that requires. Some hold that responsibility needs the real ability to have done otherwise. Others hold that it is compatible with God's ordering of all things, so long as persons act willingly. This book does not decide between them.

> `I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life.`
> *Deuteronomy 30:19*

One point falls within the book's own subject. A world in which outcomes are not fixed in advance (Chapter 5) does not settle human freedom either way. **Constrained chance is not freedom.** A coin toss is not a choice, and the book does not claim that human choice is molecular chance. Agency belongs to persons, in their own category.

## Time as moral opportunity

> `So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.`
> *Psalm 90:12*

The days are counted as an opportunity, given and limited, for wisdom. The book calls this **time as moral opportunity**. It is not a second kind of time or a clock unit. It is what the succession of Chapter 6 means for a person who must act within it.

Opportunity is relative to the person. An athlete's two minutes of competition rest on months of preparation. Earlier choices change what a person can do and even which options they can see. They shape what follows without deciding it. Responsibility follows the same shape:

> `For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required.`
> *Luke 12:48*

That is a difference of responsibility, not of worth.

## Boundaries that free

It is common to think every moral boundary is a loss of freedom. Chapter 4 argued that physical constraint makes things possible: a river needs its banks. Scripture says the same of the moral world:

> `The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple.`
> *Psalm 19:7*

> `And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts.`
> *Psalm 119:45*

A boundary rightly placed gives good action its shape. This is a structural analogy: the constraint that shapes a crystal and the commandment that shapes a life share a form, not a nature, since the commandment is spoken by a Person to persons.

## Chapter recap

- Physically possible, within one's power, and right are three independent questions; the physical never settles the moral.
- Alignment is a moral and relational condition, and constrained chance is not freedom.
- Time is moral opportunity, and good boundaries free.

# Chapter 14: Corruption

The stone is beautiful in the morning light, but there is litter on the path, the river may be poisoned, and the person who sees the stone may be grieving or lying to someone they love. The world is good, and it is also deeply wrong.

## Real, but not ultimate

> `Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned.`
> *Romans 5:12*

Scripture rules out two opposite mistakes: treating disorder as ultimate, so that goodness is an illusion, and treating it as superficial, a minor glitch. Since Augustine, Christians have understood corruption as **parasitic**: a distortion of what is good, with no being of its own. Evil cannot create; it can only spoil.

## The subjection of creation

> `cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee... for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.`
> *Genesis 3:17-19*

Three things need to be kept apart:

1. **What the text says.** The ground is cursed because of human disobedience. Paul adds that creation "was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope" (Romans 8:20). The subjection is God's act.
2. **The author's interpretation.** The curse was a deliberate divine realignment of created order to the consequences of disobedience, so that creation would go on telling the truth. A world in which the breach with God had no visible consequence would teach falsely. **Status:** theological interpretation.
3. **The mechanism, which is open.** The author thinks God changed the deepest conditions of created order. Other readings: he changed settings within an order that otherwise stayed the same, or brought about a new state under the original conditions. Genesis describes none of them.

The author also holds that **animal death** entered with this realignment, and connects it with Genesis 3:21, where God made "coats of skins" for Adam and Eve. That the skins mark the first death is the author's reading; the text names no lamb and no first death. And the position meets a real difficulty: **the fossil record shows animal death in rock layers conventionally dated long before humans.** The book's own standard does not allow that to be ignored. It states the author's position, names the conflict as an open problem, and does not resolve it.

On every reading, the subjection is God's act, and God's acts are not corrupt. It is a judgment, coherent and given "in hope". And not every suffering follows from the sufferer's sin. Asked whose sin made a man blind, Jesus answered, "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3). Creation's subjection is the condition of all, not a ledger of each.

## Corruption as persisting misalignment

A wrong act is wrong in itself, even if it never happens again: a single lie, swiftly repented, was still a lie. But misalignment that is not restored tends to **entrench**: it hardens into habit, narrows what a person can see and choose, and reaches into others.

> `But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin.`
> *Hebrews 3:13*

> `Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and when sin is finished, it bringeth forth death.`
> *James 1:15*

Chapter 6 showed that things carry their history: a bent paper clip keeps its bending. Something with the same form appears in character. A habit of deceit makes the next deceit easier. Sin itself belongs to persons in their relation to God, and is no configuration of the substrate. But its embodied consequences are real, carried in a person's dispositions, body and relationships.

## Corruption in transmission

Chapter 7's grammar gives a second lens, used only where it helps:

- A **lie** sends out a false difference to be received and acted on.
- A **broken promise** breaks a carriage that was relied on.
- **Gossip** distorts in carriage.
- A lie believed changes what someone does, which changes others, until its effects can never be traced back. That history is real, carried in all the changed relations. Consequence persists where its origin is lost.

Corruption can also be a **failure of reception**: a word that arrives and is not heard.

> `I spake unto you, rising up early and speaking, but ye heard not; and I called you, but ye answered not.`
> *Jeremiah 7:13*

## Five patterns

**Degradation** (a friendship starved of attention), **fragmentation** (a family that has stopped speaking), **inversion** (a government that uses its people), **counterfeit** (virtue performed for an audience) and **parasitism** (a predatory loan).

None is creative. All depend on an order still working around them.

## Suffering and sin

Scripture gives voice to suffering: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1). And humans are not only victims but agents: "All we like sheep have gone astray" (Isaiah 53:6). Corruption is personal and culpable, not just impersonal decay. The relational account shows how wrongdoing reaches others and the land. It cannot make sin less personal.

## Limitation is not corruption

Creatures need sleep, know in part and depend on others. None of this is a defect; Genesis calls it very good. If every limit were evil, the only salvation would be to stop being a creature. Scripture's hope is creatures restored *as* creatures. **Limitation is good creaturehood. Corruption is its distortion.**

## Darkness and remembrance

The darkness of Genesis 1:2 is not evil. Moral darkness is something else: the created capacity for concealment seized for evasion and deceit. "Men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19).

What holds a people aligned over time? Scripture's answer is **remembrance**: the Passover, the feasts, the retelling of the exodus and the Lord's Supper, "this do in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). Each is a repeated return, so that what God did is received again in each generation. Forgetting is an ordinary road into drift.

## Chapter recap

- Corruption is parasitic: it spoils and cannot create, and unrestored wrong entrenches.
- The subjection of creation is God's act; the realignment reading is the author's, its mechanism open, and the animal-death claim faces an open fossil-record problem.
- Limitation is not corruption, and remembrance renews alignment.

# Chapter 15: Restoration

Optimism, distraction and denial are not hope. Hope, in the strong sense, needs reality itself to hold more promise than corruption can destroy. If disorder is ultimate, hope is theatre. If creation is God's, hope may be the most realistic stance there is.

## Restoration is God's act

Chapter 6 showed that preserving is not repairing: when information is destroyed, nothing in the system brings it back. Living things partly overcome this with templates (Chapter 11), but every living thing dies. So the healing of creation cannot come from creation's own resources.

> `And, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself; by him, I say, whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven.`
> *Colossians 1:20*

The One through whom all things were made is the One through whom all things are reconciled. Redemption is not a process inside the substrate or a law of nature. It is the Creator's act. The resurrection of Christ was not an unusually improbable configuration of matter. The book receives miracles as Scripture records them. It does not explain them as hidden physics, and it does not explain them away.

## The scope of hope

> `Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God.`
> *Romans 8:21*

> `And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain... Behold, I make all things new.`
> *Revelation 21:4-5*

The hope is not souls escaping a ruined world. It is the whole creation delivered, all things reconciled, new heavens and a new earth.

On a picture of sealed objects, the redemption of persons and the renewal of creation would be two unrelated projects. On the relational account, persons are formed from the dust of a creation relational from its foundation, so their healing and creation's belong together. The ontology does not *explain* redemption. It shows why Scripture's redemption is redemption of *this* world.

## Restoring reception

Chapter 14 described corruption partly as a failure of reception. Scripture describes restoration partly as reception restored:

> `A new heart also will I give you... and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh.`
> *Ezekiel 36:26*

A heart of stone does not receive; a heart of flesh does. The gospel that came "not... in word only, but also in power, and in the Holy Ghost" (1 Thessalonians 1:5) has been received, not merely arrived. This is a structural analogy. The Spirit's work is not a physical process, and faith is not a change in a receptor. What is shared is only the outline: something sent, carried and received as a change in the one who hears.

## The same person, raised

> `Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see.`
> *Luke 24:39*

> `It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption... It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body.`
> *1 Corinthians 15:42, 44*

The risen Christ is recognisably himself, with his wounds. Resurrection is the raising of the same persons, transformed, not the manufacture of replacements. Scripture says it concerns everyone, to life or to judgment (John 5:28-29).

How can the same person be raised when the body has returned to dust? Chapter 6 showed that a reconstruction can be the same kind without being the same individual. Chapter 12 added that a person's identity is grounded in the inner person and in God's knowing and calling. So resurrection is **not** the substrate preserving a person through death, and **not** an indistinguishable copy. The identity of the person raised is held by God. The book makes no claim about the mechanism: "it doth not yet appear what we shall be" (1 John 3:2). What it contributes is clarity about why resurrection cannot be a natural process, and so must be, as Scripture says, the act of God.

## Death and the Cross

Scripture presents human death as an enemy to be destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26). The author's further view that animal death entered with the Fall carries the open fossil-record problem of Chapter 14. On any reading, the death of persons is not what God made them for.

Christ's death is presented as yielded, not suffered helplessly:

> `No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again.`
> *John 10:18*

The suffering and death were real. But corruption did not overtake him: "neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption" (Psalm 16:10).

## Restored alignment can be higher than before

As theological and pastoral reflection: when disorder is truthfully faced, repented of and repaired, the result can be an alignment more tested, chosen and humble than untested innocence, like a marriage rebuilt after rupture. Restoration does not erase history; the wound and the repair both stay part of the story.

Four clarifications:

1. **Disorder is not good in itself**, and evil is not God's means of producing good. God redeems evil that occurred; that is a greater claim than that evil was needed.
2. **Restoration is costly.** At the scale of creation it cost the Cross (Romans 5:6).
3. **Freedom is for love and trust**, not a licence to corrupt.
4. **Freedom never required evil.** Before the Fall the command was real (Genesis 2:16-17), yet creation was "very good" (Genesis 1:31). A genuine moral choice existed with no actualised evil.

## Consummation and judgment

Scripture's hope is not just a return to Eden but consummation: death "swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). Judgment belongs to that hope. Without it, redemption would be sentimental. Scripture is sober about those who refuse (Matthew 25:46).

Such judgment does not depend on what a damaged record can show. Created order carries every act's consequences, but not always its origin; God knows each act's intention, circumstances and effects, which is why his judgment can be just where human courts fail.

## A foretaste now

Between Christ's resurrection and the renewal of all things, Scripture places the gathered people of God. In local churches believers pray, worship, teach, forgive and remember. They do not bring in the new creation; that is God's work. But they can be real, if imperfect, places where restored relation becomes visible.

## Chapter recap

- Restoration is God's act, for the whole creation.
- Resurrection is God raising the same person, not a reconstruction.
- Restored alignment can be deeper than before, but evil was never necessary.

# Chapter 16: One World, Many Kinds

The stone is still on the path. Nothing about it has changed since the first page. What has changed, the book hopes, is how much can be seen in it.

It is a creature, made and held by God, not divine and not self-explaining. On the book's proposal it is a stable form within a created order of relation: real and distinct, but constituted within relation. Its possible forms were bounded and weighted before any became actual, and those bounds were shaped by purpose, though the stone intends nothing. Its history unfolded by constrained chance. It persists because its changes preserve what it is. It reaches you because a difference at its surface changes the light, is preserved, and becomes a consequence in you.

And you, who see it, are formed from the same dust, alive by the same gift as the woodlouse beneath it, and yet a person: known and called by God, answerable for what you do, wounded by corruption and offered restoration. You belong to the same world as the stone, and you are not the same kind of thing.

## The shape of the whole

God creates and upholds reality. The book's deepest proposal is that created physical order is **relational rather than object-first**, with outcomes drawn by **constrained chance** from what is forbidden and favoured, and **T-C-R** as the way any difference becomes consequential. **Matter** is one achievement of that order; that it rests on a non-material foundation is a premise, and how is a hypothesis.

Because the physical world is relational from its foundation, life, persons, morality, corruption and restoration need not be bolted onto a world of a different kind. Yet the categories stay distinct. The substrate is not the soul. A law of physics is not a commandment. Creation is not God.

## What kind of confidence this deserves

| Kind of claim | Examples | How it is held |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scripture teaches | Creation; Creator and creature; the reality of persons; corruption; resurrection | Commitments of faith, received not derived |
| Observation and mathematics | Physics' precise predictions; quantum probabilities; stable large-scale laws | Not in dispute, though no single interpretation inherits their authority |
| Philosophical argument | Description does not settle ontology; no elastic explanations; plausibility without numbers; analogy is not identity; global coherence beats local success | Stands or falls with the arguments |
| The book's hypotheses | Relation is foundational; constrained chance; T-C-R; matter's emergence; the grammar of emergence | Coherent and workable in models, which do not show nature works this way |
| Open | How matter emerges; what gravity is; consciousness; the fossil-record interface | Named, not hidden |

That calls for **disciplined confidence**: confidence where the grounds are strong, openness where they are not, and honesty about which is which. The models have also marked limits, such as no relational account of gravity's effect on clocks (Chapter 9).

## The limits of creaturely knowing

> `Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.`
> *Job 38:4*

> `For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known.`
> *1 Corinthians 13:12*

We know from inside creation, learning its behaviour well without its inner nature being fixed by what we learn. But Job's question is no rebuke to inquiry. Partial knowledge is still knowledge, and searching out creation is one of the honours God gives to those made in his image.

The author reads Christ's words "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6) with this in mind: the way as a road walked, the truth as what grounds it, the life as sharing in it. That is a theological reading, offered as one. Truth is what is so. The way towards it is walked by creatures, and is not the same as the truth it seeks.

The fitting response to such a world is **wonder**: the response of a creature to coherence that is intelligible and yet exceeds what it can grasp. And creation's **beauty**, on the author's account the visible expression of the patterns of goodness, can be met in created things without being exhausted by them.

## Living in a relational world

Not deductions from physics, but ways of living that fit the grain of the world:

- **Honour science**, and keep it in its place: it describes how creation behaves, not everything it is or is for.
- **Take consequence seriously.** Words, promises, neglect and care all travel.
- **Seek wisdom, not just technique.** "Through wisdom is an house builded" (Proverbs 24:3).
- **Care for creation**, which groans with us and waits for its deliverance.

## The end of the matter

> `Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil.`
> *Ecclesiastes 12:13-14*

The framework ends there, because that is where everything it describes finally rests.
