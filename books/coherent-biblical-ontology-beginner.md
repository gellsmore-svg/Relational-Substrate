# Before You Begin

This book has two hard words in its title. Let's deal with them first.

**Ontology** is a long word for a simple question: *what is something, really?* Not what it looks like, and not only what it does, but what it actually is. This book asks that question about the whole world.

**Biblical** means the book starts with the Bible. It does not begin by asking science for permission to talk about God. It begins where the Bible begins, with God making a world that is really there.

So *Coherent Biblical Ontology* means a clear picture of what the world really is, one that hangs together and starts from the Bible.

## Four kinds of claim

The book makes different kinds of claim, and it tries to tell you which is which. Watch for these four:

- **The Bible says so.** For example, God made the world.
- **We can see or measure it.** For example, a dropped stone falls.
- **This is the book's best idea.** It might be right, and it might be wrong. The book will tell you why it thinks the idea is worth holding, and what would count against it.
- **Nobody knows yet.** Some questions are open, and the book says so.

The most important thing to remember is this: the book's big new idea, which it calls the *Relational Substrate*, belongs to the third kind. It is a careful guess about how God's world works. It is not something the Bible teaches, and the book will never pretend that it is.

## One ordinary scene

Every chapter comes back to one scene: a clear morning, and a stone on the path in front of you. Almost every question in this book is hiding there: what the stone is, how you see it, why it is the same stone tomorrow, and who *you* are, the one who sees it.

## A note for readers of earlier editions

Earlier versions of this book described particular mechanisms, such as tiny "knots" that make up matter, as if they were settled. This edition does not rely on them. They remain ideas worth testing, and nothing more.

A free copy of this book is available online, meant to be shared rather than sold. You can find it, and the other books in the series, at <https://relational-substrate.blogspot.com/>.

# Chapter 1: Starting With the Bible

Imagine you arrive in a country where you do not speak the language. You could try to work everything out yourself, guessing as you go. Or you could use a guidebook written by someone who knows the place well.

Using the guidebook is not cheating. It is the sensible thing to do. This book treats the Bible in that way. If God made the world, then what God says about the world is the best place to start.

> `All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness.`
> *2 Timothy 3:16*

## What the Bible is, and is not

The Bible is not a science textbook in disguise. It tells us who made the world, what kind of world it is, what went wrong with it and what God is doing about it. It does not tell us how electricity works.

So the Bible sets the *boundaries*, and inside them there is plenty of room to explore. When the Bible and science seem to clash, the problem is usually a human mistake: a Bible passage pushed to say more than it says, or a scientific result stretched beyond what was measured.

The book keeps one rule all the way through. When it puts forward its own idea, it will say whether:

- the **Bible teaches** something,
- the idea simply **fits** what the Bible teaches,
- the idea is a **possible way** something the Bible says could work, or
- the Bible **raises a question** the idea explores.

Only the first carries the Bible's authority. The others never borrow it.

## A world that is really there

The Bible begins with an act, not an idea:

> `In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep.`
> *Genesis 1:1-2*

Look at the order. In verse 1 the world is created. In verse 2 the earth is already there, though unformed. There is water, and there is darkness. Only after that does God say, "Let there be light."

So the darkness in verse 2 is not *nothing*. The earth and the waters are there with it. And it is not called evil. It is part of a real world before light was spoken. That matters, because it means that what you cannot see is not less real than what you can. The New Testament says the same:

> `Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear.`
> *Hebrews 11:3*

The things we see rest on things we do not see. Keep that in mind when we ask what the stone is made of.

## God is not the world

The second big lesson is the most important boundary in the book. God made the world, and the world is not God.

> `For by him were all things created... and by him all things consist.`
> *Colossians 1:16-17*

"By him all things consist" means that everything holds together in him. Nothing in creation holds itself together by its own power. And nothing in creation, however huge or wonderful, is God or part of God.

This book will suggest that there is a deep, hidden order underneath the physical world. The Bible closes one door before we even start. That deep order, if it exists, is something God made. It is not God, it is not a mind, and it is not a soul.

## Different kinds of reality

Genesis also shows that creation contains different kinds of things. God creates the world (Genesis 1:1), then living creatures (1:21), then human beings in his own image (1:27). A person is formed from the dust, like the rest of the world, and then God breathes life into him:

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

So there are different kinds of reality: things like stones, living things, persons and spiritual realities. God is not one more kind among them. He is the Maker of them all.

## The same word, not the same thing

Here is a trap to avoid. The Bible uses the word *light* for the light God made in Genesis 1, for Jesus ("I am the light of the world", John 8:12) and for God himself ("God is light", 1 John 1:5). Does that mean God is made of sunlight? Of course not.

The word is not used by accident, though. Light makes things visible, and Jesus does something *like* that when he shows us the truth about God. The two share a *likeness of form*. They are not the *same kind of thing*. Whenever this book points out a pattern that appears in very different places, that is what it means. It never means that God's work is secretly physics.

## What this chapter has shown

- The Bible sets the boundaries, and the book's own ideas never borrow its authority.
- The world is real from the start; God holds it together, and it is not God.
- Creation has different kinds of reality, and a shared pattern is not a shared nature.

# Chapter 2: Knowing How and Knowing What

Drop the stone. It falls. Scientists can tell you exactly how fast it will be moving when it hits the ground. They can work out the path of the Moon and keep satellite navigation accurate to a few metres. We know how gravity *behaves* extremely well.

Now ask a different question: what *is* gravity? Is it a pull reaching across space? Is it a bending of space and time? Clever people who agree about every measurement still disagree about this. Nobody disagrees that the stone falls. They disagree about what kind of thing is making it fall.

That difference, between knowing how something behaves and knowing what it is, is the subject of this chapter.

## The machine you cannot open

Think of a vending machine. You put in a coin, press a button, and a drink comes out. Do it often enough and you learn a lot: which button gives which drink, what happens when you use the wrong coin, what it will never do. That is real knowledge.

But you still do not know what is inside. Two machines with completely different insides could behave the same way for every button you press. Knowing what a thing *does* is not the same as knowing what it *is*.

That is how we meet the world. Scientists study what things do, very carefully, and that knowledge is real and precious. But the deepest questions about what things are cannot simply be read off what they do. Some things can be worked out from their effects, as atoms were long before anyone could see one. Some questions may be answered one day. Some may always be partly beyond us, though we can still know something true about them.

So one rule runs through this book: **describing what something does very well does not, by itself, tell you what it is.**

## Everyone starts somewhere

If what we measure does not settle what things are, then every picture of the world, the atheist's, the Christian's and this book's, has starting assumptions. The honest thing is to show them.

There is one bad habit to watch for. Imagine someone who blames every strange noise in a house on an invisible ghost. A creaky stair? The ghost. A lost sock? The ghost. Whatever happens, the ghost gets a new power to explain it. Nothing could ever prove the ghost wrong, and that is exactly why it explains nothing.

The author calls this the *Harry Potter problem*: if you can add a spell whenever you need one, you can explain anything, and so you really explain nothing. This book has to obey that rule too. Its own big idea must not get new powers every time a new problem comes along.

## Hanging together

How, then, do we decide between different pictures of the world, if we cannot open the machine and look?

Think about how a doctor works. A cough, a temperature and a rash prove nothing alone. The doctor looks for the explanation that fits all of them, and changes her mind if a new result does not fit. Detectives and historians work the same way, and so do you, most of the time.

This book calls that **coherence**: everything fitting together. A good picture of the world has to make sense of falling stones, living things, people, promises, right and wrong, and what the Bible teaches, all at once. It must not need one set of beliefs for the science lesson and another for church.

And this test works both ways. The book must agree with what has been carefully observed and measured. If it does not, it is wrong. But the usual picture of the world that people pick up alongside science, a world of separate little objects floating in empty space, is also just a picture. It does not get a free pass because it is familiar. It has to pass the same test.

## Not the same as being true

One warning before we go on. A story can hang together perfectly and still be false. A detailed, consistent story about a flat Earth is still wrong. So coherence is not the same as truth. It is a good guide, and a way of comparing pictures honestly, but it is not proof that a picture is right.

That is why this book tries to say plainly what it thinks, why it thinks it, what supports it, what would count against it, and what is still unknown.

## What this chapter has shown

- Knowing how something behaves is not knowing what it is.
- Every picture has starting points, and no explanation may grow new powers to escape a problem.
- The best picture makes everything fit together, but fitting together is not the same as being true.

# Chapter 3: Made of Relationships

Look at the stone again and list what you know about it. It is grey. It is hard. It is heavy. It is cold this morning. It has a shape.

Now look at each item on the list. *Grey* is how the stone answers daylight: which colours it sends back to your eye. In the dark it has no visible colour at all. *Hard* is how it resists being scratched by other things. *Heavy* is the pull between it and the Earth; on the Moon it would weigh far less. *Cold* is which way heat flows between it and your hand.

Every item on the list is about how the stone stands towards something else: the light, other things, the Earth, your hand. Take all those relationships away and ask what is left. It is surprisingly hard to say.

## Two pictures of the world

There are two ways of picturing the world.

In the first picture, the world is a collection of **separate objects**, like snooker balls on a table. Each ball is complete in itself. When balls collide, things happen *to* them, but each ball would be exactly the same ball if it had never touched another.

In the second picture, the world is **relational**. Things are real, and they really are different from one another, but what each thing is cannot be separated from how it stands towards everything else. Relationships are not an extra added on afterwards. They are part of what things are.

The first picture works well for building bridges. This book suggests that, at the deepest level, the second is closer to the truth.

## One language everywhere

Take a stone from your garden and a stone from the other side of the world. Nobody arranged for them to fit together. Yet they scratch, warm and press on each other by exactly the same rules, and a meteorite that fell from space joins in straight away.

Everything in the physical world, everywhere, "speaks the same language": anything can affect anything else, by one shared set of rules.

If everything were a completely separate object, made entirely on its own terms, this would be a huge coincidence. If everything is made *within* one shared order of relationships, it is exactly what you would expect. Things fit together because they belong to the same order.

## The book's big idea

So the book's central idea is this: **at the deepest level, the physical world God made is made of relationships.** Atoms, stones and stars are real, but each is a steady pattern *within* relationship, not a separate lump that exists first and then gets connected.

A knot in a rope helps. The knot is real: you can point to it, count it and untie it. But it is not a separate object added to the rope. It is a shape the rope itself is in. (The picture has a limit: a rope is made of fibres that existed before the knot, and the book's idea has nothing like those fibres underneath.)

The book gives the deep order a name: the **Relational Substrate**. *Substrate* just means "what lies underneath". Three things must be said about it at once:

- It is something **God made**. It is not God.
- It is **not a mind**. It does not think, want or plan.
- It is **not a soul**, and it is not anything spiritual.

## What we believe, and what we are testing

One more distinction matters here, and it is easy to miss.

The author believes, from the Bible, that the material world rests on something that is not itself material. That is how the author reads verses like Hebrews 11:3: "things which are seen were not made of things which do appear". That belief is a **starting point** taken from Scripture.

*How* that works, how a steady, solid stone could come from an order that is not itself solid stuff, is the book's **idea being tested**. Nobody has shown how it happens yet. The book says that plainly. It is a debt the idea still owes.

## Held by God

The Bible never mentions a relational substrate. But it does tell us what any deep order must be like:

> `Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power...`
> *Hebrews 1:3*

Whatever is deepest in the physical world is upheld by Christ. A world in which nothing stands completely on its own fits well with a world in which everything depends on God.

## What this chapter has shown

- Everything we know about the stone is about how it relates to other things.
- The book's big idea is that the physical world is made of relationships, within an order God made: not God, not a mind, not a soul.
- That matter rests on something non-material is a belief from Scripture; *how* is still being tested.

# Chapter 4: What Can Happen, and What Does

Water, cooled enough, becomes ice. It does not become glass, or iron. Every snowflake is different, and yet every snowflake has six sides. Something leaves room for endless variety and still draws firm limits around it.

This chapter is about those limits, and about how one thing, out of everything that *could* happen, actually *does*.

## Could and does

A coin could land heads or tails. It lands tails. An acorn could grow into an oak. This one gets eaten by a squirrel. What *could* happen is always wider than what *does*.

But what could happen is not "anything at all". A coin cannot land on a colour. An acorn cannot grow into a sunflower. The range of possible things is already shaped before anything happens. Scientists describe these shapes in many ways, for example with rules that say energy is never created or destroyed.

## Locked doors and worn paths

There are two quite different ways a rule can shape what happens.

A rule can **forbid**. It makes something impossible. Think of a locked door: you simply cannot go that way.

A rule can **favour**. It makes something more likely, but leaves other options open. Think of a well-worn path across a field: most people take it, but nobody has to.

Scientists working on this project tested the difference using very small computer models. They found something clean and simple. Only "locked door" rules ever changed what was possible at all. "Worn path" rules changed how often things happened, but never closed anything off. These are facts about the models, not discoveries about nature. But they show that the difference between forbidding and favouring is real and sharp, and the book suggests that God's world has both.

## Limits that make things possible

We often think of limits as bad. But think of a river. A river is a river because of its banks. Take the banks away and the water does not become freer. It becomes a marsh.

The Bible describes creation in just this way, as God setting bounds:

> `Thou hast set a bound that they may not pass over; that they turn not again to cover the earth.`
> *Psalm 104:9*

Light is divided from darkness, sea from land, kind from kind. Each limit makes room for a richer world.

## What happens carries on

When something actually happens, it leaves a mark that changes what can happen next. A stone that cracks now has a weak line in it. That does not decide whether the stone will break or exactly how, but it changes how future knocks will affect it. A seed that has sprouted can no longer stay a seed. What has already happened shapes what can happen next.

## A game with no players

Chess is a helpful picture. The rules say which moves are allowed, some moves are played more often than others, and then there is the game actually played tonight. But the picture breaks at one important point. In chess, *players* choose the moves. In the deep order of the world there are no players. Nothing inside it chooses, and nothing inside it wants anything. If we imagine a little player inside the world making the choices, we have turned the world into a mind, and the Bible forbids that.

## So where is the purpose?

If nothing inside the world is choosing, does that mean the world has no purpose? No. The Bible is clear that the world is made with purpose:

> `While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.`
> *Genesis 8:22*

The book's answer is that **the purpose belongs to God, not to the world.** God's purpose is built into the shape of what is possible. The world then runs within that shape, really and reliably, without itself intending anything. A wooden spoon is made for stirring, but the spoon does not want to stir.

That also means God is not trapped inside the world's rules. He made them. The book describes how the world ordinarily works. It does not claim to describe how God acts in miracles.

## What this chapter has shown

- What could happen is shaped before anything happens, by rules that forbid and rules that favour.
- What has happened shapes what can happen next.
- Nothing inside the world chooses; the purpose belongs to God, who is not trapped by his own rules.

# Chapter 5: Chance With Rules

Somewhere in an old campfire there is a tiny atom of a kind of carbon that slowly breaks down. When will this particular atom break down? On the usual understanding of science, nobody can say. It might be next second. It might be in fifty thousand years. Yet in a large pile of these atoms, half will have broken down in about 5,730 years, so exactly that scientists use it to date the campfire.

Each single atom is open. The whole pile is as reliable as clockwork. This chapter is about how both can be true.

## Three meanings of "random"

People say "random" and mean different things:

- **Lawless**: anything at all could happen, for no reason.
- **Unknown to us**: there was a cause, but we could not see it.
- **Chance with rules**: each result is open, but the odds are fixed.

Think of a fair die. Any face can come up on a throw. But it can never come up seven, and over thousands of throws each face comes up about one time in six. The die is not lawless. It follows exact odds.

The book means the third kind. To keep it clear, it calls this **constrained chance**: chance working inside firm limits.

## Is the world really chancy?

Scientists' best theory of tiny things, quantum theory, gives its answers as odds. They disagree about *why*. Some think the world really is open at the smallest scale; others think everything is fixed underneath and the odds only show what we do not know. Every measurement so far fits both.

So the book is careful. It suggests that the world really is open, within its limits: that nothing in the world fixes in advance which of the allowed things will happen. That is one of the book's ideas, not a proven fact, and the book says so.

## Chance and God

Does that mean the world slips out of God's hands? The Bible is surprisingly calm about chance:

> `The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.`
> *Proverbs 16:33*

In Bible times, casting lots was the classic chance event. Proverbs does not deny that the lot falls by chance. It says the whole outcome belongs to the Lord.

So in this book, chance is part of how God's world works. It is not a rival power, and it does not mean God is unsure what will happen. When the book says an event is "open", it means nothing *in the world* fixes it in advance. It does not mean God does not know. How God's complete knowledge fits with this openness is not something the book claims to explain.

## Steady from shaky

How can a world full of chance be so reliable? Part of the answer is very old and well understood.

Toss a coin ten times and you might get seven heads. Toss it ten million times and you will get very close to half heads, every time. Many small chance events add up to something steady. That is why a bicycle tyre feels firm, even though the air inside is made of countless molecules rushing about in every direction, each one impossible to follow.

But how events are linked matters too. Imagine two rooms of a hundred people tossing coins. In the first room, everyone tosses on their own. In the second, people work in pairs, and each pair agrees that one will always call the opposite of the other. Every single person still has an even chance of heads. Yet in the first room the number of heads jumps about, and in the second it is exactly fifty, every time. The same odds can give very different wholes, depending on how the events are joined.

Mathematics describes all this beautifully. But remember Chapter 2: describing the world well with numbers is not the same as the numbers *being* the world. A map can be perfect without being the land. The Bible says the world can be understood:

> `For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made.`
> *Romans 1:20*

It is no surprise that a world made by a God of order can be described so well.

## What this chapter has shown

- The book's chance is chance with rules, and whether the world is chancy at bottom is still debated.
- Open does not mean God does not know.
- Many chance events can add up to something steady, and mathematics describes this without being it.

# Chapter 6: Staying the Same

An old saying goes: you cannot step into the same river twice. The water you stepped into has flowed away, and new water has taken its place. Yet everyone calls it the same river. A candle flame is the same: the burning gas is replaced every moment, but the flame stays steady. And you are like this too. Much of the material in your body has been replaced over the years, but you are still the same person as the child in your old photographs.

So what makes something the same thing through change? It cannot simply be that its stuff stays the same, because often it does not.

## What every change keeps

One answer is that every change a thing goes through keeps something. No physical process ever changes the total amount of energy, for example. Tie a knot in a loop of string and then twist and squash the loop without cutting it: the knot can look very different, but it is still the same knot. Something can be carried by a pattern rather than by stuff.

That is a good start. But it is not the whole story.

## The same for what?

Imagine two coins in your hand. Same size, same weight, same design. For buying sweets they are the same. Then a bank tests one and finds it is a fake. The difference was there all along, in its metal. Nothing you did with it could find it. The bank's test could.

Or think of two keys cut to look alike. Side by side you cannot tell them apart. Try them in the lock, and only one turns.

So whether two things are "the same" depends on what could tell them apart. Two things count as the same, for some purpose, if nothing that could happen to them would ever show a difference. If something could, they were never fully the same.

## Same kind, or the same one?

There is a second question, and it is easy to mix up with the first.

Two coins from the same machine can be identical in every test. They are still two coins. If you lose a coin and get a new one exactly like it, you have not found your lost coin. You have a replacement.

So there are two questions:

- Is it the **same kind** of thing? (Could anything tell them apart?)
- Is it the **same one**? (Is it the very thing that was there before, with an unbroken history?)

A copy can be the same kind without being the same one. Keep this in mind. It will matter a great deal when the book talks about people, and about the resurrection.

## Carrying its history

Bend a paper clip and straighten it again. It looks almost new, but it is weaker, because the bending is still there in its metal. A healed bone looks whole but still carries the mark of its break.

Things carry their history in their present shape. The past is not written down in some separate record somewhere. It is kept in the way things are now. And that history can change what happens next: the bent paper clip will snap sooner.

The book suggests that this is what memory is at the most basic level: past events kept in present shape, so that they change what can happen next. That is one of the book's ideas. It will help later when the book talks about habits and character.

## Picking up, and passing through

You can **pick up** the stone and carry it, and it arrives as the same stone. But clap your hands and a sound crosses the room while each bit of air only wobbles a little. What crosses is a *pattern*: real, but not one more thing you could carry. The book uses the word **material** for things like the stone, which stay themselves and can be picked up and moved.

## So what is the stone?

The book's idea is that **matter is a steady pattern**. An atom is a very stable pattern within the deep order of relationships. A stone is a huge number of those patterns holding together, steady enough to lie on a path for a thousand years.

That does not make the stone any less real. Its reality is a pattern that holds, rather than a lump of stuff that never changes.

The Bible says that created things last because God holds them:

> `Thou hast established the earth, and it abideth. They continue this day according to thine ordinances: for all are thy servants.`
> *Psalm 119:90-91*

And it reminds us that God is the same in a quite different way from us. Created things last like a garment and can wear out. God does not wear out at all: "thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end" (Psalm 102:27).

## What this chapter has shown

- Whether two things are the same depends on what could tell them apart.
- Being the same *kind* is different from being the same *one*.
- Things carry their history, and on the book's idea matter is a steady pattern held by God.

# Chapter 7: How the Stone Reaches You

Sunlight falls on the stone. Some of it bounces off and reaches your eye. Tiny parts inside your eye change. Signals pass along your nerves. And you see a grey stone, there on the path.

The stone did not move. Nothing flew off it into your head. Yet a difference in the stone, its grey body and its pale stripe, became a difference in you. How?

## Three steps

The book describes every case like this in three steps. It calls them **Transmit, Carry, Receive**.

- **Transmit.** Something has a difference that can affect something else. The stone's grey and its pale stripe change the light in different ways.
- **Carry.** The difference is kept on the way. The light still depends on the stone when it reaches you.
- **Receive.** Something else is changed *because of* that difference. Your eye changes in a way that depends on the stone.

## Carried without travelling

Here is the surprising part. Nothing has to travel the whole way.

Long ago, people sent news across a country with signal fires. A fire was lit on one hill. On the next hill, a watcher saw it and lit another. And so on, for a hundred miles. No fire travelled. Each was a different fire. Yet the news arrived, because each fire was lit *because of* the one before.

Or think of a phone call. Your voice becomes electricity, then radio waves, then light in a cable, then sound again at your friend's ear. Everything carrying it changed several times. Your friend still hears what you said.

What has to be kept is not a thing. It is the **difference** that matters, passed along a chain.

## Arriving is not receiving

A letter that lands on the doormat and is never opened has *arrived*. A letter that is opened, read and acted on has been *received*. The words are the same. What is different is whether they made a difference to someone.

So receiving means being changed. And not everything can receive everything. Your eye can see light, but not the radio waves passing through you right now. A radio has to be tuned to the right station, and set up the right way, to turn the signal back into music. What can be received depends on the receiver.

## A tune on any instrument

Think of a well-known tune. You can hear it played on a cello, whistled, picked out on a piano, or played through a tiny phone speaker. Everything about the sound is different. Yet you recognise the tune at once.

What stays the same is the *pattern*: how the notes step up and down, and their rhythm. The tune lives in the relationships between the notes, not in any one instrument. That is a good picture of how things can keep who they are even when what carries them changes.

History works like this too. The dodo died out more than three hundred years ago, yet sailors' accounts, paintings, bones and a head kept in Oxford each carry a little of what it was. None of them *is* a dodo. Knowing about something is not having it back.

## The pattern everywhere

The book suggests that these three steps are not just a handy description. Whenever one thing truly makes a difference to another, something like *transmit, carry, receive* has to be happening. That is the book's idea, and it explains why the same pattern turns up in light, in living cells, in speech and in history.

The Bible describes God's word in a way that has the same shape:

> `So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please.`
> *Isaiah 55:11*

God's word goes out and does what he sends it to do. Remember Chapter 1: this is a likeness of form. God's word is not a radio signal, and faith is not light hitting an eye.

## What this chapter has shown

- One thing makes a difference to another by transmit, carry, receive.
- Nothing has to travel the whole way, and arriving is not receiving.
- A tune keeps its identity on any instrument; God's word shares the shape, not the substance.

# Chapter 8: Life, and You

Turn the stone over. Underneath, in the damp, there is a woodlouse, a pale root and a thread of fungus. A moment ago this book was thinking about a lump of rock. Now it meets things that eat, grow, heal and make more of their own kind.

## Life is a pattern that keeps itself going

The stone stays a stone mostly by being left alone. A living thing is the opposite. It is always taking things in, changing them, and getting rid of what it does not need. A woodlouse is not a fixed heap of molecules. It is a pattern that keeps rebuilding itself, like a flame, but far more complicated. It repairs damage. It grows. It makes more woodlice.

So life is **a pattern that keeps itself going**. A living thing does not just last. It works to stay what it is.

Living things carry their own instructions for repair and for making new life, most famously in DNA. But DNA does nothing on its own in a test tube. It only works inside a living whole.

## Life is given

The Bible is clear that living things are real and active. "Let the earth bring forth the living creature," God says (Genesis 1:24). But life is never something living things own by their own right. It is given and kept by God:

> `Thou takest away their breath, they die, and return to their dust. Thou sendest forth thy spirit, they are created.`
> *Psalm 104:29-30*

So the book can describe *how* life works without pretending to explain where it comes from. Life is not a magic fluid added to matter, and it is not "nothing but chemistry". It is God's gift, working through the order he made.

## Dust and breath

Now we come to you. Genesis tells how God made the first man:

> `And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.`
> *Genesis 2:7*

First a body is formed from **dust**, the same stuff as the rest of the earth. Then God gives **breath**. And the result is a **living soul**: a living person.

Your body is fully part of the physical world, made of the same order as the stone and the woodlouse, and that is good. The Bible does not treat the body as a prison, which is why Christians hope for the resurrection of the body, not escape from it.

## A person is more than a body

But a person is more than a body. Here is one way to see it. Christians believe God the Father is a person, the Son is a person, and the Holy Spirit is a person. The Father and the Spirit have no bodies. So being a person cannot simply *be* having a body. Being a person is the deeper thing.

The Bible speaks of an inner person, the soul and spirit:

> `For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.`
> *2 Corinthians 4:16*

So the book must be very clear here. **Your soul is not a pattern in the physical world.** It is not one more thing that the Relational Substrate makes. The physical world is where you live and act, but your soul is not part of its furniture.

## Seeing is not just signals

Think back to Chapter 7. Light carried the stone's difference to your eye, and signals passed along your nerves. But the story did not end in a nerve. It ended in *you*: someone who saw the stone, liked it, and decided to pick it up.

How does a signal in a nerve become the experience of seeing grey? Nobody knows. **The book does not claim to explain it.** It only claims something smaller: a world made of relationships is a fitting home for persons who see, think and choose.

And when you do something, like telling a lie or keeping a promise, your body and your words take part in it. But the rightness or wrongness of what you did is not the same thing as the sound of your words. The two are connected, and they are not the same.

## Made in God's image

Above everything else, humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27). That is not a part of the body. It is a calling: to know God, to answer to him, and to care for his world. What makes you *you*, in the end, is not a pattern of dust, however steady. It is that God knows you and calls you by name:

> `Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine.`
> *Isaiah 43:1*

## What this chapter has shown

- Life is a pattern that keeps itself going, and it is God's gift.
- A person is more than a body, and the soul is not a pattern in the physical world.
- The book does not explain how seeing becomes experience; what makes you *you* is God's knowing and calling.

# Chapter 9: Right and Wrong in a Real World

On Monday you promise a friend you will help her move house on Saturday. On Saturday morning you would rather stay in bed. You get up and go.

That ordinary scene holds a lot. The person who gets up on Saturday is the same person who promised on Monday. Your words went out from you and became something in your friend: a plan she is counting on. And the promise *binds* you. If you stayed in bed, she would not just be surprised. She would be let down. You *owed* it.

## Why the kind of world matters

If the world were made of completely separate objects, like snooker balls, relationships would be something added on top of a world with no room for them. If the world is made of relationships from the very bottom, then a life full of promises, families, friendships and trust is not a stranger in it. It fits the grain of the world God made. That is one of the book's main reasons for its big idea: it helps everything we know, from stones to promises, hang together.

That does not mean promises are physics. A promise is not a force. Love is not a wave. The book is showing that the world has room for these things, not that they are made of the same stuff as stones.

## Three kinds of "allowed"

A surgeon cuts a patient's skin with a scalpel. An attacker cuts someone's skin with a knife. The two cuts might look almost the same. One is healing, and the other is a crime. Nothing in the cut itself tells you which is which.

So when we say something is "allowed", we can mean three different things:

- **Can it happen at all?** Is it possible in the world God made?
- **Can *I* do it?** Is it within my power?
- **Should it be done?** Is it right?

These are different questions. Lying is possible. Almost anyone can do it. And it is wrong. Holding the whole world in being is possible, since it happens, but no creature can do it. Only God can.

The Bible's first story of wrongdoing turns on this. The fruit was within reach, and eating it was within Adam and Eve's power. But it was not right.

The most important point is this: **knowing what is possible never tells you what is right.** The science of a lie is the same as the science of the truth. What is right is shown to us by God:

> `He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?`
> *Micah 6:8*

## Chance is not choosing

Chapter 5 said the world might be open, with chance working inside its rules. Does that make you free? No. A coin toss is not a choice. The book does not claim that your choices come from tiny chance events. Science cannot settle whether your will is free, and this book does not try. Choosing belongs to persons.

## Time as a gift

The Bible speaks of time in a way a clock does not:

> `So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.`
> *Psalm 90:12*

Our days are an **opportunity**, given and limited, to grow wise and do good. What you did yesterday shapes what you are able to do today. An athlete's two minutes in a race rest on months of training. That is why the Bible says that more is asked of those who have been given more (Luke 12:48).

## Rules that free

People often think every rule is a loss of freedom. But remember the river and its banks in Chapter 4. The Bible says God's commands work the same way:

> `And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts.`
> *Psalm 119:45*

A good boundary gives life its shape, so that you can grow into what you were made to be. (This is a likeness of form: a command is spoken by God to persons, and a riverbank is not.)

## What this chapter has shown

- A world of relationships is a natural home for promises and love, without turning them into physics.
- Possible, within my power and right are three different questions, and the first never settles the third.
- Chance is not choosing, and time is an opportunity.

# Chapter 10: What Went Wrong

The stone is beautiful in the morning light. But there is litter on the path. The river nearby may be polluted. And the person looking at the stone may be grieving, or angry, or lying to someone they love. The world is good, and it is also badly wrong.

## Wrong spoils what is good

The Bible takes this seriously:

> `Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned.`
> *Romans 5:12*

But it also shows what kind of thing evil is. Evil is not a second creator. It cannot make anything. It can only **spoil** what God made, as rust needs iron to exist and mould needs bread.

## What happened to creation

After Adam and Eve disobeyed, God said:

> `cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee.`
> *Genesis 3:17-18*

Paul adds that creation "was made subject to vanity... in hope" (Romans 8:20). *Vanity* here means frustration and emptiness, not pride. So the Bible says that God himself placed creation under this burden.

It helps to keep three things apart.

- **What the Bible says.** God cursed the ground because of human sin, and work became hard.
- **What the author thinks it means.** The author believes God changed the world's deepest settings, so that creation would keep telling the truth about what had gone wrong. A world where turning from God made no visible difference would teach a lie. This is the author's interpretation, not something the Bible spells out.
- **What nobody knows.** *How* God did it. Genesis does not say.

The author also believes that animal death began at that point, after the Fall, and reads the "coats of skins" God made for Adam and Eve (Genesis 3:21) as a sign of it. That too is the author's interpretation. And it faces a real difficulty: fossils show animal death in rocks that scientists usually date long before there were any people. The book does not hide that problem, and it does not claim to have solved it. It names it as an open question.

One thing is clear on every view. God's judgment is not itself evil. It was given "in hope". And not every suffering is a punishment for the sufferer's own sin. When people asked Jesus whose sin had made a man blind, he answered, "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3).

## How wrong grows

Chapter 6 showed that things carry their history: a bent paper clip stays weaker. People are a bit like that too, in their habits. One lie makes the next easier and honesty harder. A grudge kept for years changes what a person can see. The Bible uses a strong word for this: being "hardened" (Hebrews 3:13).

A wrong act is wrong in itself, even if it happens only once. But when it is not put right, it tends to grow: into habits, into relationships and into whole communities. That is why sin is a trap and not just a slip.

It grows in recognisable ways: wearing away (a friendship starved of attention), breaking apart (a family that stops speaking), turning upside down (a leader who uses people), faking (kindness put on for show) and feeding off others (a con that lives on someone's trust).

## Limits are not evil

One important point. Not every limit is a fault. You need sleep. You cannot be in two places at once. You do not know everything. None of that is evil. It is simply what it means to be a creature, and God called his creation "very good" (Genesis 1:31).

If every limit were evil, the only rescue would be to stop being a creature. The Bible's hope is quite different: creatures put right, as creatures.

## Remembering

How do God's people stay on track over many years? The Bible's answer is: by **remembering**. The Passover, the feasts and the Lord's Supper ("this do in remembrance of me", Luke 22:19) keep bringing people back to what God has done. Forgetting is one of the easiest roads into going wrong.

## What this chapter has shown

- Evil cannot create; it spoils what is good, and unhealed wrong hardens and spreads.
- God placed creation under a curse; how is unknown, and the author's view of animal death faces an open problem with the fossil record.
- Being limited is not evil.

# Chapter 11: Putting Things Right

Real hope needs something real underneath it: a reason to believe that what is broken can be mended. If the world were finally chaos, hope would be pretending. If the world belongs to God, hope may be the most sensible thing there is.

## Only God can mend it

Chapter 6 showed something important. When something is truly destroyed, nothing in the world can bring it back by itself. Living things can repair a lot, but in the end every living thing dies.

So the healing of creation cannot come from creation itself. The Bible says it comes from God:

> `And, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself; by him, I say, whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven.`
> *Colossians 1:20*

The One who made all things is the One who puts all things right. That is not something the world does to itself. It is God's act. When Jesus rose from the dead, that was not a very unusual pattern in the physical world. It was an act of the Creator. The book does not try to explain miracles as hidden science.

## Hope for the whole world

The Bible's hope is not that souls escape from a ruined world. It is that God will make the world new:

> `And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new.`
> *Revelation 21:4-5*

Creation itself "shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Romans 8:21). If people are made from the dust of a world built from relationships, then their healing and the world's healing belong together. It is one rescue, by one God, of one world.

## The same person, raised

The centre of Christian hope is the resurrection, and it is a hope about being the *same person*. After he rose, Jesus said:

> `Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see.`
> *Luke 24:39*

Remember the difference between the same kind and the same one (Chapter 6). A perfect copy of you would be the same kind, but it would not be *you*. So resurrection is not God making a copy. It is God raising the same person. How? The book does not know, and the Bible does not tell us. But it is clear why it must be God's work: nothing in the physical world could do it. God, who calls each of his people by name, does not lose track of who they are.

## Mended, and more

A restored life is not always just put back where it was. A friendship mended after a serious quarrel, or a faith that has come through hard doubts, can become deeper and more honest than before. The wound and the healing both stay part of the story.

That does **not** mean evil was needed, or that suffering is good in itself. God made a "very good" world with no evil in it, where Adam and Eve could truly choose (Genesis 2:16-17). Evil was never part of the plan. What God does is answer the evil that happened, and the answer cost him dearly:

> `For when we were yet without strength, in due time Christ died for the ungodly.`
> *Romans 5:6*

## A taste now

Between Jesus' resurrection and the renewal of everything, God gathers his people together. In local churches, people pray, worship, forgive, remember and serve. They do not bring in the new creation; only God does that. But they can give a real, if imperfect, taste of it now.

## What this chapter has shown

- Restoration is God's act, for the whole world.
- Resurrection is God raising the same person, not making a copy.
- A mended life can be deeper than before, but evil was never needed.

# Chapter 12: Putting It Together

The stone is still on the path. Nothing about it has changed since the start of the book. What has changed, hopefully, is how much you can see in it.

## The stone, again

The stone is something God made, and he holds it in being. It is not God, and it does not hold itself together.

On the book's big idea, it is a steady pattern within a deep order of relationships. What it could be was shaped before it ever existed, and God's purpose is built into that shape, though the stone itself wants nothing. Its history unfolded by chance working within rules. It stays the same stone because the changes it goes through keep what it is, and it carries its history in its present shape. It reaches you because a difference in it changes the light, the light keeps that difference, and you are changed by it.

And you, who see it, are made from the same dust, alive by the same gift that keeps the woodlouse alive, and yet a person: known and called by God, able to keep or break a promise, hurt by what has gone wrong and offered healing. You belong to the same world as the stone. You are not the same kind of thing.

## How sure is the book?

Remember the four kinds of claim from the start. Here they are again, with what belongs to each.

- **The Bible says so**: God made the world; the world is not God; people are more than bodies; sin is real; God will raise the dead and make all things new.
- **We can see and measure it**: stones fall, light bends, and science describes the world with amazing accuracy. The book does not argue with any of that.
- **The book's best idea**: that the physical world is made of relationships; that chance works within rules; that *transmit, carry, receive* is how one thing makes a difference to another; and that matter is a steady pattern. These are ideas. The book's computer models show they are possible and workable. They do not show that the world really works this way.
- **Nobody knows yet**: how matter could arise from something that is not matter; what gravity really is; how seeing becomes experience; how the fossil record fits the author's view of animal death.

## What could prove it wrong?

A good idea should be able to fail. Here are places where this one could:

- If careful measurement ever contradicted it, it would be wrong, however neat it looked. One of the project's own models has already failed this way, and the failure was written down, not hidden.
- Some puzzles in physics, such as particles that stay strangely linked across great distances, and the way clocks run slower near heavy objects, are hard for this idea, and it has no full answer to them yet.
- If nobody can ever show how solid matter arises from the deeper order, the idea will stay unproven.

The book's case is not that it has proved anything. Its case is that this picture helps everything we know, from stones to promises to the Bible's story, fit together better than the alternatives.

## We see in part

The Bible gives a good reason to hold all this humbly:

> `For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known.`
> *1 Corinthians 13:12*

We know the world from the inside and cannot see its foundations as God does. But knowing in part is still knowing, and searching out God's world is one of the good things he gives us to do:

> `It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter.`
> *Proverbs 25:2*

The right response to a world like this is **wonder**: being amazed by something you can partly understand and that is still bigger than you.

## Living in a world like this

If the world is like this, a few things follow.

- **Honour science.** It studies a real, ordered world, though it describes how things behave, not everything about what they are or are for.
- **Take what you do seriously.** Words and actions go out, are carried and are received. Promises, kindness and neglect all travel.
- **Care for creation.** It groans and waits with us for God to make it new (Romans 8:22).

And above all, remember who made the stone, the light, your eyes and you:

> `Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man.`
> *Ecclesiastes 12:13*
